## 3. Independent seven-point obstruction and order-type mechanism

Here is a proof of the required special case independent of the imported citation. Let p_1,…,p_7 be in general position in R^4. Form the 5×7 matrix A with columns (1,p_i). Its kernel has dimension two. Choose a kernel basis and write g_i in R^2 for the i-th row of the resulting 7×2 matrix. Any two rows g_i,g_j are linearly independent: otherwise a nonzero kernel vector could vanish in coordinates i,j, contradicting independence of the other five columns of A.

As a vector u rotates through a half-turn in R^2, the signs of g_i·u reverse. Their zero crossings are distinct because the g_i are pairwise independent. Away from zeros, let k be the number of positive signs. Across each zero, k changes by one; the endpoints of the half-turn have counts k and 7−k. Consequently at some crossing the count changes between 3 and 4. At that crossing exactly one coefficient vanishes; of the remaining six, three are positive and three negative. The vector c=(g_i·u) lies in ker A, so sum c_i=0 and sum c_i p_i=0. Normalizing its positive and negative coefficients gives an intersection between the convex hulls of two disjoint triples. This proves the balanced obstruction needed in ambient four.

For six generic points, the unique affine dependence is given by the signed 5×5 cofactors of A. All coefficients are nonzero. Thus signs of five-point affine determinants determine whether this six-set has a 3–3 split, and determine the two triples. An intersection of disjoint triples cannot use a relation supported on five or fewer points, because those points are affinely independent. Hence the labeled simple order type determines the candidate's entire forbidden-pair family.

Goodman–Pollack count **realizable simple numbered configurations**, with n>d and no hyperplane containing more than d points. This is exactly the general-position class needed. Their bound is finite, not merely an asymptotic bound with an unspecified constant. To check it at the candidate's enormous n, reconstruct their product-polynomial argument: for d=4, the degree is D=4 binom(n,5) in 4n variables. Their component bound is (D+2)(D+1)^(4n−1). For n≥5, D+2≤n^5, so this is at most n^(20n). We do not count arbitrary abstract oriented matroids or assume a universal bound for nongeneric configurations.

The candidate's small-perturbation reduction is valid. For a linear embedding of a finite generated complex, images of each pair of disjoint nonempty faces are compact and disjoint. Their finitely many distances have a positive minimum, preserved by a sufficiently small vertex perturbation. Any failure of simplex injectivity or any excess intersection between overlapping faces would, by cancellation of common barycentric coefficients, imply an intersection between two disjoint faces. Therefore a generic perturbation remains an embedding. Unused labels in [n] can be added as generic dummy positions without changing the complex.

## 4. Janson denominator, hypotheses, and the candidate's sampling space

The Bernoulli coordinates here are the M possible **whole triangles**. For a fixed forbidden pair e={σ,τ}, the event set D_e is the two-element subset of these coordinates. Downward closure adds faces to the generated simplicial complex; it does not add new Bernoulli variables or complete missing triangles from their edges. Two indicators with no common triangle coordinate are independent even if their constituent triangles share geometric vertices or edges.

The required denominator is

B = sum over ordered pairs (e,f) with D_e∩D_f nonempty of E(I_e I_f)
  = μ + Δ_ordered_distinct.

The current book's brace notation could be misread as unordered. Its actual proof, equation (34.40), expands sum_i E(I_i sum_{j∼i} I_j), which fixes the convention as ordered, including i=j. The candidate explicitly adopts this convention and safely uses the weaker bound P(Z=0)≤exp(−μ²/(2B)). Newman's abbreviated statement is therefore not the sole convention evidence.

For an independent derivation, write ψ(λ)=E exp(−λZ). For each e, split Z into Y_e, the indicators overlapping D_e including itself, and U_e, the others. After conditioning on I_e=1 the remaining coordinates still form a product measure. Positive association for decreasing functions gives E(exp(−λY_e)exp(−λU_e)|I_e=1) ≥ E(exp(−λY_e)|I_e=1)E(exp(−λU_e)). Also I_e is independent of U_e and exp(−λU_e)≥exp(−λZ). Two applications of Jensen yield −(log ψ)'≥μ exp(−λB/μ). Integration and λ→∞ give P(Z=0)≤exp(−μ²/B), which is stronger than the candidate's displayed bound. The standard product-measure association step follows by induction on coordinates; no random-complex threshold assumption enters.

For distinct overlapping pairs, the joint expectation is p³: they have exactly one common triangle ID. Ordered overlapping pairs inject into choices of their common triangle and the two other triangle IDs, so their number is at most M³. Diagonals contribute μ, bounded by M²p²/2. These are the quantities used in the candidate. A deletion set is fixed before applying this probability estimate; the subsequent finite union over all deletion sets permits the actual cleaning set to depend on the sample.

## 5. PL inflation with original vertex labels retained

Lee–Nevo's definition in the abstract/introduction treats hyperedges as the maximal simplices of the generated complex. Section 3 defines PL embedding by a geometrically embedded simplicial subdivision. Lemma 3.1 therefore supplies the required filled simplices and all their faces, not merely an embedding of the incidence graph or the boundary spheres.

I verified the marked d=3 case directly as follows. This strengthens the initial placement in the source proof and makes its Q=P branch sufficient throughout.

1. Place all original hypergraph vertices generically in R^3. For every four-edge f, let P_f be the tetrahedron spanned by its four points. Choose the incidence-center c_f in int(P_f), successively avoiding the finitely many planes spanned by triples of previously placed points. Each choice is possible in this nonempty open set. All points of the incidence graph remain in general position. Its straight segments form an embedding, since two disjoint intersecting segments would have four coplanar endpoints.
2. Around each c_f choose a small regular tetrahedron σ_f entirely inside P_f and a facet τ_f. Form C_f = σ_f ∪ union_{v∈f} conv(v,τ_f). As the radii tend to zero these filled cells approach the embedded incidence stars. Away from shared vertices the finitely many unrelated graph segments have positive clearance. Near a shared vertex, each cone occupies an arbitrarily narrow angular sector around its distinct incidence ray; the finite positive angle separations prevent overlap. Thus sufficiently small radii give C_f∩C_g=f∩g. Hypergraph linearity ensures the intersection is empty or one prescribed point.
3. Every constituent tetrahedron shares τ_f. A point x in its relative interior sees all of C_f by segments. Because τ_f is inside P_f, original vertices occur on both sides of its plane; the corresponding tetrahedra show x is interior to C_f. The cell is a polyhedral ball: its radial exit distance about x is continuous. On each of the two hemispheres the exit distance is the maximum of finitely many constituent-tetrahedron exit distances, each continuous up to the equator; on the equator they all agree with the exit distance of τ_f. The exit distances are positive and finite.
4. Since C_f⊆P_f, each marked vertex v∈f is on both boundaries. Radial projection about x from ∂C_f to ∂P_f fixes v. Refine both boundaries by the projected boundary cells and the faces of P_f. Finite projective images on planar faces are polytopal; intersections give a common polytopal refinement. Compatible triangulations of corresponding cells give combinatorially identical subdivisions. Choosing paired vertices and extending affinely on corresponding simplices produces a PL boundary homeomorphism fixing all four marked vertices. Cone the two boundary triangulations from x to get a PL homeomorphism P_f→C_f with those same labels.
5. Different original tetrahedra share only marked vertices, so these maps agree on every overlap and assemble into a finite PL embedding of the generated complex. Finitely many subdivisions suffice.

This checks the source mechanism rather than declaring correctness from the theorem citation. The source's additional outside-P radial branch need not be interpreted as a literal fixed-label certificate for this application; choosing centers inside their simplices avoids it.

For the candidate, appending one private vertex to each cleaned triangle creates a linear four-uniform hypergraph. Restricting the resulting PL embedding to the original triangular subcomplex is legitimate. Subdivision respects that subcomplex, and the marked-label argument proves global compatibility.

## 6. Candidate chain and quantitative boundary checks

The seven-point obstruction gives at least binom(n,6)/7 forbidden pairs by double counting seven-sets and the n−6 seven-sets containing a fixed six-supported pair. Deleting m triangles removes at most mM such pairs. With the displayed c=1/645120, n≥12, and n^(7/4)≥107520, the residual family has at least cn^6 pairs. The exponent dominates both terms, so the uniform property survives an adaptive cleaning choice.

Each offending selected-triangle pair sharing an edge has unique common edge and two extra vertices; its expectation is p², giving E B≤n/4. Removing one chosen triangle per pair uses at most B deletions and meets every edge-sharing pair. The cleaned generated complex is linear. Independent triangle count concentration guarantees more than n retained triangles. Their edges are distinct, giving more than 3n graph edges on at most n vertices; planar graph bounds exclude a PL embedding in R². The moment curve in R⁵ gives the linear upper bound because every six placed vertices are affinely independent. The resulting exact minima are consequently 3 and 5.
