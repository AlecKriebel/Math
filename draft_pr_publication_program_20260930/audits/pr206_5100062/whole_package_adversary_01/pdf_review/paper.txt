                                                 Abstract
         For an odd periodic billiard in a proper confocal ellipse pair, invert its original vertices in
     unit circles centered at the original outer foci. The product of the two signed straight-edge areas
     is independent of phase. This is the historical invariant k903,a of Reznik–Garcia–Koiller and
     Conjecture 1 of Reznik–Garcia–Helman. Ferudun’s 2026 preprint proves the same theorem using
     substantially the same meromorphic divisor mechanism. This attributed veriﬁcation note gives
     a self-contained intrinsic ﬂag-curve presentation: four isotropic common tangents identify every
     possible pole, odd torsion separates their orbit grids, and reversal supplies the opposite-focus
     zeros needed for compactness. Primitive admitted star windings, signs, and indexed repetitions
     are speciﬁed. Four exact ﬁnite billiard controls accompany the proof, including two same-caustic
     even orbits with diﬀerent products. No novelty or absolute-priority claim is made.


1    Historical target and attribution
Reznik, Garcia, and Koiller deﬁne signed polygon area in [1, Eq. (1)], original-vertex inversion about
the original billiard foci in [1, §3.9], and the odd focal-inverse area product in [1, Table 10, p. 12,
k903,a]. The row is dated 10/20. The shorter journal edition does not contain this row. Reznik,
Garcia, and Helman give the triangle product as Proposition 7 and its odd-period extension as
Conjecture 1 in [2, p. 6].
    Ferudun’s Products of the Two Focal Inversion Areas in Elliptic Billiards, version 1.0 [3], states
and proves this same strict odd-period theorem, including admitted stars, reversal, and repetitions.
Its Jacobi-coordinate proof and the ﬂag-curve proof below share the essential architecture: bound
the area poles, use reversal to reduce their orders, obtain opposite-focus zeros, and apply compact-
ness. The intrinsic formulation here is an attributed veriﬁcation and exposition of that theorem and
mechanism.
    Complexiﬁcation, ﬂag involutions, and removal of poles on a compact curve are established tools
for Poncelet invariants; see Akopyan–Schwartz–Tabachnikov [4], Chávez-Caliz [5], and Schwartz [6].
Their cited invariants concern other observables. For the present inverse areas the focal divisor and
adjacent-pole calculation must still be supplied; these are made explicit below. Source and chronology
limitations are recorded in Section 7.

Theorem 1. Suppose the pair (1) admits a primitive real billiard of odd period N ≥ 3. For each
oriented family of such billiards, the product Ac A−c in (2) is independent of phase and is strictly
positive. The assertion includes each coprime star winding actually admitted by the pair. Reversal
negates both areas and preserves the product. Repeating a primitive cycle r ≥ 1 times multiplies each
area by r and the product by r2 .

does not permit arbitrary reordering of one vertex set. In Stachel’s convention [7, Theorem 4.3], the

       cn v = β/b,      a = α dn v/ cn v,       v = 2νK(k)/N,            1 ≤ ν < N/2,   gcd(ν, N ) = 1.

The software parameter for these functions is k 2 . These relations specify the period and winding
conventions; the proof uses no complex Jacobi period identity. Circular, coincident, degenerate, and
hyperbolic caustics are outside the strict statement.

    Let σ exchange the two outer endpoints on ℓ, and let τ exchange the two caustic tangents through
P . These extend to involutions of X, with

                           T = τ σ,     πτ = π,          πT = πσ,            σT σ = T −1 .          (5)

The map T advances along the tangent chord and then chooses the other outgoing caustic tangent: it
is the billiard successor. Each involution has ﬁxed points and is nonidentity. A ﬁxed-point involution
on a genus-one curve acts in an Abel coordinate as u 7→ d−u: its linear part is −1, since a nonidentity
translation cannot ﬁx a point. Consequently T is a translation. A primitive period-N real ﬂag makes
its translation element have exact order N on all of X.
    A ﬁxed ﬂag of σ corresponds to a common tangent of the two conics. For a line ux + vy + w0 = 0,
the two tangency conditions are

                               a2 u2 + b2 v 2 = w02 ,            α2 u2 + β 2 v 2 = w02 .

Their diﬀerence is λ(u2 + v 2 ) = 0. Thus the four common tangents, all distinct and transverse as
dual-conic intersections, are

                                z = x + iy = ±c,                 w = x − iy = ±c.                   (6)

Write their σ-ﬁxed ﬂags as sf,z , sf,w , f = ±c.

5    Reversal removes every product pole

                                         Af (σQ) = −Af (Q).                                      (10)

    At s = sf,z , only vertices 0 and 1 have poles, both of order two in the same coordinate R. Every
Sj is regular there by Lemma 1. In particular, the determinant for the edge joining the two singular
vertices contains no R0 R1 term. The area pole therefore has order at most two before any symmetry
is applied.

    At the same phase the opposite-focus area A−f is regular, since its pole grids are disjoint from

At sf,w interchange R, S; the same pole bound and oddness apply. Translation handles all points of
each grid. Elsewhere the inverse coordinates are regular. The product is therefore holomorphic on
compact connected X, and is constant. This proves phase constancy in Theorem 1.

    The accompanying standard-library exact veriﬁer checks the four polygons’ incidences, segment
tangencies, reﬂections, inversions, areas, reversal, and triple repetition. It also distinguishes inversion
radius scaling from physical similarity: radius 2 multiplies each area by 16 and the product by 256;
scaling the orbit and both foci by s = 3/2, with radius ﬁxed at 1, multiplies area by s−2 and product
by s−4 . Its 245 checks in 27 nonempty groups are ﬁnite controls, not a certiﬁcate of the all-period
proof. Explicit exceptions remain active under Python optimization; deliberate false and malformed
inputs are tested separately. All four ﬁnite controls are convex winding-one trajectories; higher stars
are covered by the analytic proof, not by this ﬁnite suite.
