# Checkable implication and boundary calculations

These calculations distinguish classical partial coverage from the target claim. They are mathematical deductions, not evidence of historical priority.

## 1. Classical polarity gives the outer conic

Put

`E: x²/a²+y²/b²=1`, `c: x²/alpha²+y²/beta²=1`.

Under polarity in `E`, a point `Q=(X,Y)` corresponds to the line

`(X/a²)x+(Y/b²)y=1`.

This line is tangent to `c` exactly when

`alpha² X²/a⁴ + beta² Y²/b⁴ = 1`.

The pole of the chord `P_i P_(i+1)` is the intersection of the tangents to `E` at its endpoints, namely `Q_i`. Since every original edge is tangent to `c`, the `Q_i` lie on the polar reciprocal conic

`C_o: X²/(a²/alpha)² + Y²/(b²/beta)²=1`.

Moreover the line joining consecutive outer vertices is the tangent to `E` at their shared original vertex. Thus `Q` is a Poncelet polygon inscribed in `C_o` and circumscribed about `E`. This is the direct specialization of the classical polar-reciprocal conic chain stated by Fouché 1915 §4 pp. 149–150; the elementary coordinates make the translation checkable.

The candidate's canonical relations are `a=alpha dn(v)/cn(v)` and `b=beta/cn(v)`, so its coefficients satisfy

`A_o = a dn(v)/cn(v) = a²/alpha`, `B_o = b/cn(v) = b²/beta`.

The outer locus is generally not confocal with `E`. Its own foci should not be substituted for `F±`, which are the foci of `E`.

## 2. An exact nonimplication control for Chasles / 1853

Let `E: x²/4+y²=1`, with focus `F=(sqrt(3),0)`.

The four lines `x=±2`, `y=±1` form a circumscribed rectangle with vertices `(±2,±1)`. Its full focal-distance product is `16`.

The four lines

`x=±2`, `x/4+sqrt(3)y/2=±1`

also circumscribe `E`: the support/tangency test is `4(1/4)²+(sqrt(3)/2)²=1`. Their vertices, in cyclic order, are

`(2,1/sqrt(3)), (-2,sqrt(3)), (-2,-1/sqrt(3)), (2,-sqrt(3))`.

For an antipodal pair `(x,y),(-x,-y)`, the squared product of distances to `F` is

`[(x-sqrt(3))²+y²][(x+sqrt(3))²+y²] = (x²+y²+3)²-12x²`.

For the rectangle both pair values are `16`, so its four-factor product is `sqrt(16·16)=16`. For the second parallelogram the pair values are `52/9` and `52`, so its product is `sqrt((52/9)·52)=52/3`.

Each polygon is centrally symmetric. For a quadrilateral the antipodal map preserves the two alternating vertex sets and interchanges the foci. Therefore the alternating focal-ratio identity holds in both cases, while the full products differ. This disproves the implication from the old circumscribed-polygon ratio theorem **alone** to an unrestricted full-product theorem. The second parallelogram is not asserted to be a k115 orbit for the same confocal pair.

## 3. Optional exact parity deduction from the candidate divisor

Use the candidate's parametrization for any primitive admissible period `N≥3`:

`delta=4K tau/N`, `gcd(tau,N)=1`, `0<tau<N/2`.

The algebraic identity and zero/pole computation in candidate §§4–5 use `v=delta/2` in `(0,K)` before any divisibility assumption. Except when `delta=K`, they give, on

`T = C/(2K Z + 2iK' Z)`, `p=iK'`,

`div H = 2[p+K]+[p+K+delta]+[p+K-delta]-4[p]`.

The exceptional equation `delta=K` forces `4 tau=N`; primitivity then forces `N=4`, already in the positive class, with divisor `2[p+K]-2[p]`.

For all other periods put `g=gcd(N,2)`, `m=N/g`, and let `G` be the cyclic subgroup generated by `delta` on `T`. Since `delta/(2K)=2 tau/N`, the order is exactly `m`. Every real element of order two on this real period circle is `K`; consequently

`K in G` iff `m` is even iff `4 | N`.

For

`F_m(u)=product_(j=0)^(m-1) H(u+j delta)`

the two simple-zero orbits and the double-zero orbit coincide with the coset `p+K+G`. Thus

`div F_m = 4 sum_(x in G)[p+K+x] - 4 sum_(x in G)[p+x]`.

If `4 | N`, the cosets agree and the divisor vanishes (the exceptional `N=4` has the analogous order-two cancellation). If `4` does not divide `N`, then `m` is odd and the two cosets are disjoint: equality of one point from each would give `K in G`. The displayed divisor is nonzero, so `F_m` is nonconstant.

For real phases define

`R±(u)=product_(j=0)^(N-1) |Q(u+j delta)-F±|`.

The full paired product always obeys

`(R+(u) R-(u))² = F_m(u)^g`.

When `N` is even, the orbit is antipodally paired and `R+=R-`, giving `R+²=F_m` on the real line. When `N` is odd, `m=N`, `g=1`, so instead

`F_m=(R+ R-)²`.

One must not use even-orbit pairing here. But `Q(u+2K)=-Q(u)` gives `R-(u)=R+(u+2K)` for all `N`. If either one-focus product were constant in the real phase, both would be constant, hence `F_m^g` would be constant on a real interval. The positive real factors have no real zeros or poles; the identity theorem would make the meromorphic norm constant, contradicting its nonzero divisor. Therefore neither focal product is phase-constant when `4` does not divide `N`.

This proves the converse as a consequence of the supplied divisor. It does not independently reprove all of the candidate's geometric algebra, and it supplies no priority evidence.

## 4. Reproducible finite checks

`verify_implications.py` uses exact rational arithmetic. It checks the cyclic subgroup order, half-period membership, and complete norm-divisor coefficients for all 2,192 primitive admissible `(N,tau)` with `3≤N≤120`, including the `N=4` exceptional degree. It also verifies the two exact full-product values in §2. All assertions passed; results are in `VALIDATION.json`.

These finite checks are supporting controls. The general arguments above do not depend on extrapolating from them.
