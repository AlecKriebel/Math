## Literal question reconstructed independently
The original dated September 2, 2019 question asks whether a smooth closed immersed curve in Euclidean three-space, with a continuous binormal vector field whose values are injective around the parameter circle, must have nonzero linking number with the curve obtained by adding a small scalar multiple of that field. The preceding definition admits a continuous choice of osculating planes containing the first and second derivative vectors, and calls any continuous field perpendicular to those planes a binormal field. The preceding surface discussion motivates the question with closed asymptotic curves and the Gauss map. The actual quantification in Problem 1.4 concerns curves and fields; it does not state a negative-curvature annulus assumption.

## Exact success and counterexample criteria
An affirmative theorem would have to cover the stated regularity, possible curvature zeros, and immersed curves whenever the linking expression is well-defined. To refute the universal implication, a single smooth embedded regular closed curve with an injective, continuous, everywhere nonzero binormal field and disjoint embedded sufficiently small push-offs of linking number zero suffices. An everywhere unit field would meet the natural Gauss-map normalization as well and remove magnitude-based ambiguity. A construction must establish actual parameter-circle closure and seam smoothness, regularity, binormal orthogonality, injectivity of field values including seam endpoints, and the linking value by a topological proof. Numerical plots or quadrature alone cannot establish these universal properties. The strongest counterexample would give a full open range of admissible small push-off sizes and parameter values with analytic inequalities rather than one sampled instance.

## Hidden assumptions and boundary cases to attack later
1. The printed definition does not explicitly normalize B or require it to be nonzero. Orthogonality by itself permits zero vectors, and variable magnitude can make B injective while its direction repeats. An interpretation using injectivity of normalized directions is stronger than literal value injectivity. A unit, nonzero example avoids that ambiguity; a proof cannot silently impose it on the positive statement.
2. Positive curvature is not a stated assumption. At curvature zeros, the first two derivatives need not determine a unique plane. A candidate must produce a continuous plane field containing both, not divide by a zero cross product or claim a nonexistent Frenet binormal. Such points may be the essential boundary between this question and a stricter Frenet-regular problem.
3. Smooth refers to the curve; B is only continuous. A proof must not infer differentiability of B, regularity of its spherical image, or a nonvanishing derivative from continuity/injectivity. A smooth example is admissible but stronger regularity must remain correctly scoped.
4. The curve is allowed to be immersed rather than embedded. Ordinary knot self-linking requires separated supports and choices that may be noncanonical at crossings. A robust negative example should be embedded so that the standard linking number is unambiguous. The push-off requires epsilon small enough for separation and should be checked, not presumed.
5. Injective means the map from S^1 to field values, not the graph of a field attached to distinct basepoints. Identical coordinates at the parameter seam are the same domain point; injectivity on a half-open interval plus seam compatibility is the correct test.
6. Reparametrization preserves the span of the first two derivatives when regular; reversing an oriented field or epsilon changes conventions and can affect signs, but zero linking is invariant under these choices. Scaling B affects the permissible epsilon and must be tracked.
7. The sentence connecting a positive answer to tight-surface rigidity is one-way. A negative answer alone does not settle Problem 1.2 or prove existence of a negatively curved annulus with the additional boundary conditions of Problem 1.3. A free-standing ribbon or local ruled strip is not that global surface.
