## Equation-level formulation comparison

Two primary fulltexts give checkable reproductions of the canonical formulation: Conforti–Cornuéjols–Zambelli, *Extended Formulations in Combinatorial Optimization*, February 2010 revision, §4.3, printed pp. 14–15, DOI [10.1007/s10288-010-0122-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10288-010-0122-z), [author-hosted text](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/gc0v/webpub/ExtFor-Feb2010.pdf); and Aprile–Fiorini, *Regular matroids have polynomial extension complexity*, arXiv [1909.08539v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08539), 20 December 2019, §5.1, printed pp. 8–9, later DOI [10.1287/moor.2021.1137](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.2021.1137).

Using this audit's notation, give each arc a common capacity variable `y_a`, and for every `v ≠ r` give it a unit-flow vector `f^v`. The essential constraints are

```text
f^v(δ⁺(r)) − f^v(δ⁻(r)) = 1,
f^v(δ⁺(u)) − f^v(δ⁻(u)) = 0       (u ≠ r,v),
0 ≤ f^v_a ≤ y_a                     (a ∈ A, v ≠ r),
y(A) = |V| − 1                     (spanning-arborescence face).
```

The sink balance follows by summing all balances. Incoming-root arcs can be removed, as in the 2010 presentation. The flow bound is **individual for each commodity**; it is not `Σ_v f^v_a ≤ y_a`. The cardinality condition is an equality selecting the face, not merely the valid lower bound `y(A) ≥ |V|−1`.

The cited results prove the capacity projection is the rooted-arborescence dominant, and its cardinality face is the arborescence polytope. On a bidirected undirected graph the further projection `x_{ij}=y_{ij}+y_{ji}` gives the spanning-tree polytope. They do not, by these projection theorems, prove the complete joint capacity/flow extension integral. Aprile–Fiorini's §5.2 capacity/circulation modifications serve matroid decomposition, rather than arbitrary joint-objective optimization.

Here is the relevant model correspondence, stated as an audit deduction. If `y` is integral on the cardinality face, its support contains a spanning rooted arborescence and has exactly `|V|−1` positive capacity units, so it is the incidence vector of that arborescence. A supported unit flow to `v` is then uniquely the incidence vector of its root-to-`v` path; continuous auxiliaries cannot add a circulation in this directed tree. Consequently `Σ_v Σ_a c^v_a f^v_a` equals Kaibel's literal path objective at integral capacities. Requiring every auxiliary flow integer gives the same trees and objective. Removing cardinality would allow larger supports and circulation freedom, invalidating the unique-path interpretation.

Wolsey's *Using Extended Formulations in Practice*, [Optima 85](https://mathopt.zib.de/Optima-Issues/optima85.pdf), April 2011, pp. 7–9, makes this correspondence explicit on p. 8: one root, a distinct commodity for each other node, binary edge and arc variables, the edge/arc projection, and the `|V|−1` cardinality constraint. Its flow variables record the arcs on the unique path to each destination. The printed balance display appears to repeat the same arc indices on both terms; the correct signed incidence equations above are independently available in the 2010 and 2019 sources. This typographical issue is not an alternative model.

## Integral projection versus nonintegral extension

Goemans–Myung, *A Catalog of Steiner Tree Formulations*, Networks 23 (January 1993), pp. 19–28, DOI [10.1002/net.3230230104](https://doi.org/10.1002/net.3230230104), [author-hosted fulltext](https://math.mit.edu/~goemans/PAPERS/GoemansMyung-1993-ACatalogOfSteinerTreeFormulations.pdf), supplies a precise historical distinction. Its p. 23 system `Q_xf` uses bidirected unit flows and couples opposite directions by `f^h_ij + f^k_ji ≤ x_{ij}` for every commodity pair `h,k`. Proposition 1 identifies it as the projection of the directed-capacity flow system. The projected `Q_x` is integral when all vertices are terminals. Footnote 9 nevertheless records nonintegrality of `Q_xf` itself. Reference [21] is Martin's **1986 Chicago working paper**, *A sharp polynomial size linear programming formulation of the minimum spanning tree problem*. It is not Martin's 1991 separation paper. The original 1986 working paper was not obtained.

The same catalog's uncoupled undirected cut relaxation `S_x` is already nonintegral on a cycle even with every vertex a terminal (p. 21). That is a different relaxation and cannot be used as a counterexample to the tight bidirected projection. Its root-independence theorem concerns the projected undirected Steiner polyhedron, not independent objectives on all the roots or on the auxiliary flows.

Thus the historical sources support an integral projection/nonintegral flow-extension distinction. They do not supply, in the material inspected here, an NP-hardness proof for the candidate's arbitrary joint flow objective, or a checked fractional witness for the exact cardinality face of every directed presentation. Such stronger statements are not certified from a footnote or generic extension title.

## Classical unrestricted-cost comparison

The literal source permits negative coefficients. This makes a weaker historical consequence available independently of nonnegative-cost network-design papers. Karp's 1972 [original chapter](https://www2.seas.gwu.edu/~simhaweb/champalg/tsp/papers/KarpOriginal.pdf), *Reducibility among Combinatorial Problems*, printed p. 94, lists directed Hamilton circuit in its completeness theorem. Its relevant page was rendered and visually checked here.

The following is explicitly an audit deduction from that published hardness, not a claim that Karp printed Kaibel's model. For a strongly connected digraph with `N ≥ 2` vertices, split a chosen vertex into a root `r` carrying its outgoing arcs and a sink `t` carrying its incoming arcs. Retain the other arcs. Every vertex is reachable from `r`: original simple paths from the chosen vertex give reachability of the unsplit vertices, and a directed cycle gives reachability of `t`. Put coefficient `−1` on every arc only in destination `t`'s vector; all other vectors are zero. The objective of a spanning arborescence is minus the length of its `r–t` path. A simple `r–t` path of length `N` exists exactly when the original graph has a Hamilton circuit. Such a path itself spans the split graph and forms an out-arborescence. Conversely an objective at most `−N` forces that Hamilton path, since the split graph has `N+1` vertices. Non-strongly-connected Hamilton-circuit instances can be recognized in polynomial time and mapped to a fixed no instance; the finite tiny cases can also be handled directly.

This certifies an elementary consequence for unrestricted negative costs. It says nothing about the candidate's nonnegative binary DAG restrictions. It is included to prevent treating arbitrary-real-cost hardness as a new theorem merely because the exact question is stated in 2018.
