# Primary scope sealed before candidate review

The exact literal definition is preserved in the independently fetched primary HTML, lines 396–397, and v4 PDF/text Definition 2.1. It requires a random real time S at which both centered forward and backward processes are independent standard Brownian motions. S is not required to be a stopping time. This is the paper’s 2BM, which differs from its 2BM(0).

The target permits independent stopped Brownian pairs with different laws and zero durations. A single deterministic finite c bounds every T_k strictly, and the two length sums diverge almost surely. Endpoint information belongs to the independent stopped pair even though the concatenation interval is half open. The pinned source statement omits the explicit random-origin definition; source priority repairs its interpretation, not its assumptions.

I read the entire operative Definitions 2.1–2.2, all of the proofs of Theorems 5.1–5.3, and the open-problem context ending in literal Problem 7.7. Theorem 5.1 assumes iid stopped pairs and finite mean; its proof chooses a renewal-stationarizing random Θ from the junction process, transfers total variation convergence of uniformly shifted renewal configurations through a common mark kernel, and uses exact Brownian finite windows far to the right. Identical laws supply the common mark kernel. A fixed deterministic junction is not its conclusion.

Theorem 5.2 uses iid heavy-tailed lengths: a square-root boundary gives moments below one but infinite mean. Its proof rotates each negative piece by 180 degrees while keeping endpoints, thereby producing a Brownian comparator, then shows a persistent macroscopic separation that rules out a backward Brownian random origin. Rotation does not itself construct the requisite random origin.

Theorem 5.3 uses nonidentical rare long pieces and permits zero lengths, calibrating probabilities to make a prescribed moment uniformly finite. Those lengths are not uniformly bounded deterministically. The accumulated rare pieces impose backward path events incompatible with Brownian law, even after a finite random shift. It therefore obstructs a moment-only extension but does not resolve deterministic bounded lengths.

Problem 7.7 literally asks for a decomposable FBM with an almost surely finite pathwise supremum of lengths that is not a BBM. A random finite supremum is weaker than one deterministic c, and absence of BBM is stronger than absence of 2BM. No equivalence of that question with the author’s Problem 8 is assumed.

The author-maintained live page still presents Problem 8 as open without a solution notice on this date. This is scoped source evidence, not an exhaustive novelty or resolution search. A valid approximation theorem may support an unsolved partial checkpoint; it cannot establish the target without an exact random S.
