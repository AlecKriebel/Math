# Restricted obstructions for the Macaulay quartic thickening question

**Target:** 30000224 / OWR-824-008
**Date:** 2026-09-30
**Status:** partial, original question unresolved; independent review pending

## 1. Exact question and the scope of this note

Let K be a field of characteristic zero, R=K[w,x,y,z], and

\[
A=K[s^4,s^3t,st^3,t^4]=R/\mathfrak a,
\qquad
\mathfrak a=\ker(w\mapsto s^4,\ x\mapsto s^3t,\ y\mapsto st^3,\ z\mapsto t^4).
\]

The question asks whether some ideal \(\mathfrak b\) has
\(\sqrt{\mathfrak b}=\mathfrak a\) and Cohen–Macaulay quotient \(R/\mathfrak b\).
There is **no homogeneity hypothesis on \(\mathfrak b\)**. This is the question
in Singh's report, [OWR 19/2005, p. 1116](https://ems.press/content/serial-article-files/45993?nt=1),
and [Singh–Walther, Question 3.6](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0701524).

This note does not answer that question. It establishes these restrictions:

1. A solution cannot be a binomial ideal
2. A solution cannot contain the reduced quadric equation \(q=wz-xy\)
3. If a solution is homogeneous, its generic multiplicity along \(\mathfrak a\)
   cannot be one or two

The first two statements allow nonhomogeneous ideals. The third does not.
A concrete Koszul obstruction also explains why a natural application of a
2025 residual-intersection theorem fails. These deductions use standard
algebra and geometry; no priority or novelty claim is made.

For the geometric arguments one may extend K to its algebraic closure.
Cohen–Macaulayness is preserved under field extension here, and the toric prime
\(\mathfrak a\) remains prime. Since some power of \(\mathfrak a\) is contained
in \(\mathfrak b\), radical equality also survives. A contradiction over the
algebraic closure therefore excludes a proposed ideal over K.

## 2. The one-dimensional deficiency

Let \(u=s^2t^2\) and \(B=K[s^4,s^3t,s^2t^2,st^3,t^4]\), with each degree-four
monomial assigned graded degree one. Then

\[
u=x^2/w\in\operatorname{Frac}(A),\quad
u^2=wz,\quad u^3=x^2z,\quad
wu=x^2,\ xu=wy,\ yu=xz,\ zu=y^2.\tag{2.1}
\]

The degree-one component of A omits exactly u. Every monomial of degree eight
is a product of two members of \(\{s^4,s^3t,st^3,t^4\}\): its t-exponent is a
sum of two members of \(\{0,1,3,4\}\), giving every integer from zero to eight.
Induction then gives every exponent from zero to \(4d\) in degree \(d\ge2\).
Consequently

\[
B/A=K\cdot\bar u\simeq K(-1).\tag{2.2}
\]

Over \(T=K[w,z]\), B is free with basis \(1,x,u,y\), by reducing the two
exponents modulo four. In particular B is Cohen–Macaulay of dimension two.
The extension of the homogeneous maximal ideal of A has radical the
homogeneous maximal ideal of B. Local cohomology of
\(0\to A\to B\to K(-1)\to0\) therefore yields

\[
H^1_{\mathfrak m}(A)\simeq K(-1)\ne0.\tag{2.3}
\]

Thus A itself is not Cohen–Macaulay. Also u is absent from A although its square
and cube belong to A. This violates seminormality. Over the complex numbers
the cone is therefore not Du Bois. The Du Bois obstruction of
[Ma–Schwede–Shimomoto, Proposition 4.9](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.02755)
cannot be applied. Their graded Corollary 4.10 detects nonpositive-degree
local cohomology, whereas the defect (2.3) lies in positive degree one.
These are failures of those particular tests, not evidence of existence.

## 3. No binomial Cohen–Macaulay thickening

**Proposition 3.1.** There is no binomial ideal \(\mathfrak b\subset R\) with
\(\sqrt{\mathfrak b}=\mathfrak a\) and Cohen–Macaulay quotient in characteristic
zero. Homogeneity is not assumed.

**Proof.** A Cohen–Macaulay ring has no embedded associated primes. Since
\(\mathfrak a\) is the unique minimal prime, \(\mathfrak b\) would be
\(\mathfrak a\)-primary. None of w,x,y,z belongs to \(\mathfrak a\), so
localization at their product contracts \(\mathfrak b\) back to itself.

Every binomial in \(\mathfrak a\) has equal coefficients on its two monomials,
up to a common nonzero factor: evaluate it at (1,1,1,1). There are no nonzero
monomials in \(\mathfrak a\). In the Laurent polynomial ring
\(L=K[w^{\pm1},x^{\pm1},y^{\pm1},z^{\pm1}]\), the ideal
\(\mathfrak b L\) is thus generated by \(X^v-1\) for a subgroup
\(\Lambda\subset\mathbb Z^4\). Its quotient is

\[
L/\mathfrak bL\simeq K[\mathbb Z^4/\Lambda].\tag{3.1}
\]

A finitely generated abelian group is a product of a free abelian group and a
finite abelian group. In characteristic zero the group algebra of the finite
factor is a finite product of separable field extensions, and adjoining
Laurent variables preserves reducedness. Therefore (3.1) is reduced. This is
also a special case of the Laurent-binomial theory of
[Eisenbud–Sturmfels, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/alg-geom/9401001).

It follows that \(\mathfrak bL=\sqrt{\mathfrak bL}=\mathfrak aL\).
Contracting gives \(\mathfrak b=\mathfrak a\), contradicting (2.3). ∎

**Boundary.** Characteristic zero is essential in the group-algebra step.
Taking powers of binomial generators in characteristic zero usually produces
polynomials with more than two terms. The proposition does not exclude such
ideals, determinantal ideals with non-binomial minors, or other polynomial
thickenings.

## 4. No solution containing the quadric equation

The smooth projective curve
\(C=\operatorname{Proj}(A)\subset\mathbb P^3\) lies on
\(Q=V(wz-xy)\simeq\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1\). Under the Segre coordinates

\[
(w,x,y,z)=(ac,ad,bc,bd),
\]

its equation on Q is \(bc^3-ad^3=0\), so C is the divisor of type (1,3).
Equivalently its parametrization in the two factors is
\(([s^3:t^3],[s:t])\); the second factor shows C is isomorphic to \(\mathbb P^1\).

**Proposition 4.1.** No ideal \(\mathfrak b\) satisfying the original question
can contain \(q=wz-xy\). This assertion permits nonhomogeneous \(\mathfrak b\).

**Proof.** Suppose otherwise. Set \(S=R/(q)\), \(P=\mathfrak a/(q)\), and
\(J=\mathfrak b/(q)\). The ring S is a normal Cohen–Macaulay domain of
dimension three: it is a hypersurface, and its only singular point is the
vertex, so it is regular in codimension one and satisfies Serre's S2 condition.
The prime P has height one.

Since \(S/J\) is Cohen–Macaulay, the depth lemma applied locally to
\(0\to J\to S\to S/J\to0\) shows that J is maximal Cohen–Macaulay at every
prime containing P. At all other primes J equals S. Thus J is a torsion-free
rank-one S2 module, and hence is reflexive over the normal domain S.

A rank-one reflexive ideal is the intersection of its height-one
localizations inside the fraction field. The localization at P is
\(P^rS_P\) for some integer \(r\ge1\), because \(S_P\) is a discrete valuation
ring. At every other height-one prime J localizes to S. Hence

\[
J=P^{(r)}.\tag{4.1}
\]

Symbolic powers of a homogeneous prime are homogeneous. This can also be seen
here because scaling the grading preserves P and hence preserves the
intersection defining \(P^{(r)}\); invariance under all nonzero scalars over an
infinite field makes the ideal graded. Therefore the allegedly arbitrary ideal
\(\mathfrak b\) in this case is forced to be homogeneous.

On the smooth surface Q, the sheaf of \(P^{(r)}\) is the ideal of the Cartier
divisor \(D=rC\), of type (r,3r). On \(\mathbb P^3\) there is an exact sequence

\[
0\to\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^3}(k-2)
\to\mathcal I_D(k)
\to\mathcal O_Q(k-r,k-3r)\to0.
\]

The intermediate cohomology of line bundles on \(\mathbb P^3\) vanishes.
At \(k=r\), the Künneth formula gives

\[
h^1(\mathbb P^3,\mathcal I_D(r))
=h^1(Q,\mathcal O_Q(0,-2r))=2r-1>0.\tag{4.2}
\]

A homogeneous Cohen–Macaulay quotient of dimension two has a saturated ideal
and zero deficiency \(H^1(\mathcal I_D(k))\) in every degree. Equation (4.2)
is a contradiction. ∎

**Boundary.** Radical equality only says that some power \(q^N\) lies in
\(\mathfrak b\). Proposition 4.1 says that \(N=1\) is impossible. It does not
exclude the necessary transverse nilpotent structure with \(q\ne0\) in
\(R/\mathfrak b\).

## 5. No homogeneous double structure

Here generic multiplicity means
\(\ell_{R_{\mathfrak a}}((R/\mathfrak b)_{\mathfrak a})\).

**Proposition 5.1.** If \(\mathfrak b\) is homogeneous, has radical
\(\mathfrak a\), and \(R/\mathfrak b\) is Cohen–Macaulay, its generic
multiplicity is at least three.

**Proof.** Multiplicity one forces \(\mathfrak b=\mathfrak a\): primaryness
and localization at the only associated prime recover the ideal. This
contradicts (2.3).

Suppose the multiplicity is two, and write \(Y=\operatorname{Proj}(R/\mathfrak b)\).
In a local Artinian ring of length two, the square of the maximal ideal is
zero. Thus \(\mathfrak a^2\subseteq\mathfrak b\) after localization at
\(\mathfrak a\); primaryness gives the inclusion globally. The sheaf
\(\mathcal L=\mathcal I_C/\mathcal I_Y\) is consequently an
\(\mathcal O_C\)-module, generically of rank one. It is a subsheaf of
\(\mathcal O_Y\), which has no zero-dimensional submodules since Y is locally
Cohen–Macaulay. On the smooth curve C this makes \(\mathcal L\) a line bundle.
We have

\[
0\to\mathcal L\to\mathcal O_Y\to\mathcal O_C\to0,\qquad
\mathcal I_C/\mathcal I_C^2\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal L.\tag{5.1}
\]

For completeness, the conormal splitting is checked explicitly. Pull back
along \(f(s,t)=(s^4,s^3t,st^3,t^4)\). The conormal bundle is the kernel of

\[
J_f:\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-4)^4\to\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-1)^2,
\qquad
J_f=\begin{pmatrix}
4s^3&3s^2t&t^3&0\\
0&s^3&3st^2&4t^3
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

This identification follows from the Euler sequences and the differential of
f; the degree-four Euler identity commutes with them up to the invertible
scalar 4. The minors in columns (1,2) and (3,4) are \(4s^6\) and \(4t^6\),
so the displayed map is surjective on every fiber.
The two columns

\[
U=\begin{pmatrix}
2t^3&0\\
-3st^2&t^3\\
s^3&-3s^2t\\
0&2s^3
\end{pmatrix}
\]

satisfy \(J_fU=0\). Their endpoint minors are \(2t^6\) and \(2s^6\), so they
are independent at every point. They give the bundle isomorphism

\[
f^*(\mathcal I_C/\mathcal I_C^2)
\simeq\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-7)^{\oplus2}.\tag{5.2}
\]

This agrees with the classical normal-bundle computation of
[Eisenbud–Van de Ven](https://eisenbud.github.io/papers/pdfs/1981-001.pdf).
Write \(f^*\mathcal L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(e)\). The surjection in (5.1)
and (5.2) force \(e\ge-7\). Since \(\mathcal O_C(1)\) pulls back to
\(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(4)\), twisting (5.1) by two gives

\[
h^0(Y,\mathcal O_Y(2))
=h^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(e+8))+h^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(8))
=e+18\ge11.\tag{5.3}
\]

Here \(H^1(\mathcal O(e+8))=0\), since \(e+8\ge1\), so the equality is
justified. But for an arithmetically Cohen–Macaulay Y the restriction map
\(R_2\to H^0(Y,\mathcal O_Y(2))\) is surjective, while \(\dim_K R_2=10\).
This contradicts (5.3). ∎

**Boundary.** No reduction from a nonhomogeneous ideal to a homogeneous ideal
is proved here. A degeneration may lose Cohen–Macaulayness. Thus Proposition
5.1 cannot be promoted to a multiplicity bound for the original unrestricted
question.

## 6. An exact obstruction to a recent linkage shortcut

Let \(I=(w,x)\cap(y,z)=(wy,wz,xy,xz)\). The quartic is linked to these two
skew lines by the complete intersection \((q,wy^2-x^2z)\). On Q, the second
equation factors as

\[
wy^2-x^2z=ab(bc^3-ad^3),
\]

identifying the two ruling lines and C. This is also the example in
[Boix–Eghbali, Remark 5.7](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.04405).

Theorem 1.2 of [Hassanzadeh (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05705)
does not automatically produce a Cohen–Macaulay thickening of the residual
quartic. Its first alternative requires the residual codimension s to be at
least the number r of generators of I, whereas here s=2 and r=4. The second
alternative and the sliding-depth corollary also fail in this example.

Here is a checkable certificate. Put \(f=(wy,wz,xy,xz)\) and use the Koszul
convention \(d_{ij}=f_i e_j-f_j e_i\). The degree-four cycle

\[
c=-xz\,e_1+xy\,e_2
\]

is not a boundary. Indeed, degree-four boundaries are constant combinations
of the six \(d_{ij}\). The coefficient of \(xz e_1\) forces the coefficient of
\(d_{14}\) to be one; that introduces \(wy e_4\), which no other constant
boundary can cancel. Nevertheless

\[
\begin{aligned}
wc&=x d_{12},\\
xc&=x d_{14}-w d_{34}-x d_{23},\\
yc&=z d_{13}-y d_{23},\\
zc&=z d_{14}-y d_{24}.
\end{aligned}\tag{6.1}
\]

Thus \(H_1(f;R)\) has a nonzero element killed by the homogeneous maximal
ideal, and its depth there is zero. Sliding depth would require depth at least
\(4-4+1=1\), so that hypothesis fails.

More explicitly, \(\operatorname{depth}(R/I)=1\), from the exact sequence
\(0\to R/I\to R/(w,x)\oplus R/(y,z)\to K\to0\). Hence the first Koszul
cycle module \(Z_1\) has depth three. If \(B_1\) denotes the first boundary
module, (6.1) and \(0\to B_1\to Z_1\to H_1\to0\) imply depth \(B_1=1\).
Then \(0\to Z_2\to K_2\to B_1\to0\) gives depth \(Z_2=2\).
Auslander–Buchsbaum over \(R_{\mathfrak m}\) gives
\(\operatorname{pdim}Z_2=2\), exceeding the theorem's required bound
\(r-2-1=1\). This rules out that application without assuming the theorem's
conclusion or confusing linkage with preservation of set-theoretic CMness.

## 7. Verification, remaining gap, and stopping point

`python3 verify.py` uses only exact rational/integer arithmetic. Its 135
assertions check the monomial identities, degree slices through 20, the
conormal matrices and covering minors, all four Koszul identities, an exact
rank-6/rank-7 nonboundary certificate, the Segre factorization, and sample
cohomology formulas. The proofs above supply the universal statements; the
finite checks do not replace them.

The unexcluded problem is a non-binomial \(\mathfrak a\)-primary ideal with
nonzero nilpotent q in its quotient. A homogeneous example would need generic
multiplicity at least three; nonhomogeneous ideals remain unrestricted by
that last bound. No construction or exclusion of this remaining class was
obtained. Normalization changes the reduced scheme, while the known vanishing
and linkage tests do not force a contradiction. The attempt is therefore
**partial/stalled**, not a candidate resolution of OWR-824-008.
