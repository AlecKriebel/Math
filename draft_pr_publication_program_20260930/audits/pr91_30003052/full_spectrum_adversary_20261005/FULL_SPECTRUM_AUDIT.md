## Self-contained proof

### Peripheral geometry in the actual norm

The norm of K is one because composition by A is contractive in the supremum norm and fixes the constant function 1. Every eigenvalue of A has modulus at most one. A unit-modulus Jordan chain of length at least two would give vectors v,w with Av=lambda v and Aw=lambda w+v, so A^n w=lambda^n w+n lambda^(n-1)v. Its norm grows at least n||v||-||w||, contradicting ||A^n w||<=||w||. Thus all peripheral eigenvalues are semisimple.

Let E be the sum of those eigenspaces and F the sum of the remaining generalized eigenspaces. Let P be the commuting projection onto E. Write A=B direct-sum N in this decomposition. The finite-dimensional Jordan formula gives N^n->0 in norm; polynomial factors in n are dominated by exponential decay when the eigenvalue is nonzero and stable, and zero-eigenvalue blocks are nilpotent.

Simultaneous recurrence of the finitely many peripheral phases gives n_j->infinity with B^(n_j)->I. For completeness, partition the phase torus into small boxes and apply pigeonhole to enough consecutive powers. Differences of two powers produce positive q with every lambda^q arbitrarily close to 1. If such q have an unbounded subsequence, take it. If they remain bounded while errors tend to zero, some positive q is an exact common period, and its growing multiples give the required sequence. Hence A^(n_j)->P and ||P||<=1. This proves P(U)=V:=U intersection E, including nonsmooth or non-Euclidean norm balls.

Also B^(n_j-1)->B^(-1), so ||B^(-1)||<=1. Together with ||B||<=1, this proves B is a surjective isometry of V. When E=0, V={0} and its function space still contains constants.

### Boundary eigenvalues

For Kf=mu f and |mu|=1,

    |f(x)-f(Px)| = |f(A^n x)-f(A^n Px)|.

The arguments on the right differ by N^n(I-P)x, tending uniformly to zero on U. Both remain in U. Uniform continuity of f on this compact set therefore forces f=f composed with P. Conversely every eigenfunction of composition by B on V pulls back to one on U, with the same eigenvalue and nonzero norm.

Choose eigenvector coordinates z_i on E, with z_i(Bx)=lambda_i z_i(x). Every monomial z^p conjugate(z)^q is an eigenfunction with multiplier product(lambda_i^(p_i-q_i)). Every integer exponent vector is obtained by differences of nonnegative vectors, and a monomial is nonzero on the relative ball because a point with all its required coordinates nonzero can be scaled into that ball. Thus every element of Gamma is an eigenvalue.

These monomials span a uniformly dense subalgebra of C(V): it contains constants, separates points, and is closed under conjugation, so complex Stone-Weierstrass applies. For mu on the circle outside Gamma, the operators M_m=(1/m) sum_(n=0)^(m-1) mu^(-n) K_B^n have norm at most one and annihilate each monomial in the limit by the geometric Cesaro formula. By density, M_m g->0 for every g in C(V). A mu-eigenfunction is fixed by every M_m, so must be zero. Since K_B is an invertible isometry, any of its eigenvalues lies on the circle. Its point spectrum is exactly Gamma.

Its full spectrum is on the circle, using the outer Neumann series for K_B and the inner Neumann series for its inverse. The spectrum is closed and contains Gamma. If Gamma is infinite, its closure is the entire circle: a proper closed subgroup of the circle is finite (equivalently, its preimage under the exponential map is a closed additive subgroup of the real line). If Gamma is finite of order m, B^m=I and K_B^m=I. The explicit resolvent below excludes every z with z^m!=1, while all m-th roots already occur as eigenvalues because the finite subgroup is exactly those roots. Therefore sigma(K_B)=closure(Gamma), also when V is the one-point space.

### Strictly stable nonzero matrix eigenvalues

Choose a nonzero complex-linear eigenfunctional ell with ell(Ax)=a ell(x), where 0<r:=|a|<1. This is an eigenvector of the transpose action on the complex-linear dual and exists even for defective A. Put R=max_U |ell|>0. For any nonzero mu with |mu|<1 choose c=(log|mu|+i theta)/log r where exp(i theta)=mu/|mu|. Then Re c>0 and r^c=mu. The function

    f_mu(x)=exp(c log(|ell(x)|/R)) when ell(x)!=0,
    f_mu(x)=0 when ell(x)=0

is continuous, since its absolute value tends to zero at the entire hyperplane ell=0. It has norm one and Kf_mu=mu f_mu. For mu=0, f_0(x)=max(0,|ell(x)|/R-r) is continuous, nonzero at a maximizer of |ell|, and Kf_0=0. Thus the entire open disk is point spectrum, while the boundary calculation gives precisely Gamma on the circle. No point lies outside the closed disk because ||K||=1.

The full spectrum contains the closure of the open disk and is inside the closed disk, so is exactly that disk. There is also an explicit boundary check: for any |mu|=1 and 0<t<1, normalize the eigenfunction f_(t mu) above. Then ||(K-mu I) f_(t mu)||=1-t. Every boundary point is therefore in approximate point spectrum, even when it is not a true eigenvalue.

### Nilpotent remainder when J is empty

If J is empty, the restriction N has only zero matrix eigenvalues. For F nonzero choose d with N^d=0. Define Qf=f composed with P. It is a bounded projection, commutes with K, and its range is isometrically C(V), via restriction and pullback. Its kernel consists of the functions vanishing on V. For such f,

    K^d f(x)=f(A^d x)=f(B^d Px)=0,

because B^d Px belongs to V. Thus the restricted operator H=K|ker Q is nilpotent. If F is nonzero, a nonzero linear functional vanishing on E provides a nonzero member of ker Q. A nilpotent operator on a nonzero vector space has a nonzero kernel (take the final nonzero member of any iterated orbit), hence zero in point spectrum; its finite resolvent excludes all nonzero complex numbers. If F=0, this function-space summand is absent and contributes no spectrum at zero.

Both summands are closed, and their projections are bounded. K is therefore a Banach direct sum of K_B and H. An inverse on both summands yields a bounded inverse on their sum; failure on either summand prevents invertibility of the full block operator. Point spectra also take the union. Here F is nonzero exactly when A has eigenvalue zero. This proves both formulas for J empty, without silently adding zero when the complementary space is absent.

## Alternative full-spectrum controls and resolvents

These formulas supply a direct exclusion proof in addition to the spectral closure argument. Write R for restriction to V and E_0 for pullback by P, so Q=E_0 R. Where both restricted resolvents exist,

    (zI-K)^(-1) f = E_0 (zI-K_B)^(-1) Rf
                    + (zI-H)^(-1) (I-Q)f.

For H^d=0 and z!=0,

    (zI-H)^(-1) = sum_(j=0)^(d-1) z^(-j-1) H^j.

For a finite peripheral group of order m and z^m!=1,

    (zI-K_B)^(-1) = [sum_(j=0)^(m-1) z^(m-1-j) K_B^j] / (z^m-1).

For any peripheral group and |z|>1 the resolvent is sum_(n=0)^infinity z^(-n-1) K_B^n. For |z|<1 it is -sum_(n=0)^infinity z^n K_B^(-n-1). Both converge in operator norm. When F=0, zero is allowed in the latter formula and gives -K_B^(-1). When F is nonzero, the nilpotent summand excludes zero. `boundary_checks.py` verifies the finite resolvent polynomial identities with exact fractions; the identities hold for arbitrary complex z algebraically, rather than just the sampled fractions.

## Why finite matrix numerics cannot establish this spectrum

C(U) is infinite-dimensional for k>=1, even though A is a finite matrix. The induced operator on degree-bounded coordinate polynomials is an invariant finite-dimensional restriction, not the operator on all continuous functions. For A=(1/2)I on a complex disk its polynomial monomial multipliers are 2^(-p-q), all positive real. Even taking every degree gives only a real dyadic sequence with accumulation at zero. But the genuine continuous radial function exp(c log|z|), extended by zero at z=0 with Re c>0, gives any chosen nonzero complex mu in the open unit disk. Polynomial density does not imply convergence of finite restriction spectra, particularly for nonnormal operators. Finite matrix eigenvalues, grid discretizations, sampled residuals, and numerical irrationality tests cannot replace uniform continuity, exact eigenfunction constructions, bounded resolvents, or spectral closure.
