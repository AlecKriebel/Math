## Exact conclusion being tested

Let S consist of the modulus-one eigenvalues of A, J the nonzero eigenvalues of modulus less than one, Gamma the multiplicative group generated by S (Gamma={1} when S is empty), and Z={0} exactly when A has eigenvalue zero. The target claims are:

* If J is nonempty: point spectrum = {mu: |mu|<1} union Gamma; full Banach spectrum = {mu: |mu|<=1}.
* If J is empty: point spectrum = Gamma union Z; full Banach spectrum = closure(Gamma) union Z.

## Derivation

1. K has norm one (constants attain its norm), so its full spectrum is inside the closed unit disk. Power boundedness of A rules out all Jordan blocks of size greater than one at a modulus-one eigenvalue. Decompose V=E direct-sum F into the peripheral eigenspaces and the remaining generalized eigenspaces. Write A=B direct-sum N. All eigenvalues of N have modulus less than one; hence N^n tends to zero, including every strictly stable Jordan block.

2. The peripheral spectral projection P is a contraction for the given, possibly non-Euclidean norm. Indeed, simultaneous recurrence of the finitely many unit phases gives integers n_j tending to infinity with B^(n_j) tending to the identity of E. Thus A^(n_j) tends to P, and each power has norm at most one. Consequently P(U)=U intersection E. The same recurrence gives B^(n_j-1) tending to B^(-1), so B and its inverse both have norm at most one on E. B is a surjective isometry of U intersection E. If E={0}, the restriction is the one-point space and its composition operator is identity.

3. For Kf=mu f with |mu|=1, uniform continuity and N^n tending to zero imply

   f(A^n x)-f(A^n Px) tends to zero uniformly on U.

   Both terms equal mu^n times their corresponding original values. Therefore f(x)=f(Px). Every unit-modulus eigenfunction comes from C(U intersection E).

4. In eigenvector coordinates z_1,...,z_d on E, polynomials in z_i and conjugate z_i are dense in C(U intersection E) by complex Stone-Weierstrass. Every monomial is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue product(lambda_i^p_i conjugate(lambda_i)^q_i), in Gamma. Every element of Gamma occurs because the exponent differences p_i-q_i may be arbitrary integers, and a nonzero monomial does not vanish identically on the relative unit ball. Conversely, for any mu on the unit circle outside Gamma, the averages

   (1/M) sum_(n=0)^(M-1) mu^(-n) K_B^n

   have norm at most one and converge to zero on each polynomial. Density makes them converge to zero on each continuous function. A mu-eigenfunction is fixed by every average, hence is zero. Thus the peripheral point spectrum is exactly Gamma.

5. The peripheral full spectrum is closure(Gamma). Since K_B is an invertible isometry, its spectrum is on the unit circle. An infinite subgroup of the circle is dense, so the inclusion Gamma in its spectrum suffices when Gamma is infinite. If Gamma is finite of order m, B^m=I and K_B^m=I; polynomial spectral mapping confines the spectrum to the m-th roots of unity, exactly Gamma. This also covers Gamma={1}.

6. If J is nonempty, choose a nonzero eigenfunctional ell for an eigenvalue a with 0<|a|<1, so ell(Ax)=a ell(x). For every nonzero mu with |mu|<1, select a logarithm L of mu and set c=L/log|a|. Its real part is positive. Define f(x)=exp(c log|ell(x)|) away from ell(x)=0, and f(x)=0 on that hyperplane. This is continuous, nonzero, and Kf=mu f. Rotation of a does not affect the modulus. Also 0 is an eigenvalue of K: A(U) is a proper closed subset of U, since the absolute real determinant is |det_C A|^2<1 (or A is singular); a nonzero continuous function vanishing on A(U) lies in ker K. Hence the entire open disk is point spectrum. The full spectrum is closed, contains this disk, and lies in the closed disk; it is exactly the closed disk. Step 3 excludes any additional boundary eigenvalues.

7. If J is empty, N is nilpotent. Define Qf=f composed with P. Q is a bounded projection commuting with K; its range is an isometric copy of C(U intersection E), and its kernel consists of functions vanishing on U intersection E. For some m, A^m(U) is contained in E, so K^m vanishes on ker Q. Thus K is the bounded direct sum of K_B and a nilpotent operator. If F is zero, the second summand is zero-dimensional and contributes no spectrum. If F is nonzero, its function space is nonzero and a nilpotent operator on it has zero as its only spectral and point-spectral value. F is nonzero in this branch precisely when A has eigenvalue zero. This proves the second target formula.

## Counterexample attempts already resolved

* A is invertible with a nonzero strictly stable eigenvalue: this does not make K injective; A(U) is proper and provides a nonzero kernel function.
* A is zero in positive dimension: K is the projection onto constants, with both spectra {0,1}; not just {0}.
* A has only nilpotent stable directions plus peripheral directions: the function-space complement is actually nilpotent, rather than carrying an open disk.
* Stable nonnormal Jordan blocks decay; peripheral Jordan blocks violate the contraction hypothesis.
* E={0} gives a one-point peripheral image and Gamma={1}; it does not remove the constant eigenfunction.
* Finite and irrational unit phases give respectively a finite spectral set and the entire unit circle as full spectrum. Boundary point spectrum remains the actual generated group, even if it is dense and countable.
* The argument uses the contraction norm as given, never an unproved orthogonal decomposition. P(U)=U intersection E is supplied by recurrence.
