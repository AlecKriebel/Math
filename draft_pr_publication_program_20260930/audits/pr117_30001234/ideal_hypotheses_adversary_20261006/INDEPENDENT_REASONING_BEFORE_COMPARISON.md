# Independent ideal and hypothesis reasoning

This reasoning was recorded before inspecting the original verification or review and before reading any other new family's reports.

Let S=k[x1,x2,x3,y1,y2,y3], m=(x1,x2,x3,y1,y2,y3), and I=(x1*y2-x2*y1, x2*y3-x3*y2, x3*y1-x1*y3). For the intended application k has characteristic zero. All arguments in this note about the ideal are valid over any field; that auxiliary boundary observation does not expand the source question.

Every exponent vector is a nonzero vector in N^6, the displayed monomials are squarefree of degree two, and the coefficients gamma_i=1 are nonzero. The generator pairs have disjoint supports within each binomial, so they have no common monomial factor. The three polynomials are exactly the 12, 23, and negative 13 minors of the matrix with rows (x1,x2,x3) and (y1,y2,y3). Changing the last minor's sign multiplies a generator by the unit -1; in characteristic two the same relation has the plus spelling. Its ideal and coefficient nonvanishing do not change.

The k-algebra evaluation e:S->k at the all-one point kills I. For every nonzero c in k and every exponent u in N^6, e(c*x^u)=c, so no nonzero scalar monomial, including a nonzero constant, belongs to I. This proof controls polynomial combinations of arbitrary degree, not merely membership checks up to a degree bound.

One must not apply that torus evaluation directly to S_m: for example, x1-1 is invertible in S_m but vanishes at the all-one point. Nevertheless no monomial enters I_m. If a monomial M were in I_m, there would be h outside m with h*M in I. The homogeneous component of h*M in degree deg(M) is h(0)*M, with h(0) nonzero. Homogeneity of I would force M into I, a contradiction. This direct local proof does not need the optional prime claim.

Because I is homogeneous and generated in degree two, I_2 is the span of the three displayed binomials. Their six monomials are pairwise distinct, so the span has dimension three over any field. Every term of mI has degree at least three. Conversely, every homogeneous element of I of degree d>2 is a sum of generators multiplied by homogeneous polynomials of degree d-2>0; it therefore belongs to mI. Hence I/mI is exactly the degree-two space and has dimension three over k. Localization outside m leaves this quotient unchanged: an element outside m acts on I/mI by its nonzero constant coefficient, and is already invertible there. Thus I_m/(mS_m)I_m has dimension three. Any generating system of I_m must span this vector space and has at least three members. The displayed system has three members, so is minimal in S_m and S, and indeed has the minimum possible cardinality among polynomial generators, not just among binomial generators. This also rules out the weaker objection that one displayed minor might be redundant.

The usual alternate interpretation of "minimal" as an irredundant binomial system does not change the example. A degree-two binomial in I_2 must be a scalar multiple of one displayed f_i: a nonzero coefficient of one pair supplies both of its distinct monomials, while adding another pair would supply at least four terms. More generally every binomial in I is homogeneous. Indeed, if its two terms had different total degrees, homogeneity of I would put each of the two nonzero monomials separately in I, already ruled out. Any binomial generating system must span I_2 with its degree-two elements, since I has no nonzero terms of lower degree. Therefore it must include a scalar multiple of each f_i. Those three elements already generate I, so any extra member is redundant. Consequently every irredundant binomial generating system consists of these three minors up to nonzero scalar factors, order, and term orientation. This strengthening is a check of the candidate's hypotheses, not a changed target or a required extra assumption.

For an additional direct irredundancy check, set all variables in the third column to zero and take x1=y2=1, x2=y1=0. Only f1 is nonzero. Analogous choices using only columns 2,3 or 3,1 separate f2 and f3. The more general local quotient proof above is the controlling argument.

The optional prime claim admits an all-degree proof independent of determinantal theorems. Map x_i to s*z_i and y_i to t*z_i in k[s,t,z1,z2,z3]. A monomial x^alpha*y^beta maps to s^|alpha|*t^|beta|*z^(alpha+beta). Two monomials have the same image if and only if their three column counts c=alpha+beta and their x-degree |alpha| agree. In that case |beta| agrees automatically. If alpha differs from alpha', equality of total x-degrees supplies j with alpha_j>alpha'_j and i with alpha_i<alpha'_i; i and j differ. Since alpha_j>=1 and beta_i=c_i-alpha_i>=1, x_j*y_i divides the first monomial. Replacing it by x_i*y_j uses one of the three minors, up to a unit sign. The nonnegative integer sum of absolute coordinate differences of alpha from alpha' decreases by two, so this process terminates and proves equality modulo I. All intermediate exponents remain nonnegative. For a polynomial in the kernel, partition its finitely many terms by their image monomial. Each image monomial's coefficient sum is zero. Replacing every monomial of one group by a chosen representative modulo I therefore makes that group's sum zero. This proves kernel=I over any field with arbitrary coefficients and at all degrees, including finite characteristic. The quotient is a subring of a polynomial domain, so I is prime. No saturation or unproved cancellation argument is used.

Localization caveat: x3*f1+x1*f2+x2*f3=0. If x2 is inverted, f3 belongs to the ideal generated by f1,f2. Likewise the three binomials need not form a minimal generating system in a Laurent ring or at a point where x2 is a unit. The source asks about the polynomial ideal and (if the threshold context is read locally) its homogeneous maximal ideal; all six variables lie in m and no such inversion is permitted there. This distinction is a guard against applying a toric/Laurent rank argument to polynomial generator count.

The displayed sequence is not a regular sequence: in S/(f1,f2), the nonzero degree-one class of x2 annihilates the nonzero degree-two class of f3, by the cyclic syzygy. Nonzeroness of the two classes follows from degree constraints and the quadratic linear independence, respectively. A space-curve or complete-intersection restriction would exclude this example, but no such hypothesis appears in the supplied original statement.
