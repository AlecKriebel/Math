## Exact claim and source conclusion

For every integer n >= 3 and every finite, real-valued, nonconstant harmonic u on all of R^n, seek a continuous path gamma:[1,infinity) -> R^n with |gamma(t)| -> infinity and u(gamma(t)) -> +infinity as t -> infinity. Equivalently, for each real L and compact K, an entire sufficiently late parameter tail must satisfy u > L and avoid K. Merely having high vertices, an unbounded image, or a sequence escaping to infinity does not suffice. No fixed ray, rate, monotonicity, simplicity, finite total length, or bound on segment count is requested.

The original 2018 Update 3.2 credits Fuglede with asymptotic-path existence for subharmonic functions unbounded above; it expressly includes the harmonic case. It then discusses the earlier regularity question but expressly states that continuous subharmonic and harmonic functions admit the regular paths, and cites Carleson's general polygonal result. Therefore treating the harmonic problem as currently unresolved would conflict with the supplied original update. The word 'largely' in its opening does not override these later explicit affirmative sentences.

Carleson's printed theorem on p.35 assumes subharmonicity on all of R^n and sup u = +infinity and concludes a polygonal path to infinity with u tending to infinity. Section 2 assumes continuity; Sections 3–5 address discontinuity using potential approximation, dyadic cubes, Egorov and Fubini. The displayed potential representation in Section 3 explicitly says n >= 3. I read this whole argument but do not independently recertify its 'well-known' endpoint estimate or all approximation assertions. The harmonic problem requires only the continuous case.

One source caution: the printed Section 2 Phragmen–Lindelof criterion says 'thin' at the inversion origin. Under its stated definition the sign appears reversed. For A={|x|>1} in n>=3, h=1-|x|^(2-n) is bounded harmonic, boundary-zero, and positive, so A fails that property; the complement of its inversion is locally just {0}, thin at 0. This is an apparent source typo, not a refutation of the theorem. The independent elementary proof below does not use this criterion.

## Independently worked mechanism

1. A nonconstant entire harmonic function is unbounded above. If u <= M then h=M-u is nonnegative harmonic. Differentiating the ball Poisson formula at a center a gives |partial_i h(a)| <= (n/R) average_{S(a,R)} h = n h(a)/R. Send R -> infinity: every first derivative vanishes, so u is constant. This works for n>=2; the one-dimensional affine case is immediate. Harmonic nonconstancy, not mere subharmonic nonconstancy, provides the needed hypothesis.

2. Bounded continuous subharmonic f on R^n with 0<=f<=M=sup f has spherical averages tending to M. On B(0,R), comparison with the harmonic Poisson extension of its continuous boundary values gives f(a) <= integral K_R(a,theta) f(R theta) d sigma(theta). For each fixed a, K_R=(1-|a|^2/R^2)/|theta-a/R|^n tends uniformly to 1. Thus liminf_R average f(R theta) >= f(a); take the supremum over a, while each average <= M. This establishes the full R -> infinity limit without any unproved probabilistic assertion.

3. At most one of two disjoint positivity-supported bounded nonnegative continuous subharmonic functions can be nonzero. If f,g are both nonzero and bounded, normalize by their respective positive suprema. Disjoint supports imply f/sup f+g/sup g <= 1 pointwise, but Step 2 makes the spherical averages of the two summands tend to 1 each, a contradiction. The assumption of subharmonicity matters; arbitrary continuous disjoint bumps do not satisfy this conclusion.

4. For any component A of {u>c}, the function v_A=u-c on A and 0 elsewhere is continuous nonnegative subharmonic on R^n. Its boundary values are 0 by continuity and the definition of a component. At each boundary point, v_A=0 and every spherical average is nonnegative, so the local submean inequality holds there. Away from the boundary, it is either u-c or locally 0. The local characterization of continuous subharmonicity proves the patching claim. This proof covers non-smooth level sets and requires neither regular values nor gradients being nonzero.

5. If u is unbounded above on a component A of {u>c}, then, for any d>c, it is unbounded above on at least one component B of {u>d} inside A. Otherwise the individual extended v_B's would all be bounded. By Step 3 at most one such component could exist; if there were just one, its boundedness plus u<=d on the rest of A would bound u on A, a contradiction. Starting with A=R^n and increasing integer levels therefore selects a nested sequence A_m of components of {u>m}, each with sup_A_m u=infinity. The selection is countable and existential; it is not an algorithm for an arbitrary unspecified u. This is a mechanism rather than an unsupported assertion that high vertices lie in common components.

6. Select x_m in A_m. Since x_m,x_{m+1} lie in the connected open set A_m, they can be joined by a finite polygon P_m contained in A_m. The elementary proof of polygonal connectedness uses the clopen subset of points reachable by finite polygonal chains: an open ball at each point gives both relative openness and openness of its complement. Parametrize P_m continuously on [m,m+1], with a finite subdivision, and concatenate. Shared endpoints ensure continuity. Every point on P_m has u>m, so all tails, including segment interiors, have u(gamma(t))>floor(t). For a compact K, continuity bounds u on K by M_K; every P_m for m>M_K misses K. Only finitely many earlier polygons, each finite, can meet K. Thus the pieces are locally finite in space and gamma is proper: gamma^{-1}(K) is closed and contained in a bounded parameter interval. Properness implies |gamma(t)| -> infinity. Loops or repeated points within a finite stage are harmless; global injectivity is not proved or required. Degenerate segments can be omitted or parametrized constantly. Countably many stages occupy unbounded parameter time; no finite-time accumulation is permitted by this parametrization.

This proves the continuous subharmonic existence theorem, and hence the exact harmonic question, using standard subharmonic comparison, the ball Poisson formula, compactness, and elementary topology. It also exposes the precise gap in a weaker high-vertices argument: segment interiors and compact returns remain uncontrolled unless the complete connecting polygons stay in growing superlevel sets.

## Boundary controls and status

The bounded counterexample in the original problem is u=max(-1,-r^(2-n)) for n>2, with u(0)=-1 by extension. It is constant near 0, harmonic for r>1, continuous at r=1, and its outward derivative jumps from 0 to n-2. Distributionally Delta u=(n-2) surface_measure_{S^{n-1}}, which is positive; there is no mass at 0. Its supremum is 0, approached as r -> infinity. It refutes the replacement of harmonic nonconstancy by subharmonic nonconstancy, but lies outside the unbounded-above theorem. For n=2 this displayed formula degenerates to the constant -1; the n>2 qualification is essential.

The independent path proof works in n>=2 and the convex one-dimensional case can be handled separately. The exact requested range remains n>=3.
