## Turn1: finite-support obstruction and integral torsion

A finitely generated subgroup of the abelian restricted base has finitely many supporting coordinates, hence is a finitely generated free abelian subgroup of a finite-rank integer lattice. If K were trivial the kernel would be trivial. Otherwise every K element is supported within the fixed finite union S of supports of its generators. Conjugation shifts a nonempty support by a nonzero displacement; all forward iterates cannot stay in S. Thus K=1 and H is infinite cyclic. The orientation choice changes the sign of the shift without affecting the contradiction. The proof is a genuine arbitrary-ring deduction from the credited splitting theorem, not an FP2 algorithm.

## Turn2: derived kernel equals the actual subdirect commutator

For finite products of nonabelian simple groups, a normal subgroup containing a nontrivial coordinate projection contains that whole coordinate factor: commutation with a suitable single-coordinate element produces a nontrivial element in that factor, and normality plus simplicity completes it. A subdirect subgroup is therefore a product of twisted diagonal blocks, by induction on factors and the kernel of the last projection. A graph homomorphism from the preceding simple blocks to the last simple group has exactly one nontrivial image, because distinct block images commute and nonabelian simplicity excludes two surjective commuting images.

Within the product itself a diagonal block is self-normalizing: a normalizing tuple induces the same inner automorphism in every identified coordinate, and their ratios are central; a nonabelian simple group has trivial center. Coordinate permutations in a larger semidirect ambient group are not relevant. These blocks are perfect. For a subdirect H of Gi with Si=Gi′ nonabelian simple, D=H′ projects onto every Si. Every h in H∩∏Si normalizes D; the self-normalizer gives h∈D. Thus H′=H∩∏Si, not merely equality of its projections. H_ab injects into the product of the torsion-free abelian Gi/Si.

## Turn5: finite-piece centralizers and the failed abstract proxy

If a finite-piece map centralizes a one-bump map and has initial slope1, it fixes an initial interval.
