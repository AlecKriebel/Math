The source is Problem 10.4 in the AIM workshop list *Degenerations in
algebraic geometry*.  The preceding record supplies notation that is missing
from the extracted problem.  Let
\[
E=H^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(d)),\qquad
n=d+1,\qquad k=r+1,
\]
so that \(g^r_d\)'s are points of \(G=G(k,E)\), of dimension
\(N=k(n-k)\).  For \(x\in\mathbb P^1\), the filtration by order of
vanishing at \(x\) is a complete osculating flag \(F_\bullet(x)\).
Given pairwise distinct points \(x_1,\ldots,x_s\) and partitions
\(\lambda_i\subseteq k\times(n-k)\), form the **scheme-theoretic**
intersection
\[
 S=S(\lambda_\bullet;x_\bullet)
   :=\bigcap_{i=1}^s\Omega_{\lambda_i}(F_\bullet(x_i))
   \subseteq G.
\]
The case in the problem has
\[
 \sum_i|\lambda_i|=N-1,                                      \tag{1.1}
\]
so the Eisenbud--Harris proper-intersection theorem makes \(S\) a
projective Cohen--Macaulay curve (when nonempty).  All \(x_i\) are assumed
real and distinct.

With that notation, the recovered question is:

> **Problem 10.4.** Is the entire complex curve \(S_{\mathbb C}\) smooth?
> Equivalently, can \(S\) have a conjugate pair of nonreal singular points,
> even though every real point of \(S\) is smooth?

The OCR phrase at the end of the record, “B. Osserman suggests:”, is not a
missing hypothesis or proposed continuation of Problem 10.4.  Inspection of
the next source line shows that it introduces Problem 10.6, concerning a
degeneration proof of the Mukhin--Tarasov--Varchenko theorem.  Example 10.5
in the source treats five single-box conditions on \(G(2,5)\) and suggests
testing longer partitions or \(G(2,6)\).

status_literature
The general question remains open in the literature located in this run.
Levinson formulates precisely this assertion as Conjecture 4.4: for a real
point of \(M_{0,s}\), the corresponding **complex** fiber should be smooth.
His Corollary 2.9 proves that the curve is reduced and that all its real
points are smooth, but explicitly leaves open conjugate nonreal
singularities.  His Example 4.3 analyzes the five-box problem on \(G(2,5)\)
by iterated discriminants and sum-of-squares identities.  An iterated
discriminant gives a necessary containing locus for singular fibers, not
automatically the exact singular locus; this distinction is retained here.

The results used for the baseline are:

- Eisenbud--Harris properness gives the expected dimension for intersections
  with distinct osculation points.
- Mukhin--Tarasov--Varchenko prove reduced real transversality in the
  zero-dimensional case.
- Speyer constructs a flat Cohen--Macaulay extension over
  \(\overline M_{0,s}\).
- Levinson proves reducedness and smoothness of the real locus for the
  one-dimensional fibers.
- Gillespie--Levinson study the topology, monodromy, and \(K\)-theory of
  these curves, while noting that \(K\)-theoretic data alone does not decide
  complex smoothness.

Targeted searches through 2026-07-23 used the exact problem and conjecture
phrases, the title and forward literature around Levinson's paper, and
arXiv/web searches for “one-dimensional osculating Schubert curve
smoothness” and variants.  They also checked the later type-\(B\) analogue
of Gillespie--Levinson--Purbhoo.  No general proof or counterexample was
located.  This is not an exhaustive claim about all unpublished work, so the
status is recorded as **open** rather than “known unsolved” in an absolute
sense.

### 3. Definitions and assumptions

For \(0\leq j\leq n\), use the vanishing-flag convention
\[
 F_j(x)=H^0\!\left(\mathbb P^1,
    \mathcal O(n-1)(-(n-j)x)\right),\qquad \dim F_j(x)=j.       \tag{3.1}
\]
The partition \(\lambda\) lies in the \(k\times(n-k)\) rectangle and
\(\Omega_\lambda(F_\bullet(x))\) has codimension \(|\lambda|\).
Empty partitions impose no condition and are ignored when counting the
number of nonempty conditions.

All assertions below concern the projective scheme \(S\), not merely its
closed-point set.  Condition (1.1), properness, and Cohen--Macaulayness give
purity of dimension one.  For real, pairwise distinct \(x_i\), reducedness
and smoothness at real points are imported from Levinson.  The Plücker
degree is
\[
 D=\deg_{\rm Pl}(S)
   =\int_{G(k,n)}\sigma_\square\prod_i\sigma_{\lambda_i}.      \tag{3.2}
\]

For the all-single-box calculation on \(G(2,n)\), with \(n\geq3\), put
\[
 E=\mathbb C[z]_{\leq n-1},\qquad
 s=\dim G(2,n)-1=2n-5.
\]
For a finite \(x\), define the alternating form
\[
 B_x(f,g)=f(x)g'(x)-f'(x)g(x)\in\Lambda^2E^*.                 \tag{3.3}
\]
Its vanishing on \(\Lambda^2U\) is exactly the single-box Schubert
condition at \(x\).  A homogeneous formulation using the first jet of a
binary form will be used whenever a marked point is at infinity.

### 4. Approach map

Three routes give rigorous progress.

1. **Opposite flags and Richardson geometry.**  With at most two nonempty
   conditions, a projective change of coordinate makes the flags standard
   and opposite.  The intersection is then a one-dimensional Richardson
   variety, hence normal and therefore smooth.

2. **A low-degree real certificate.**  Since \(S\) is reduced, a projective
   curve of degree at most two is either a line, a conic, or a union of two
   lines.  Any singularity in the last case is forced to be real.  Thus the
   known smoothness of \(S(\mathbb R)\) rules it out.  Schubert calculus then
   supplies explicit longer-partition cases on \(G(2,6)\).

3. **An exact Jacobian reduction for boxes on \(G(2,n)\).**  The box
   conditions are Wronskian hyperplanes.  Dependence of their differentials
   at \(U\) is equivalent to the existence of a nonzero linear combination
   of the alternating forms \(B_{x_i}\) whose radical contains \(U\).  This
   packages the singular-fiber locus as a closed projective incidence over
   the point-configuration space.

Two tempting shortcuts fail.  Smoothness of the real locus alone does not
exclude a conjugate pair of singularities, and normality of each individual
Schubert variety does not imply normality of an intersection of three or
more of them.

### 7. Detailed proof, derivation, or heuristic

#### 7.1 Proof of Theorem 5.1

Let \(x\ne y\).  Formula (3.1) gives
\[
 F_j(x)\cap F_{n-j}(y)=0.
\]
Indeed, a section in this intersection vanishes to order at least \(n-j\)
at \(x\) and at least \(j\) at \(y\), hence has at least \(n\) zeros counted
with multiplicity, while its degree is \(n-1\).  Thus
\(F_\bullet(x)\) and \(F_\bullet(y)\) are opposite complete flags.  This is
also visible by using \({\rm PGL}_2\) to send \(x,y\) to \(0,\infty\).

With two nonempty conditions, their scheme-theoretic intersection is a
Richardson variety for opposite flags.  If it is nonempty, (1.1) gives
dimension one.  Richardson varieties are irreducible and normal.  A
one-dimensional normal Noetherian local domain is a discrete valuation ring
at each closed point, so the curve is regular.  Over the perfect field
\(\mathbb C\), a regular finite-type scheme is smooth.  With only one
nonempty condition, the same argument uses normality of the Schubert
variety itself.  If the intersection is empty, smoothness is vacuous.

#### 7.2 Proof of Theorem 5.2

Speyer's Cohen--Macaulay construction and expected dimension make \(S\)
purely one-dimensional; Levinson proves that, for distinct real osculation
points, \(S\) is reduced.  Each irreducible projective curve component has
positive Plücker degree.

A reduced pure projective curve of total degree one is a line.  For total
degree two, either it is an irreducible plane conic or it is the union of two
distinct lines.  One way to see the irreducible assertion is that a
nondegenerate irreducible curve spanning \(\mathbb P^r\) has degree at least
\(r\); hence a degree-two curve spans at most a plane.  Over \(\mathbb C\),
an irreducible plane conic is smooth, since a singular quadratic form
factors.

It remains to consider two lines.  If they are disjoint, their union is
smooth.  If they meet, their unique intersection point is the unique
singular point.  Complex conjugation preserves the real curve and permutes
its two irreducible components, so it fixes their unique intersection.
That point is therefore real, contradicting Levinson's theorem that every
real point of \(S\) is smooth.  Hence no singular case occurs.

For (5.1), let \(m=|\lambda^\vee|=N-|\lambda|=q+1\).  Repeated Pieri gives
\[
 \sigma_\square^m=\sum_{|\nu|=m}f^\nu\sigma_\nu.
\]
Poincaré duality says
\(\int_G\sigma_\lambda\sigma_\nu=\delta_{\nu,\lambda^\vee}\), and therefore
\[
 D=\int_G\sigma_\lambda\sigma_\square^{q+1}
   =f^{\lambda^\vee}.
\]
The two hook-length calculations in Corollary 5.3 now prove the stated
\(G(2,6)\) cases.

#### 7.3 Proof of Proposition 5.4

The homogeneous Wronskian is the linear map
\[
 \mathcal W:\Lambda^2E\longrightarrow
 H^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(2n-4)),\qquad
 f\wedge g\longmapsto fg'-f'g.                               \tag{7.1}
\]
Intrinsically, for binary forms \(F,G\) of degree \(n-1\), this is (up to
a nonzero scalar and sign) the first transvectant
\(F_XG_Y-F_YG_X\); thus (7.1) is not tied to the affine coordinate.
For finite \(x\), composing (7.1) with evaluation at \(x\) gives \(B_x\).
Thus each box condition is a Plücker hyperplane.  The expected-codimension
theorem says that their \(s=2n-5\) equations have height \(s\) in the smooth
Grassmannian of dimension \(2n-4\).  They consequently form a local regular
sequence, so the Jacobian criterion for this local complete intersection is
exact.

At \(U=\langle f,g\rangle\), differential rank drops exactly when a
nonzero vector \(c=(c_i)\) makes the differential of \(B_c=\sum c_iB_{x_i}\)
vanish on \(T_U G\).  The affine tangent space to the Plücker cone at
\(f\wedge g\) is
\[
 f\wedge E+E\wedge g.                                        \tag{7.2}
\]
Because every \(B_{x_i}\) already vanishes at \(f\wedge g\), the radial
direction causes no projective ambiguity.  The form \(B_c\) annihilates
(7.2) if and only if
\[
 B_c(f,E)=B_c(g,E)=0,
\]
which is equivalent to \(U\subseteq\ker B_c\).  This proves (5.2).

There is no hidden global dependence among the defining hyperplanes.
Indeed,
\[
 \mathcal W(z^a\wedge z^b)=(b-a)z^{a+b-1}.
\]
Every monomial of degree \(0,\ldots,2n-4\) occurs with a nonzero
coefficient, so \(\mathcal W\) is surjective.  Evaluation functionals at at
most \(2n-3\) distinct points on polynomials of degree at most \(2n-4\) are
independent by interpolation.  Hence the \(B_{x_i}\), with
\(s=2n-5\), are independent.

Finally, the common vanishing of a degree-\((2n-4)\) Wronskian at
\(x_1,\ldots,x_s\) says that it lies in the two-dimensional vector space
\(P\cdot H^0(\mathcal O(1))\).  This proves (5.3).  Surjectivity of the
differential to the normal space of \(\ell_P\) is exactly independence of
the \(s\) differentials above, proving the transversality reformulation.

#### 7.4 Global incidence and collision behavior

The scalar \(c_i\)'s in (5.2) depend on trivializing the evaluation fibers,
so the genuinely global construction is as follows.  On
\[
 X=\operatorname{Conf}_s(\mathbb P^1)
\]
let \(p_i:X\to\mathbb P^1\) be the marked-point maps and set
\[
 \mathcal V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^s p_i^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2n-4).
\]
Wronskian evaluation is a bundle map
\[
 \Lambda^2E\otimes\mathcal O_X\longrightarrow\mathcal V.
\]
On the projective bundle \(\mathbb P_X(\mathcal V^\vee)\), the tautological
covector produces the global version of \(B_c\).  Define the closed
incidence
\[
 \mathcal I=
 \{(x_\bullet,U,[c]):\mathcal W(U)(x_i)=0\ \forall i,\
                         U\subseteq\ker B_c\}.                 \tag{7.3}
\]
Since \(G(2,E)\times_X\mathbb P_X(\mathcal V^\vee)\) is projective over
\(X\), the projection of \(\mathcal I\) to \(X\) is closed.  By Proposition
5.4, its image is exactly the locus of singular fibers.  The construction
is \({\rm PGL}_2\)-equivariant and can be descended, with the corresponding
tautological bundles, to the moduli quotient.

On a naive collision diagonal \(x_i=x_j\), the two evaluation maps agree.
The coefficient relation supported on \(i,j\) with values \(1,-1\) then
gives \(B_c=0\), so a raw Jacobian elimination necessarily acquires a
collision component (and may acquire powers of its equation).  This gives
a precise explanation for the collision factors observed in Example 10.5.
It does **not** say that Speyer's stable-boundary fiber is the same as an
intersection with a duplicated hyperplane: the stable model redistributes
conditions across components and must be analyzed separately.

### 8. Verification and attempted falsification

- **Scheme versus set.**  The all-box criterion uses the scheme cut out by
  the Plücker hyperplanes.  Expected codimension in the smooth
  Grassmannian makes it a local complete intersection, so differential rank
  loss is equivalent to scheme singularity, not merely a necessary
  set-theoretic test.
- **Purity and reducedness.**  The degree argument would fail for a double
  line or for embedded points.  Cohen--Macaulay purity and Levinson's
  reducedness exclude both.
- **Real versus complex.**  The proof never infers complex smoothness merely
  from the real locus.  It uses degree two to show that any possible
  singularity is conjugation-fixed.  The bound is sharp for arbitrary real
  reduced curves: the real cubic
  \[
  z(x^2+y^2+z^2)=0
  \]
  is a real line union a real conic, has two conjugate nonreal intersection
  singularities, and has no real singular point.
- **Reducible degree two.**  Conjugation may fix each line or swap the two
  lines.  In either case, if they meet, their unique intersection is fixed.
  Skew lines are disjoint and their union is already smooth.
- **Opposite flags.**  The zero-intersection calculation following (3.1)
  checks every \(j\), and the divisor count uses \(n>\deg\mathcal O(n-1)\).
  The argument therefore covers all partitions and is not restricted to
  smooth Schubert varieties.
- **Boundary and infinity.**  A projective change of coordinate can move
  any finite set of marked points away from infinity.  Alternatively,
  (7.1)--(7.3) use homogeneous Wronskians and evaluation line bundles, so
  no affine chart omits a possible singular point of \(S\) or a marked
  point at infinity.
- **Independence and expected dimension.**  The monomial computation proves
  surjectivity of \(\mathcal W\), interpolation proves independence of the
  evaluations, and Eisenbud--Harris properness supplies the height needed
  for the Jacobian criterion.
- **Numerical checks.**  The two \(G(2,6)\) degrees were checked both by the
  hook-length formula and by the repeated-Pieri identity (5.1).  No
  computer algebra or exhaustive enumeration was used.
- **Parity check.**  More generally, because the singular locus has no real
  points, its local normalization defects occur in conjugate pairs; hence
  the total normalization defect is even.  This is consistent with, but
  far weaker than, smoothness.

result
### 5. Main result

#### Theorem 5.1 (two-condition Richardson case)

Assume (1.1) and that at most two \(\lambda_i\) are nonempty.  Then every
nonempty one-dimensional osculating Schubert intersection \(S\) is smooth
over \(\mathbb C\).  The osculation points need only be distinct; they need
not be real.

#### Theorem 5.2 (Plücker degree at most two)

Let the \(x_i\) be pairwise distinct and real, and let \(S\) be the
one-dimensional osculating Schubert scheme in (1.1).  If
\[
 \deg_{\rm Pl}S\leq 2,
\]
then \(S_{\mathbb C}\) is smooth (possibly disconnected).

For one condition \(\lambda\) and \(q\) additional single-box conditions,
where \(|\lambda|+q=N-1\), write
\[
 \lambda^\vee_i=(n-k)-\lambda_{k+1-i}.
\]
Repeated Pieri and Poincaré duality give
\[
 \deg_{\rm Pl}S=f^{\lambda^\vee},                             \tag{5.1}
\]
the number of standard Young tableaux of shape \(\lambda^\vee\).
Consequently, \(f^{\lambda^\vee}\leq2\) is a combinatorial smoothness
certificate.

#### Corollary 5.3 (two explicit \(G(2,6)\) cases)

For distinct real osculation points, each of the following curves is smooth
over \(\mathbb C\):

1. \(\lambda=(2,2)\) together with three single-box conditions;
2. \(\lambda=(3,2)\) together with two single-box conditions.

Indeed, the complementary shapes in the \(2\times4\) rectangle are,
respectively, \((2,2)\) and \((2,1)\), and
\[
 f^{(2,2)}=\frac{4!}{3\cdot2\cdot2\cdot1}=2,\qquad
 f^{(2,1)}=\frac{3!}{3\cdot1\cdot1}=2.
\]
These are cases of exactly the “longer partitions, or \(G(2,6)\)” direction
suggested in the AIM source.

#### Proposition 5.4 (exact skew-kernel singularity criterion)

Consider the all-single-box curve in \(G(2,n)\) at \(s=2n-5\) distinct
points.  In an affine coordinate containing all \(x_i\), a point
\(U=\langle f,g\rangle\in S\) is singular if and only if there is
\([c_1:\cdots:c_s]\in\mathbb P^{s-1}\) such that
\[
\begin{split}
  &f(x_i)g'(x_i)-f'(x_i)g(x_i)=0\quad (1\leq i\leq s),\\
  &U\subseteq\ker B_c,\qquad B_c:=\sum_{i=1}^s c_iB_{x_i}.
\end{split}                                                   \tag{5.2}
\]
Here \(\ker B_c=\{h\in E:B_c(h,E)=0\}\).

Equivalently, if
\[
 {\rm Wr}:G(2,E)\longrightarrow
   \mathbb P H^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(2n-4))
\]
is the Wronski map and \(P=\prod_i(z-x_i)\), then
\[
 S={\rm Wr}^{-1}(\ell_P),\qquad
 \ell_P=\mathbb P\big(P\cdot H^0(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O(1))\big), \tag{5.3}
\]
and \(S\) is smooth precisely when \({\rm Wr}\) is transverse to
\(\ell_P\) at every point of its inverse image.

### 6. Candidate novel contribution

**Candidate claim.**  Every real osculating Schubert curve of Plücker degree
at most two is complex-smooth; in particular, the two longer-partition
\(G(2,6)\) families in Corollary 5.3 are complex-smooth.  In the
all-single-box \(G(2,n)\) family, Proposition 5.4 gives an exact,
coordinate-independent-after-bundle-packaging incidence test for every
possible hidden complex singularity.

The claim is concrete and testable: the degree is a Schubert intersection
number, and (5.2) is an exact system of equations rather than a heuristic
discriminant.  The checked papers state the real-smoothness theorem and
analyze the \(G(2,5)\) example, but no explicit statement of this degree-two
certificate, the displayed \(G(2,6)\) cases, or this skew-kernel packaging
was located.

**Novelty confidence: low.**  The proof uses elementary curve
classification and a standard tangent-space calculation, so it may be
folklore or implicit in existing work.  The two-condition theorem is
classical Richardson geometry and is included as a verified special case,
not claimed as novel.

### 9. Limitations and unresolved gaps

The general AIM problem is not solved.  The degree argument stops at two:
the displayed real cubic shows that real-smoothness alone cannot extend it
to degree three.  The Richardson proof stops at two nonempty conditions;
multi-Schubert intersections need not inherit normality.  Proposition 5.4
is an exact reduction, but it does not yet prove that the incidence
\(\mathcal I\) has empty image on the real configuration locus.  In
particular, sums \(\sum c_iB_{x_i}\) with complex coefficients can have
large radicals even when all \(x_i\) are real, and no total-positivity
argument controlling those complex coefficients was found.

The degree-two certificate relies on the known reducedness theorem for
real, distinct osculation points.  It does not assert the same conclusion
for arbitrary complex configurations, collision limits, nonreduced
schemes, or other flag varieties.  The stable boundary of Speyer's family
requires its own componentwise analysis and is not settled by the naive
collision calculation.

### 10. Concrete next steps

1. Study the rank stratification of
   \(\sum_i c_iB_{x_i}\) using Hermite interpolation.  For a singular point
   one needs a two-plane simultaneously contained in its radical and in all
   Wronskian hyperplanes; eliminating \(U\) after stratifying by
   \(\dim\ker B_c\) may be much smaller than a direct discriminant.
2. Compute the Fitting ideal of the relative cotangent sheaf using the
   bundle map in Section 7.4.  Its class on \(M_{0,s}\), and its vanishing
   order along boundary divisors, would turn the source's tautological
   bundle suggestion into an intersection-theoretic obstruction.
3. Apply (5.2) symbolically to the first unresolved all-box case on
   \(G(2,6)\) (\(s=7\)), keeping the computation exact and exploiting
   \({\rm PGL}_2\) to fix three points.  The purpose should be to identify
   rank-theoretic factors, not to run an exhaustive numerical sweep.
4. For general \(G(k,n)\), replace (7.2) by
   \(\Lambda^{k-1}U\wedge E\).  A linear dependence of box differentials is
   then equivalent to a Plücker covector annihilating that tangent space.
   Determining a useful tensor-kernel analogue of \(U\subseteq\ker B_c\)
   is the next structural lemma.
