# Turn 3: an entropy-free coupling criterion beyond finite memory

The general question remains unresolved. This turn proves equality (in fact triviality) of the two one-sided tails for a uniformly overlapping, summably continuous infinite-memory transition kernel. The classical complete-connections coupling strategy is credited to Bressaud–Fernández–Galves, *Decay of correlations for non Hölderian dynamics. A coupling approach*, EJP4 (1999), especially Sections4.1–4.4. Those sections were read; the proof below supplies its own elementary bound under explicitly stated total-variation hypotheses. Neither the whole paper nor its sharper rates are imported as a theorem.

## 1. Hypotheses and conclusion

Let A be finite and let g(.|x) be a measurable probability vector for each left-infinite history x. Let v_k bound the total variation distance of g(.|x) and g(.|y) whenever the last k symbols of x,y agree, with k=0 imposing no agreement. Assume

    0<=v_(k+1)<=v_k,  v_0<1,  sum_(k>=0) v_k<infinity.

Write epsilon=1−v_0>0. A stationary process is compatible if its next-symbol conditional distribution given its entire past is g, almost surely. Assume such a process exists.

**Theorem.** Its left and right remote tails are trivial. There is at most one compatible stationary law. A uniform remote-future coupling bound after n generated symbols is

    b(n)=min(1, inf_(1<=l<=n) [(1−epsilon^l)^floor(n/l)
                              + sum_(k>=l) v_k]).           (1.1)

In particular b(n)→0. No assertion about the bilateral tail follows from the argument. No Markov or finite-memory condition is imposed, but the uniform overlap and continuity assumptions are substantial restrictions absent from the source.

## 2. Constructing the coupling and a stopping-time bound

Starting from any two histories, at each time use a maximal coupling of their next-symbol distributions, independently of earlier auxiliary randomness given the current histories. This has disagreement probability equal to their total variation distance. It can be realized with a fresh uniform U so that U<=epsilon always forces agreement: first use a common-output branch of length equal to the common mass, then the residual disjoint distributions. Thus, regardless of the histories, a block of l auxiliary uniforms all at most epsilon forces l consecutive equal outputs.

Let tau be the first time that l consecutive generated outputs have agreed. In disjoint length-l blocks the forcing uniform events are independent, each of probability epsilon^l. Therefore

    P(tau>n)<=(1−epsilon^l)^floor(n/l).                     (2.1)

This is only an upper bound; actual agreement can happen much sooner. At time tau, the histories share at least l terminal symbols. Conditional on the entire coupling history at this stopping time, the probability that the first later disagreement occurs after k−l further agreements is at most v_k. A union bound gives

    P(any later disagreement | history at tau)
       <= sum_(k>=l) v_k.                                 (2.2)

One can first sum over a finite future horizon and then increase it; no assertion that tau is the final disagreement time is used. Combining(2.1)–(2.2), the coupled output sequences agree at every coordinate n+1,n+2,... with probability at least1 minus the displayed bound in(1.1). The coupling inequality gives the same bound for total variation of the entire remote-future path laws. It is uniform over the two initial histories.

To see convergence to0, first choose a fixed large l to make the summable tail small, then send n to infinity in the first term. Finally increase l. This ordered limit requires no rate uniform in l.

## 3. From the path coupling to both tail zero-one laws

For the given stationary compatible process, its conditional future law given a past history is generated by the successive g kernels. This follows by the conditional chain rule for finite future words, followed by uniqueness of a measure on the countable product. The uniform estimate applies for almost every conditioning history; comparison with a mixture of the other histories gives

    |P(B | sigma(X_k:k<=0))−P(B)| <= b(n) a.s.

for every B in sigma(X_k:k>=n+1). Equivalently the alpha-mixing coefficient between these two fields is at most b(n). Finite-alphabet product spaces supply regular conditional distributions, so this statement can also be expressed as total variation of the conditional remote-future law. A fixed B suffices for the argument.

Let A be a right-tail event and let C be any finite-coordinate cylinder supported in[−r,r]. Since A belongs to every future field, stationarity and the estimate above imply |P(A intersect C)−P(A)P(C)|<=b(n) for arbitrarily large separations n. Hence A is independent of every cylinder. A monotone-class argument makes it independent of the entire process field, including itself; P(A) is0 or1.

For a left-tail event interchange which event is in the remote past and which is in the fixed central cylinder. The same alpha coefficient controls the correlation, without reversing g or assuming reversibility. This proves the left-tail zero-one law as well.

For uniqueness, start one coupled chain with the past drawn from one stationary compatible law and the other from a second law. Their future blocks at times n+1,...,n+L differ in total variation by at most b(n). Stationarity identifies these with their respective blocks at1,...,L. Let n→infinity. All finite block laws coincide, giving equality of the stationary laws. This does not by itself prove existence for every merely measurable g.

## 4. A genuine infinite-memory example

On binary histories define

    g(1|x)=1/2 +(1/4) sum_(j>=1) 2^(−j)(2 x_(−j)−1),
    g(0|x)=1−g(1|x).

All probabilities lie in[1/4,3/4]. Matching k terminal symbols leaves a maximal difference exactly

    v_k=2^(−k−1),  epsilon=1/2,
    sum_(j>=k) v_j=2^(−k).

The extrema are attained by choosing all unmatched symbols oppositely. Each remote coordinate changes the kernel, so it is not a finite-order transition kernel. The series is uniformly convergent, making the append-a-symbol transition on the compact history space Feller. An invariant measure exists: take Cesàro averages of iterates of any initial measure, choose a weakly convergent subsequence by compactness, and use the Feller property and the telescoping difference of the averages to verify invariance. The usual stationary Markov extension on histories then gives a compatible two-sided symbol process. The history coordinates shift consistently, so the kernel really is the conditional next-symbol law. Section3 proves uniqueness and both tail zero-one laws.

For example choosing l=floor((log_2 n)/2), when l>=1, gives the explicit bound

    b(n)<=(1−2^(−l))^floor(n/l)+2^(−l).

It tends to0. It is a deliberately coarse bound, not a claim of an optimal mixing rate.

## 5. Exact controls and scope

verify_turn3.py checks maximal-coupling marginals and disagreement mass with exact fractions, continuity bounds for all pairs of binary finite-history truncations through length7, and finite-horizon agreement-run probabilities against both terms of(1.1). The controls do not replace the stopping-time proof or establish a general stationary tail theorem.

The obstruction left after this turn is that arbitrary finite-alphabet stationary laws may have discontinuous conditional kernels, no uniform overlap, and nonsummable memory. Conditioning on a hypothetical common tail to force these hypotheses would be circular. No such reduction is claimed.
