## Mathematical audit

### 1. Structural restrictions: PASS

The finite residual is characteristic because precomposition by any automorphism permutes all finite-target homomorphisms. Thus characteristic simplicity leaves precisely the alternatives residual finiteness or absence of every nontrivial finite image. This is stronger than the absence of a particular finite quotient and is used correctly thereafter.

Local finiteness makes element orders finite; finitely many automorphism-orbits then leave a finite list of orders. Taking their least common multiple proves finite exponent. No assertion that finite exponent implies solubility, residual finiteness, or global nilpotence is made.

An abelian torsion characteristically simple nontrivial group is elementary abelian: a nonzero characteristic p-torsion subgroup is all of it. Linear functionals separate its nonzero vectors in a finite field. Consequently the requested non-residually-finite group cannot be abelian; its characteristic derived subgroup and center force perfectness and centerlessness.

The remaining deductions check: a proper finite-index subgroup gives a nontrivial finite coset action; a finite conjugacy class gives such an index for its centralizer; a finite normal subgroup gives a finite conjugation image and hence a central subgroup. The centerless conclusion then rules out the last two possibilities for nonidentity elements and nontrivial finite normal subgroups.

For a nonidentity word, the subgroup generated by its values is characteristic and nontrivial, hence all of the group. Sets of products of at most m values and their inverses are increasing invariant sets. Finitely many element-orbits imply a common finite m. The commutator version gives ordinary finite commutator width because an inverse commutator is a commutator with the inputs interchanged.

The pseudofinite exclusion is expressly conditional on prime-power exponent and perfectness. A single sentence can assert a nonidentity element, the exponent identity, and a fixed commutator-width bound, together with the finitely axiomatized group laws. A finite model would be a nontrivial perfect finite p-group, which cannot exist. It does not follow that all locally finite groups are pseudofinite, and the package never uses that false implication.

### 2. Finite-target compactness and countable reflection: PASS

This is the most consequential infinite argument. The following potential failure points were checked separately.

1. **Finite witness lemma.** For fixed finite F, the product F^G is compact. The multiplication constraints and the requirement that x have nonidentity image are clopen. If every finitely generated subgroup containing x had a suitable homomorphism, each finite family of constraints would be satisfiable by taking a subgroup containing all elements mentioned. Extending the function outside that subgroup is harmless. Compactness would give the forbidden global homomorphism. The contrapositive gives one finitely generated subgroup whose every map to F kills x. Under local finiteness this subgroup is finite.
2. **Countably many choices.** H_n is countable, so there are countably many pairs, characteristic-generation expressions, and pairs consisting of an element and a finite isomorphism type. Finite groups have a countable set of isomorphism types: for each finite underlying cardinality there are only finitely many multiplication tables. All finite-target tests, rather than only finite quotients of a selected order, are therefore included.
3. **Automorphisms really restrict onto H.** A_(n+1) is a subgroup, not merely a collection of selected forward maps. Closing H_(n+1) under its entire action ensures invariance under inverses too. If alpha belongs to A_i and h belongs to H_j, then a later stage contains both, and both alpha(h) and alpha^(-1)(h) lie in that stage. Thus the union H is mapped bijectively to itself. No extension of an arbitrary partial automorphism is being assumed.
4. **Orbit bound has the correct direction.** Every two elements of H in one ambient orbit are joined by a selected automorphism whose restriction is an automorphism of H. Each nonempty ambient-orbit intersection therefore lies inside a single H-orbit. Additional H-automorphisms may merge different intersections, so omega(H) can decrease; it cannot exceed the ambient bound.
5. **Characteristic simplicity.** Given nonidentity x in a characteristic subgroup C of H, every y in H eventually occurs alongside x. The chosen finite expression for y uses ambient automorphisms that later restrict to H. All its factors then belong to C, forcing y in C. This uses characteristic generation by the full ambient automorphism orbit, not the generally false assumption that normal generation suffices.
6. **No new finite image survives.** For every x and every finite representative F, a witness subgroup is included at a later stage. Every homomorphism from H to F kills x by restriction. An arbitrary finite target is handled by composing with an isomorphism to its representative. Therefore every finite-target homomorphism is trivial. This is precisely the step that arbitrary countable subgroups would lack.
7. **Other hypotheses.** Infinitely many chosen initial elements force countable infinitude. Subgroups inherit local finiteness. Nontriviality plus absence of finite images implies failure of residual finiteness.

This construction preserves all required hypotheses and any specified countable subset, conditional on an ambient example already existing. It provides no effective construction of an example. The statement that a countable locally finite group is a union of an increasing chain of finite groups follows by enumerating it and generating successive finite subgroups.

The source attribution for ordinary countable characteristic-simplicity reflection is supported by Wilson's publisher extract, which attributes it to Hall. The extra orbit and finite-image preservation is independently justified above; it does not depend on an unavailable original paper. [Wilson's article and extract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-proceedings-of-the-cambridge-philosophical-society/article/abs/groups-with-many-characteristically-simple-subgroups/9EFE7A1D1CEEEE31C3F97B0400931332).

### 3. Pointed Cantor A5 functions and atomic sums: PASS

The function group's topology is essential. Compactness makes every clopen a finite union of prefix cylinders. A finite list of continuous functions has a common finite clopen refinement, so the subgroup it generates embeds in a finite direct power of A5. This proves local finiteness; countability and infinitude also follow from the prefix description. Evaluation at any point with nonidentity value separates that element in the finite group A5, proving residual finiteness.

For characteristic simplicity, a nontrivial characteristic subgroup is normal. A function nontrivial on one clopen U yields a nontrivial localized commutator. Its constant-on-U values form a nontrivial normal subgroup of A5, hence all of A5. Both U and every other nonempty clopen V avoiding the distinguished point have nonempty clopen complements containing that point. Cantor homogeneity provides a homeomorphism matching U to V while fixing the point. The induced function-group automorphism transports every localized A5 subgroup. Every function is a product of finitely many such localized functions.

The exact orbit count, not just an orbit bound for a selected subgroup, is proved correctly. Conjugation by S5 is transitive on each of the A5 element-order classes 1, 2, 3, and 5. Locally constant automorphism choices normalize all nonidentity values fiberwise. Two functions using the same nonidentity order types then have finite clopen partitions that admit a pointed matching homeomorphism. The mandatory nonempty identity fiber is what makes this argument uniform. Thus a subgroup of Aut(B) already gives at most eight orbits. The eight realizable types have distinct orders 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, and 30. These full-automorphism invariants prevent any further fusion, giving exactly eight.

For the atomic direct sum, a supported involution has centralizer index 15 per occupied coordinate. The indices 15^m are distinct while all such elements have order two. This correctly refutes the finite-order-spectrum shortcut. It does not contradict the Cantor function-group calculation: clopen regions can be subdivided, whereas atomic supports carry cardinality information.

The audit independently checked A5 simplicity by enumerating unions of its five inner conjugacy classes, an approach distinct from the author's normal-closure test. It also built pointed prefix substitutions for all eight patterns with unequal initial partition sizes. Those finite checks illustrate the proof's construction and do not replace the general Cantor-space argument.

### 4. Dense McLain group: PASS

Finitary rationally indexed upper triangular matrices form a locally nilpotent algebra. Finitely many group elements live on a finite index set and hence in a finite unitriangular p-group. Inverses have finite support there. Elementary transvections generate the group. Density supplies an intermediate index, making each transvection a commutator and proving perfectness.

The characteristic-simplicity extraction is valid with the stated fresh outer indices. Writing E=E_(r,i), the unused outer column gives g^(-1)E=E, and the first commutator reduces exactly to I+EA. A second commutator with E_(j,s) isolates A_(i,j)E_(r,s). The coefficient is nonzero in the prime field, so taking an integer power normalizes it to one. Positive rational affine changes of indices are actual order-preserving bijections of Q and transport the ordered pair (r,s) to any desired pair. Characteristicity, powers, and elementary generation now give the whole group.

This does not prove simplicity and does not need it. All finite images are nilpotent, because finitely many lifts lie in a finite unitriangular group; they are also perfect as images of a perfect group. They are therefore trivial.

Regular unipotents on arbitrarily long finite chains have order the least p-power at least the chain length. This realizes unbounded p-power orders and forces infinitely many automorphism-orbits. No finite truncation repairs the infinite construction's spectrum defect.

Independent sparse-matrix calculations reproduce every advertised extraction case, also checking the first commutator formula separately. They extend Jordan-order checks through dimension 29. A negative control demonstrates failure of row extraction if a necessary fresh outer-index condition is removed.

### 5. Extraspecial group and class-two amalgamation: PASS

The alternating bilinear cocycle proves associativity; its alternating property proves the stated inverse and odd-prime exponent. Finite support gives local finiteness. The commutator is exactly the form value, so nondegeneracy gives the asserted common center and derived subgroup.

The proposed automorphisms are valid for every invertible symplectic similitude and linear functional. Scaling all first vectors of the hyperbolic pairs realizes every nonzero multiplier and is a well-defined bijection of the finite-support space. Any two nonzero vectors lie in a common finite-dimensional nondegenerate hyperbolic subspace. Symplectic basis extension there gives a form-preserving map carrying one to the other, which extends by identity on the remaining hyperbolic coordinates. Linear functionals adjust the central coordinate. Consequently the three claimed sets are transitive under actual automorphisms. Identity and the characteristic center prevent their fusion under the full automorphism group.

For the finite residual, a finite-index kernel cannot be contained in the finite center, since otherwise the whole group would be finite. A noncentral kernel element has all center values as commutators, so normality forces the entire center into every finite-index kernel. Conversely, linear functionals on the elementary abelian central quotient separate every element outside the center. Thus the finite residual is exactly the center. It is nontrivial and proper, disqualifying the group from characteristic simplicity.

The amalgam obstruction also checks with the declared commutator convention. One embedding makes c a commutator, necessarily central in a class-at-most-two common extension; the other makes its commutator with v equal the nontrivial common element d. Injectivity on the amalgamated elementary abelian subgroup is impossible. This only obstructs class-at-most-two extensions. It does not obstruct all finite or locally finite amalgams.

The independent model uses nonsymmetric Heisenberg coordinates, with the explicit change z=a+(1/2)x dot y relating it to the author's alternating coordinates. Multiplication-table tests check every selected automorphism on every ordered pair in all three test groups, including rank two. Exhaustive associativity is checked for orders 27 and 125. These finite groups are themselves residually finite; the infinite finite-residual argument remains a separate proof.
