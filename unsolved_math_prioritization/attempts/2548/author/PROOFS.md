# Proofs and construction boundaries for Kourovka 21.39

## Scope and notation

The original existence question remains unresolved in this investigation. All assertions proved below are reductions or checks on explicit boundary examples. None is claimed to be new. A group is locally finite if every finitely generated subgroup is finite; it is characteristically simple if its only characteristic subgroups are 1 and itself. Write omega(G) for the number of Aut(G)-orbits on elements, and R(G) for the intersection of kernels of all homomorphisms from G to finite groups. Residual finiteness means R(G)=1. All groups are abstract groups, and [x,y]=x^(-1)y^(-1)xy.

The finite controls in verify_math.py supplement these proofs. They do not certify a nonexistent solution to the original problem.

## Approach 1 Structural restrictions and the finite quotient barrier

### Proposition 1.1

If G is characteristically simple, then either G is residually finite or every homomorphism from G to a finite group is trivial.

Proof. An automorphism permutes the kernels of homomorphisms to finite groups by precomposition. Thus R(G) is characteristic. It is consequently 1 or G. The second alternative says precisely that every such homomorphism has kernel G. QED.

### Proposition 1.2

Suppose G satisfies all four conditions sought in 21.39. Then:

1. G is infinite and has finite exponent.
2. G is nonabelian, perfect, and centerless.
3. G has no proper subgroup of finite index, no nontrivial finite normal subgroup, and no nonidentity element with finite conjugacy class.
4. For every group word w, either w is an identity of G or w has finite width in G, allowing values and their inverses. In particular G has finite commutator width.

Proof. Local finiteness makes every element torsion. Pick one representative from each of the finitely many automorphism orbits. Every element order occurs among the finitely many orders of those representatives, so their least common multiple is an exponent. A finite group is residually finite, hence G is infinite.

For completeness, a nontrivial abelian torsion characteristically simple group A is elementary abelian. Choose a prime p dividing some element order. The subgroup A[p]={a:pa=0} is nontrivial and characteristic, hence is A. Thus A is a vector space over F_p. A nonzero vector can be included in a basis and separated from zero by a linear map to F_p, so A is residually finite. Our G cannot be abelian. Since G' and Z(G) are characteristic, G'=G and Z(G)=1.

By Proposition 1.1 every finite quotient is trivial. A proper finite-index subgroup would give a nontrivial action on its finite coset set, hence a nontrivial finite image. If an element has finite conjugacy class, its centralizer has finite index. Therefore it centralizes all of G, and is 1. A finite normal subgroup gives a conjugation map from G to a finite symmetric group; the map is trivial, so that subgroup is central and hence trivial.

Let W be the set consisting of 1, all w-values, and their inverses. The subgroup generated by W is characteristic. If w is not an identity this subgroup is G. Each W^m is Aut(G)-invariant, and the increasing union of these sets is G. For each of the finitely many element orbits choose the least m for which a representative lies in W^m. The maximum of these finitely many integers gives W^m=G. For the commutator word, inverses of commutators are commutators, so this is ordinary finite commutator width. QED.

### Corollary 1.3

A soluble characteristically simple locally finite group is elementary abelian and residually finite. The same conclusion holds for a nilpotent such group. In particular, a construction with a global bound on derived length or nilpotency class cannot answer 21.39.

Proof. If a nonabelian characteristically simple group were soluble, it would be perfect by the preceding argument, contradicting termination of its derived series. The abelian case was handled above. QED.

### Proposition 1.4 A conditional pseudofinite exclusion

No nontrivial perfect group P of exponent p^e, for p prime, with finitely many automorphism orbits is pseudofinite.

Here pseudofinite means that every first-order sentence true in the infinite group has a finite model. This proposition does not assert that every candidate in 21.39 is a p-group.

Proof. Proposition 1.2's width argument applies to any perfect group with finitely many automorphism orbits: there is an integer d such that every x is a product of d commutators. The conjunction of the group axioms and the following is one first-order sentence:

- there is an element different from 1;
- every x satisfies x^(p^e)=1;
- every x is a product of d commutators.

Any finite group satisfying this sentence has order a power of p, by Cauchy's theorem, and is perfect. A nontrivial finite p-group is nilpotent and cannot be perfect: its lower central series would be constant if P'=P, whereas a nilpotent lower central series terminates. Thus the sentence has no finite group model. QED.

This excludes a pseudofinite construction in the p-group regime. It does not exclude a union of finite p-groups: a locally finite group need not be pseudofinite.

## Approach 2 Countable reflection without losing the finite quotient obstruction

The ordinary reflection of characteristic simplicity to a countable subgroup is classical; see J. S. Wilson's 1979 article cited in SOURCE_VERIFICATION.json. The proof here explicitly preserves two further requirements needed for this problem. No novelty claim is made.

### Lemma 2.1 Finite witness against a fixed finite target

Let F be a finite group, G any group, and x in G. Suppose every homomorphism G -> F kills x. Then there is a finitely generated subgroup K of G containing x such that every homomorphism K -> F kills x. If G is locally finite, K is finite.

Proof. Give F the discrete topology and use compactness of F^G. In this space impose all the closed constraints f(ab)=f(a)f(b), f(1)=1, and f(x) != 1. If every finitely generated subgroup containing x admitted a homomorphism to F not killing x, every finite subcollection of constraints would be satisfiable: put all group elements appearing in those constraints, and x, into one finitely generated subgroup and use such a homomorphism there, extending the function arbitrarily outside that subgroup. Compactness would then give a homomorphism G -> F not killing x. This is a contradiction. A finite unsatisfiable subcollection involves finitely many elements; the subgroup they generate together with x is the required K. QED.

### Theorem 2.2

If G is an example requested by 21.39 and omega(G)=k, then every countable subset of G is contained in a countable subgroup H that is itself an example, with omega(H) <= k. In particular, a positive answer exists if and only if a countably infinite example exists.

Proof. Enlarge the given countable subset by countably many distinct elements of G, which is possible because G is infinite. Let H_0 be the subgroup it generates and let A_0 be the trivial subgroup of Aut(G). We construct increasing countable subgroups H_n <= G and A_n <= Aut(G).

At stage n make the following countably many choices.

(a) For every x,y in H_n which lie in the same Aut(G)-orbit, choose an automorphism alpha with alpha(x)=y.

(b) For each x != 1 and y in H_n, choose a finite expression

y = alpha_1(x)^(epsilon_1) ... alpha_m(x)^(epsilon_m),

where epsilon_i is 1 or -1 and alpha_i belongs to Aut(G). Such an expression exists: the subgroup generated by the entire Aut(G)-orbit of x is nontrivial and characteristic, hence equals G.

(c) For every x != 1 in H_n and every finite group F in a countable list containing all finite groups up to isomorphism, choose a finitely generated subgroup K_(x,F) as in Lemma 2.1. Such a choice is possible by Proposition 1.1.

Let A_(n+1) be generated by A_n and all automorphisms selected in (a) and (b). Let S_n be H_n together with the union of all K_(x,F), and set

H_(n+1) = < alpha(s) : alpha in A_(n+1), s in S_n >.

Both new groups are countable. The subgroup H_(n+1) is invariant under A_(n+1), including inverses. Set H=union H_n and A=union A_n. Every alpha in A restricts to an automorphism of H, because any element of H and alpha occur together at some sufficiently late stage; the same applies to alpha^(-1).

If x,y in H are in the same global Aut(G)-orbit, they occur in one H_n and the automorphism chosen in (a) restricts to an automorphism of H taking x to y. Hence the intersections of the k global orbits with H each lie in a single Aut(H)-orbit. The number of Aut(H)-orbits is at most k; different global orbits may merge, which causes no problem.

If C is a nontrivial characteristic subgroup of H, choose x != 1 in C. For arbitrary y in H, choose n with x,y in H_n. The expression in (b) uses automorphisms restricting to H, so every factor belongs to C and y belongs to C. Thus C=H.

For a homomorphism f:H -> F to a finite group and any x != 1 in H, take a stage containing x. The subgroup K_(x,F) is in H, and the restriction of f to it kills x. Therefore f is trivial. H is nontrivial, so it is not residually finite. Local finiteness is inherited from G. The initial infinitely many distinct elements ensure that H is countably infinite. The reverse implication of the last assertion is immediate. QED.

Important distinction: simply taking an arbitrary countable subgroup would not preserve these properties. Choices (a), (b), and especially the finite-target witnesses in (c) are essential parts of the proof.

## Approach 3 Boolean functions versus atomic direct sums

### Theorem 3.1 An explicit residually finite eight-orbit group

Let X={0,1}^N be Cantor space, fix x_0 in X, and let S=A_5 with the discrete topology. Put

B = {f:X -> S continuous : f(x_0)=1},

with pointwise multiplication. Then B is countably infinite, locally finite, characteristically simple, and residually finite. It has exactly eight automorphism orbits. It therefore verifies the known side of the problem and does not answer its requested non-residually-finite side.

Proof of countability and local finiteness. Every clopen subset of X is a finite union of basic finite-prefix cylinders. Hence there are countably many clopen sets and countably many continuous maps from X to the finite discrete set S. Given finitely many such maps, refine all their fibers into one finite clopen partition. Every word in those maps is constant on each part, so the generated subgroup embeds into a finite direct power of S. Nontrivial functions with pairwise different clopen supports show infinitude.

Proof of residual finiteness. If f != 1, choose x with f(x) != 1. The evaluation homomorphism f -> f(x) maps B to the finite group S and separates f from 1.

Proof of characteristic simplicity. Let C be a nontrivial characteristic subgroup of B; it is normal. Pick f in C with f(y)=s != 1. Choose a nonempty clopen U avoiding x_0 on which f is constantly s. For a in S, let a_U be the function a on U and 1 elsewhere. Then [f,a_U]=[s,a]_U belongs to C. Because S is centerless, some such commutator is nontrivial. The set {a in S : a_U in C} is a normal subgroup of S, by conjugation with functions b_U. Since S is simple, it is all of S.

For any other nonempty clopen V avoiding x_0 there is a homeomorphism of X fixing x_0 and taking U to V. Indeed U and V are copies of Cantor space, and so are their clopen complements; a homeomorphism between the complements can be chosen to map x_0 to x_0, using the homogeneity of Cantor space. The induced pullback automorphism of B transports S_U to S_V. Thus C contains every function supported constantly on any such V. Every element of B is a product of finitely many such functions, because its nonidentity fibers are clopen and avoid x_0. Therefore C=B.

For clarity, the elementary Cantor-space facts used here follow from its finite-prefix description: any nonempty clopen is a finite disjoint union of cylinders, each a copy of X, and X is homeomorphic to a finite nonempty disjoint union of itself by a finite prefix partition. A homeomorphism of X can send any one prescribed point to another by coordinatewise addition modulo 2. Combining these facts gives the stated pointed homeomorphisms.

Proof of the orbit count. Conjugation by S_5 acts on A_5 with four orbits, represented by 1, a double transposition, a 3-cycle, and a 5-cycle; the corresponding element orders are 1,2,3,5. Thus these are also precisely the Aut(S)-orbits, since element order prevents fusion of these four sets. This finite calculation is checked exhaustively in verify_math.py.

For f in B and q in {2,3,5}, let U_q(f) be the clopen set where f has order q. The identity fiber is a nonempty clopen neighborhood of x_0. On a finite refinement of its fibers, apply automorphisms of S separately to normalize each nonidentity value to a fixed representative of its order. These piecewise automorphisms are automorphisms of B. If f and g use exactly the same subset T of the three nonidentity order types, their partitions consisting of the identity fiber and the U_q for q in T can be matched by a homeomorphism of X fixing x_0. The resulting automorphism of B takes normalized f to normalized g. Thus there are at most 2^3=8 orbits.

Every T is realizable, by assigning the chosen representatives on disjoint nonempty clopen sets avoiding x_0. Its element order is the product of the distinct primes in T, with the empty product 1. The eight orders 1,2,3,5,6,10,15,30 are distinct, so no two of these eight cases can lie in the same automorphism orbit. QED.

### Proposition 3.2 Why the atomic direct sum does not replace B

Let D be the restricted direct sum of countably many copies of A_5. Then D has exponent 30 and exactly the same eight element orders as B, but infinitely many automorphism orbits.

Proof. Let t be a double transposition in A_5. Its centralizer has order 4, hence index 15. Let d_m have coordinate t in exactly m factors and identity elsewhere. Then d_m has order 2 for every m>=1, while

[D:C_D(d_m)] = 15^m.

This follows by taking centralizers coordinatewise: only the m nonidentity coordinates impose a restriction. Centralizer index is an automorphism invariant, so these elements lie in distinct orbits. The exponent and spectrum follow by taking least common multiples of the coordinate orders. QED.

In particular, finite element-order spectrum cannot substitute for finite automorphism-orbit count. The verification code tests this false shortcut as a negative control.

## Approach 4 Dense triangular groups and the exponent obstruction

### Theorem 4.1 The McLain boundary example

For a prime p, let J be the F_p-algebra of finitely supported matrices (a_(r,s)) indexed by rational pairs r<s, with the usual matrix multiplication and zero entries otherwise. Put M=1+J. Then M is countably infinite, locally finite, characteristically simple, perfect, and has no nontrivial finite quotient. Nevertheless omega(M) is infinite.

Proof of the group law and local finiteness. Finitely many matrix entries involve only finitely many rational indices. The corresponding subalgebra is contained in the strictly upper triangular matrices on that finite ordered index set, so it is nilpotent. Thus 1+a has a finite geometric-series inverse. Every finite set of elements lies in a finite unitriangular group over F_p. There are countably many finite supports and coefficients, so M is countable.

Write t_(r,s)(a)=1+a E_(r,s). The elementary transvections generate every finite unitriangular group and hence M. For r<u<s,

[t_(r,u)(a),t_(u,s)(b)] = t_(r,s)(ab).

The density of Q supplies u for every r<s, so every transvection is a commutator and M is perfect.

Proof of characteristic simplicity. Let C be a nontrivial characteristic subgroup, hence normal, and take g=1+A in C with a nonzero entry A_(i,j). Choose a rational r less than every index in the support of A and a rational s greater than all of them. Direct matrix multiplication gives

[t_(r,i)(1),g] = 1 + sum_k A_(i,k) E_(r,k),

and then

[[t_(r,i)(1),g],t_(j,s)(1)] = t_(r,s)(A_(i,j)).

Both commutators belong to C. The first formula uses that g fixes row and column r, and the second uses that s is beyond every support index, so all undesired matrix-unit products vanish. These formulas are also checked over small finite matrix algebras by verify_math.py.

Taking a suitable integer power, since A_(i,j) is nonzero in the prime field, puts t_(r,s)(1) in C. Every order-preserving permutation of Q induces an automorphism of M by reindexing. Given any r'<s', the positive rational affine map q -> a q+b with a=(s'-r')/(s-r) and b=r'-ar maps r,s to r',s'. Thus C contains every t_(r',s')(1), and its powers contain every field coefficient. The transvections generate M, hence C=M.

Proof of the finite quotient assertion. A quotient of a locally nilpotent group is locally nilpotent: lift any finite generating set and use nilpotency of the subgroup generated by the lifts. Every finite image of M is consequently nilpotent. It is also perfect, being an image of a perfect group. A perfect nilpotent group is trivial. This proves the assertion without assuming simplicity of M.

Proof of infinitely many orbits. On an ordered set of n rational indices let N have entries 1 just above the diagonal and 0 elsewhere, and let u=I+N. In characteristic p,

u^(p^a)=I+N^(p^a).

As N^m is nonzero exactly for m<n, the order of u is p^a, where a is the least integer with p^a>=n. In particular n=p^(a-1)+1 realizes order p^a for every a>=1. Automorphisms preserve order, giving infinitely many orbits. QED.

This construction satisfies the desired characteristic and finite-quotient requirements very strongly, but the spectrum obstruction is decisive. Finite triangular truncations do not fix it: they have nontrivial centers and finite quotients.

## Approach 5 Extraspecial groups and bounded-class amalgamation

### Theorem 5.1 A non-residually-finite three-orbit group with a characteristic center

Let p be an odd prime. Let V be the countably infinite-dimensional F_p-vector space with hyperbolic basis e_1,f_1,e_2,f_2,... and alternating form beta given by beta(e_i,f_j)=delta_(i,j), beta(e_i,e_j)=beta(f_i,f_j)=0. V consists of finite-support vectors. Define E=V x F_p by

(v,a)(w,b)=(v+w, a+b+(1/2)beta(v,w)).

Then E is countably infinite, locally finite of exponent p, and has exactly three automorphism orbits. Its finite residual, center, and derived subgroup are all

Z={0} x F_p.

In particular it is not residually finite, but it is not characteristically simple.

Proof of the group structure. Bilinearity gives the cocycle identity

beta(v,w)+beta(v+w,u)=beta(w,u)+beta(v,w+u),

which proves associativity. Identity is (0,0), inverse is (-v,-a), and induction gives (v,a)^m=(mv,ma), since beta(v,v)=0. Thus the exponent is p. Every finite set uses finitely many hyperbolic basis vectors and lies in a finite subgroup. A direct calculation gives

[(v,a),(w,b)]=(0,beta(v,w)).

Nondegeneracy of beta therefore makes both Z(E) and E' equal to Z, which is nontrivial and proper.

Proof of three orbits. For T an invertible linear map with beta(Tv,Tw)=lambda beta(v,w), lambda in F_p^*, and any linear functional ell:V -> F_p, the map

(v,a) -> (Tv,lambda a+ell(v))

is an automorphism, as follows by substituting in the multiplication law. Such multipliers exist for every lambda: scale all e_i by lambda and fix all f_i. Consequently these maps are transitive on Z\{1}.

The symplectic group is transitive on V\{0}. To see this without a finiteness assumption on support, put any given nonzero v,w in a common finite-dimensional nondegenerate hyperbolic subspace. A vector v can be extended to a symplectic basis there by first choosing v' with beta(v,v')=1 and then splitting off the hyperbolic plane; repeat on its orthogonal complement. Do this for w too, map the bases to one another, and extend by identity on the remaining hyperbolic coordinates. Thus an isometry T takes v to w. Finally a linear functional ell can take any prescribed value at nonzero v, so the displayed automorphisms act transitively on all noncentral elements of E.

Identity, nonidentity center, and noncentral elements cannot fuse under any automorphism. Hence there are exactly three orbits.

Proof that R(E)=Z. Let N be the kernel of any map from E to a finite group. Then N has finite index. It cannot lie in the finite group Z because E is infinite. Choose (v,a) in N with v!=0. For every b in F_p there is w with beta(v,w)=b. Normality of N and the commutator formula imply (0,b) in N, so Z<=N. This proves Z<=R(E).

Conversely E/Z is the elementary abelian group V. Each nonzero v can be separated by a linear functional V -> F_p, which composed with E -> V gives a finite quotient separating (v,a). Thus R(E)<=Z. QED.

This shows why just combining local finiteness, bounded exponent, few automorphism orbits, and failure of residual finiteness is insufficient. A proper characteristic subgroup survives. Passing to E/Z removes that subgroup but also removes the failure of residual finiteness: the quotient is elementary abelian.

### Proposition 5.2 An exact obstruction to a naive class-two amalgamation

For every odd prime p there is an amalgam of finite exponent-p class-at-most-two groups with no embedding into any class-at-most-two group respecting both embeddings.

Proof. Let H be the Heisenberg group of order p^3 with generators x,y,z, where z is central and [x,y]=z. Let A=H x <d> with |d|=p. Let C be elementary abelian on c,d. Embed C in A by c -> z and d -> d. Let B be another copy of H with generators u,v,w and [u,v]=w, and embed C in B by c -> u and d -> w. Both embeddings are injective: in A the indicated central elements are independent; in B the subgroup <u,w> is elementary abelian of rank two.

In a class-at-most-two common extension, c=[x,y] belongs to the center. But the second embedding requires [c,v]=d. Therefore d=1, contradicting injectivity on C. QED.

The finite verification checks the embeddings and these exact commutator identities for p=3 and 5. This obstruction only concerns class at most two. It does not rule out amalgams of larger or unbounded class, and it is not a nonexistence proof for 21.39. Expanding the language by a compatible central series is a different construction problem; the resulting bounded-class groups still have the characteristic-center obstruction above.

## Final mathematical boundary

The countable reduction is complete, and all three boundary families have their advertised properties. A solution still requires a single locally finite characteristically simple group with finite omega and nontrivial finite residual, or a theorem proving that no such group exists.

The constructions above do not provide that missing intersection. In particular, no proof here reduces all possible examples to p-groups, promotes finitely many element orbits to omega-categoricity, turns a locally finite group into a pseudofinite one, or deduces a bound on nilpotency class from finite exponent. None of those unproved transitions may be used to declare the original question solved.
