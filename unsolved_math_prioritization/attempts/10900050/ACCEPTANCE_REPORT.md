# Independent AI-assisted audit of the prime difference counterexample

Audit date: 11 October 2026 UTC.

## Decision

**ACCEPTED as a complete elementary counterexample to the equality printed in the original question, with the prime-element convention of its cited primary source.** No mathematical objection remains in this scope.

The witness is \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt {10})\). The full ring of integers is \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt {10}]\); both graph clique numbers are exactly 2; the minimum nonzero prime-ideal norm is 2. The proposed equality would therefore assert \(2=4\), and is false.

## Scope checks

- **Original domain:** Question 7.1, PDF p. 5, requires an integer polynomial irreducible over the rationals whose roots are all real, and uses the full ring of integers of a root field. The polynomial \(X^2-10\) satisfies every requirement.
- **No PID premise:** Question 7.1 asks about the frequency of PIDs. Question 7.2, PDF p. 6, imports the ring setup without asserting class number one. Adding a PID assumption would change the printed problem.
- **Exact target:** Question 7.2 asks whether the product of the unit-graph clique number and the minimum prime-ideal norm always equals the prime-graph clique number. The displayed ideal quantifier and equality question were visually checked.
- **Meaning of prime:** The cited manuscript's Definition (i), PDF p. 1, requires a difference to be a unit or to generate a prime ideal. Section 3, PDF p. 9, explicitly defines prime cliques using only that condition. Irreducibility alone is insufficient.
- **Additional condition excluded:** The unit-group-surjectivity requirement is condition (ii) for Lenstra–Hurwitz cliques. It is not required for the prime graph.
- **Proof conventions:** Section 3's Theorem 3.1 and proof, PDF p. 10, speak of a principal ideal, a generator, and a quotient field. The printed theorem's principal-ideal minimum does not literally match the problem list's prime-ideal minimum. The counterexample neither assumes those quantities equal nor relies on that theorem; it computes the printed question directly.

## Arithmetic and graph checks

1. A direct trace-and-norm integrality argument proves that the ring of integers is exactly \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt {10}]\). It does not merely assume the standard quadratic-ring formula.
2. Reduction of the constant coefficient modulo 2 is a surjective ring map to \(\mathbb F_2\), with kernel \(P=(2,\sqrt {10})\). Thus \(P\) is prime of norm 2.
3. The multiplication matrix of \(a+b\sqrt {10}\) has determinant \(a^2-10b^2\), giving the exact index of every nonzero principal ideal.
4. If \(P\) were principal, an integral solution of \(a^2-10b^2=\pm2\) would exist. The square residues 0, 1 and 4 modulo 5 rule out both signs. This proves nonprincipality without an external class-number table.
5. Every nonzero prime ideal of this ring is maximal because it has a finite integral-domain quotient. The proof supplies the finite-quotient step and the elementary finite-domain-is-a-field argument.
6. A unit cannot lie in \(P\). If a prime element lay in \(P\), its principal prime ideal would be maximal and equal to \(P\), a contradiction.
7. Reduction modulo \(P\) is therefore a proper two-coloring of each graph. The common edge \(\{0,1\}\) proves both clique numbers are 2, not merely bounded by 2.
8. All proper ideal quotients have at least two elements, and \(O/P\) has exactly two; the minimum prime-ideal norm is precisely 2.

The small residue computations were also recomputed directly with integer arithmetic. They are illustrative checks of the written universal congruence argument, not substitutes for it.

## Objections considered

**Could “prime” mean irreducible?** The cited primary definition rules this out. The distinction matters: 2 is irreducible here but not prime; an irreducible-difference graph contains the triangle \(\{0,1,2\}\). No assertion about that graph is made.

**Does a nonprincipal ideal exclude the example?** No. A PID is asked about in the preceding question, not imposed as a hypothesis. A PID-only question would require separate work.

**Could a prime element be contained in a different maximal ideal without generating it?** Not in this ring: the principal ideal generated by a nonzero prime element is itself maximal, and a maximal ideal contained in a proper ideal must equal it.

**Does the theorem/problem wording mismatch prevent an answer?** No. It deserves explicit disclosure, but the strict inequality for the actual printed quantities is proved independently.

**Are prime-ideal or unit classifications missing?** No. The proof needs only one explicitly constructed ideal and the edge \(\{0,1\}\); it does not need a list of units, prime elements, or ideal classes.
