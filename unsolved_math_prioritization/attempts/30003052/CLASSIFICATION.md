# Spectrum of a linear-map Koopman operator on the closed unit ball

**Verified full classification for 30003052 / OWR-14215-004; accepted after independent mathematical and publication-package reviews.** One proof family. Known cases from Kari Küster's 2015 thesis and 2016 OWR contribution are credited below. Historical priority of the completed classification is not established.

## 1. Exact space and statement

Let \(X=(\mathbb C^k,\|\cdot\|)\), \(k\ge1\), with an arbitrary complex norm. Let
\[
 U=\{x:\|x\|\le1\},\qquad A\in\mathbb C^{k\times k},
 \qquad\|A\|\le1.
\]
The space is the complex Banach space \(C(U)\) of all continuous functions, with the supremum norm. Define the bounded Koopman operator
\[
 K_A f=f\circ A.
\]
This is precisely Example4 in **Kari Küster, “Pure Koopmanism,” OWR7/2016, printed pp.320–322**, particularly p.321. No invariant measure or holomorphic-observable restriction is imposed. Invertibility of A is not assumed.

Write
\[
 S=\sigma(A)\cap\mathbb T,\qquad
 J=\{\alpha\in\sigma(A):0<|\alpha|<1\},
\]
and let \(\Gamma=\langle S\rangle\) be the multiplicative subgroup of the unit circle generated by S. By convention \(\langle\varnothing\rangle=\{1\}\). Put
\[
 Z_A=
 \begin{cases}
 \{0\},&0\in\sigma(A),\\
 \varnothing,&0\notin\sigma(A).
 \end{cases}
\]
Here \(\sigma_{\rm p}\) denotes point spectrum, while \(\sigma\) denotes the full Banach-operator spectrum.

**Theorem.**
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{condition}&\sigma_{\rm p}(K_A)&\sigma(K_A)\\ \hline
J\ne\varnothing&\mathbb D^\circ\cup\Gamma&\overline{\mathbb D}\\
J=\varnothing&\Gamma\cup Z_A&\overline\Gamma\cup Z_A .
\end{array}
 \tag{1}
\]
In particular, multiplicities and sizes of Jordan blocks do not change these spectral sets within the stated contraction class, although they may affect other operator properties.

The source already proves the all-peripheral case, the nilpotent-only point spectrum \(\{0,1\}\) (thesis Proposition 3.1.7, printed p.39), the strictly stable case with a nonzero matrix eigenvalue, and the inclusion
\(\mathbb D^\circ\cup\Gamma\subseteq\sigma_{\rm p}(K_A)\) in the mixed case. The proof below supplies the reverse mixed inclusion and treats the remaining nilpotent/peripheral combinations and full spectrum in a single argument.

## 2. Peripheral projection stays inside the given ball

Since \(\|A^n\|\le1\), every matrix eigenvalue has modulus at most one, and each Jordan block at a unit-modulus eigenvalue has size one. Indeed, a larger such block would produce polynomial growth of its powers.

Let
\[
 X=E\oplus F
\]
be the spectral decomposition into the eigenspaces with eigenvalues in S and the generalized eigenspaces with eigenvalues of modulus less than one. Denote its commuting projection onto E by P. Then
\[
 A|_E=B,\qquad A|_F=N,\qquad N^n\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{2}
\]
The last convergence is in operator norm, by the finite-dimensional Jordan formula.

There exists a sequence of positive integers \(n_j\to\infty\) such that all the finitely many unit eigenvalues satisfy
\[
 \lambda^{n_j}\longrightarrow1.
\]
This is simultaneous recurrence of powers in a finite-dimensional torus, obtainable by the pigeonhole principle; in the finite-order case take multiples of a common period. Because B is diagonalizable, it follows that
\[
 A^{n_j}\longrightarrow P.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \|P\|\le1.
 \tag{3}
\]
In particular, P maps U into itself. If \(V=U\cap E\), then \(P(U)=V\), since P is the identity on E.

Similarly, \(B^{n_j-1}\to B^{-1}\), so \(\|B^{-1}\|\le1\) in the restricted norm on E. Thus B is an invertible isometry of E and maps V onto V. If E is zero-dimensional, V is the one-point space \(\{0\}\), and all assertions about its Koopman operator reduce to the identity on constants.

## 3. Every unimodular eigenfunction factors through P

Let \(f\in C(U)\) satisfy
\[
 f(Ax)=\zeta f(x),\qquad |\zeta|=1.
\]
For every \(x\in U\), both \(x\) and \(Px\) belong to U, so
\[
 |f(x)-f(Px)|
 =|f(A^n x)-f(A^n Px)|.
 \tag{4}
\]
Since
\[
 A^n(x-Px)=N^n(I-P)x\longrightarrow0
\]
uniformly for \(x\in U\), uniform continuity of f on the compact ball makes the right side of (4) tend uniformly to zero. Hence
\[
 f=f\circ P.
 \tag{5}
\]
Conversely, a B-eigenfunction on V pulls back along P to an A-eigenfunction on U with the same eigenvalue, and it remains nonzero because P is onto V. Consequently
\[
 \sigma_{\rm p}(K_A)\cap\mathbb T=\sigma_{\rm p}(K_B).
 \tag{6}
\]
This is the step needed to control the mixed case. It uses uniform continuity on the closed ball and does not discard stable directions without justification.

## 4. The reversible ball has exactly the generated group as point spectrum

We prove
\[
 \sigma_{\rm p}(K_B)=\Gamma,\qquad
 \sigma(K_B)=\overline\Gamma.
 \tag{7}
\]

For \(E\ne\{0\}\), choose complex-linear coordinates \(z_1,\ldots,z_r\) on E that diagonalize B:
\[
 z_i(Bx)=\lambda_i z_i(x),\qquad |\lambda_i|=1.
\]
For nonnegative integer multi-indices p and q, the monomial
\[
 z^p\overline z^{\,q}
\]
is a continuous eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\[
 \lambda_1^{p_1-q_1}\cdots\lambda_r^{p_r-q_r}.
\]
Such a monomial is not identically zero on V, because V contains a neighborhood of zero in E. Every member of \(\Gamma\) is therefore an eigenvalue.

The linear span of these monomials is uniformly dense in C(V) by the complex Stone–Weierstrass theorem: it contains constants, is closed under conjugation and separates points.

Suppose \(\zeta\in\mathbb T\setminus\Gamma\) and \(K_B f=\zeta f\). Define
\[
 M_m=\frac1m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{-j}K_B^j.
\]
Since B maps V onto itself, \(K_B\) is an isometry, and \(\|M_m\|\le1\). For every monomial eigenfunction with eigenvalue \(\gamma\in\Gamma\), the geometric Cesàro sum implies \(M_m p\to0\). The same holds for every polynomial in the coordinates and their conjugates. Approximation then gives
\[
 \|f\|=\|M_m f\|\le\|f-p\|+\|M_m p\|,
\]
which forces f to vanish. This proves the first equality in (7). No Lebesgue or invariant-probability model is substituted for C(V).

Both \(K_B\) and \(K_B^{-1}\) have norm one, so \(\sigma(K_B)\subseteq\mathbb T\). It contains the closure of its point spectrum. If \(\Gamma\) is infinite, its closure is the whole circle, since a closed subgroup of the circle is either finite or the circle. This proves (7) in that case.

If \(\Gamma\) is finite of order q, then \(B^q=I\), hence \(K_B^q=I\). Thus the full spectrum is contained in the qth roots of unity, which are exactly \(\Gamma\) and already belong to the point spectrum. The one-point case E=0 has both spectra \(\{1\}\), agreeing with the convention for \(\Gamma\).

Together, (6) and (7) give
\[
 \sigma_{\rm p}(K_A)\cap\mathbb T=\Gamma
 \tag{8}
\]
in every case.

## 5. A nonzero stable matrix eigenvalue yields the whole open disk

Assume \(\alpha\in J\), and put \(r=|\alpha|\in(0,1)\). Choose a nonzero complex-linear functional \(\ell\) with
\[
 \ell(Ax)=\alpha\ell(x).
\]
Such a left eigenfunctional exists even when A has nontrivial Jordan blocks. Set
\[
 R=\max_{x\in U}|\ell(x)|>0.
\]

For any \(0<|\mu|<1\), choose a complex number z with
\[
 r^z=\mu,\qquad
 \Re z=\frac{\log|\mu|}{\log r}>0.
\]
For instance, take \(z=(\log|\mu|+i\theta)/\log r\) with \(e^{i\theta}=\mu/|\mu|\). Define
\[
 f_\mu(x)=
 \begin{cases}
 \exp\!\left(z\log\frac{|\ell(x)|}{R}\right),&\ell(x)\ne0,\\
 0,&\ell(x)=0.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{9}
\]
The logarithm here is the real logarithm of a positive number; no branch of a complex argument of \(\ell(x)\) is used. Because \(\Re z>0\), (9) is continuous across the whole hyperplane \(\ker\ell\). It is nonzero, and
\[
 f_\mu(Ax)=r^z f_\mu(x)=\mu f_\mu(x).
\]
For \(\mu=0\), the continuous nonzero function
\[
 f_0(x)=\max\left\{0,\frac{|\ell(x)|}{R}-r\right\}
 \tag{10}
\]
satisfies \(f_0(Ax)=0\) throughout U.

Thus every point of the open disk is a Koopman eigenvalue. Since \(\|K_A\|=1\), no eigenvalue lies outside the closed disk. Equation (8) now proves the first point-spectrum formula in (1). The full spectrum is closed and contains the open disk, so it is the entire closed disk.

These are the known interior eigenvalues in the source, reconstructed explicitly to make the complete argument independent of a hidden regularity or diagonalizability assumption.

## 6. Only peripheral eigenvalues and zero: the nilpotent remainder

Now suppose \(J=\varnothing\). All eigenvalues of N in (2) are zero, so \(N^d=0\) for some positive integer d if F is nonzero.

Define the bounded projection on C(U)
\[
 Qf=f\circ P.
\]
It commutes with \(K_A\). Its range is isometrically isomorphic to C(V), with \(K_A\) corresponding to \(K_B\). Its kernel consists exactly of the functions vanishing on V.

For \(f\in\ker Q\) and \(x\in U\),
\[
 A^d x=B^d Px\in V,
\]
so
\[
 K_A^d f(x)=f(A^d x)=0.
 \tag{11}
\]
Thus the restriction of \(K_A\) to \(\ker Q\) is nilpotent. When F is nonzero, this kernel is nonzero: a nonzero linear functional vanishing on E supplies an element. A nilpotent operator on a nonzero vector space has zero as an eigenvalue and has no other spectral value. When F=0, this summand is absent.

The two closed invariant summands give
\[
 C(U)=\operatorname{ran}Q\oplus\ker Q.
\]
The spectra of a bounded operator on such a direct sum are the unions of the two restricted spectra, and the same is true for point spectra. Combining (7) and (11) proves the second row of (1), because F is nonzero here exactly when \(0\in\sigma(A)\).

This includes the nilpotent-only case:
\[
 \sigma_{\rm p}(K_A)=\sigma(K_A)=\{0,1\}.
\]
It also distinguishes invertibility of A from injectivity of the composition operator. For example, \(A=\tfrac12 I\) is invertible as a matrix but \(K_A\) has zero as an eigenvalue, as (10) shows. \(\square\)
