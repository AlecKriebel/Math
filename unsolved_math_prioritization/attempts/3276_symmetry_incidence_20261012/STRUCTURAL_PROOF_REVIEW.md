## 1. Balance, reversibility, and the small-palette theorem

Let a finite directed graph have equal indegree and outdegree at every vertex. For an actual arc u to v, take the set R reachable from v, including v. No arc exits R. If u is outside R, at least the chosen arc enters R. Summing outdegree minus indegree over R counts exiting minus entering arcs, and would be negative, contradicting zero. Thus v reaches u. The proof works for each color independently and uses actual arcs, not reflexive diagonal entries.

In a tournament, the actual arc between each pair now certifies mutual reachability in its own color. Therefore every vertex is a universal monochromatic source in every balanced coloring with any finite palette. This does not imply one color spans. The nine-vertex balanced counterfixture has two unequal SCCs of sizes four and five in each of its three colors. Every vertex remains a source, while no color spans.

For a color-preserving transitive group, each color's outgoing degree is constant and its incoming degree is constant. The two total degree sums count the same arcs, so the constants agree. No assumption on stabilizers or regularity of the action is needed. The uncolored outgoing degree is also constant; n times that integer equals n(n-1)/2. Hence n is odd when n>1.

An automorphism sends an entire monochromatic SCC onto a monochromatic SCC of the same color. Transitivity on vertices therefore makes every SCC of a fixed color have one common size s_c. Its number q_c is an integer divisor of n. If that color does not span and p is the least prime divisor of n, q_c >= p, and s_c <= n/p.

Fix x and retain k nonempty color labels. By reversibility, every vertex y belongs to the SCC E_c(x) for the color of the arc joining x and y; x belongs to every such set. If all colors fail to span, inclusion counting gives

    n <= |union_c E_c(x)| <= sum_c |E_c(x)| - (k-1)
      <= k n/p - (k-1).

The first inequality is equality because the sets cover V; the displayed orientation is harmless. If 2<=k<=p, the right side is at most n-(k-1)<n. For k=1 it is n/p<n. This is a contradiction. For at most three colors, p>=3 applies. Missing colors can be dropped or kept as singleton SCC partitions; they create no gap. The stronger assertion with at most p colors is accepted as stated.

This is a strongly connected monochromatic spanning subdigraph, not a claim of a monochromatic Hamilton cycle. In the algorithm, using only positive powers of the supplied permutation generators is sufficient for the orbit check: each inverse is a positive power of its finite-order generator. An orbit of all vertices therefore verifies transitivity of the generated group.

## 2. Classical attribution and sharpness

An explicit search for a standard name found the underlying classical group-cover theorem, rather than a reliably established dedicated name for this exact tournament statement. Scorza's characterization is stated as Theorem 1(ii) in Mira Bhargava, *Finiteness criteria for coverings of groups by finitely many subgroups or cosets*, International Electronic Journal of Algebra 2 (2007), 83–89: https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/232921 . The journal PDF's extracted first page was inspected. Its characterization says that a three-proper-subgroup cover exists exactly when there is a Klein-four quotient. The same page rules out a cover by fewer than three proper subgroups.

Here monochromatic SCCs are blocks for the action. The setwise stabilizer H_c of E_c(x) consists precisely of elements g with g(x) in E_c(x). Indeed, g sends that SCC to the SCC containing g(x), so an intersection forces equality. Reversibility implies G is covered by these H_c. If all are proper, Scorza contradicts the odd order of G: a tournament automorphism cannot be a nonidentity involution, since one swapped vertex pair would have its arc reversed; Cauchy's theorem then implies the finite automorphism group has odd order. This independently explains the three-color conclusion. The author's elementary component count is valid without importing this result and has the n-based least-prime extension directly. No novelty, priority, or exhaustive literature-status claim is accepted or made.

For every odd prime p, the affine-line construction on F_p^2 is a tournament: each nonzero difference belongs to exactly one one-dimensional subspace and precisely one of each pair of opposite nonzero scalars is chosen. Translation preserves orientation and line color. Each monochromatic digraph has exactly p components, the affine lines parallel to its color; the selected unit step gives a directed p-cycle within each. Thus p+1 colors need not have a spanning color. The least prime divisor of the order p^2 is indeed p. This verifies sharpness in general, not a claim of sharpness at each individual odd order.

The balanced nine-vertex example is also valid. Its three undirected color graphs are even-degree and each has two connected components of orders five and four. Orienting an Euler traversal of every component balances each color and makes each component strongly connected. The frozen explicit matrix is independently checked, rather than relying on that verbal construction alone. This is not asserted to be cyclic-rainbow-free.

## 3. Colored-prime full-core examples and precise separation

Suppose the actual arcs of a color are exactly one directed Hamilton cycle. If a proper nontrivial colored module M contains a vertex whose successor y is outside M, every vertex of M must send an arc of that color to y. This contradicts y's color-indegree one. Thus M is closed under the successor permutation. A nonempty successor-closed subset of a spanning cycle is all vertices, another contradiction. The proof uses exactness of the color class, not mere containment of a Hamilton cycle. Both extra same-color arcs and a color consisting of several disjoint cycles are tested as rejection controls.

For odd n>=7, orient Z/nZ by positive offsets 1 through (n-1)/2, with offsets 1 and 2 in colors zero and one, and all larger offsets in color two. This is a tournament and its translation group preserves all colors. The zero-color class is exactly a spanning directed cycle, proving both colored primeness and one-color reachability.

In an actual directed cyclic triangle, the three directed positive offsets sum to a positive multiple of n. If the triangle were rainbow its offsets would be 1,2,d with 3<=d<=(n-1)/2. Their sum is at most (n+5)/2<n when n>=7, impossible. This does not forbid transitive rainbow triples. Offsets 1,2,3 give every vertex all three colors in both directions, so the whole incidence core survives without a first deletion.

For a fixed finite bound b choose odd n>max(b,6). Every factor block in any uniform colored-substitution representation is a colored module. Since this T_n is colored-prime, a root with at least two factors can have only singleton factors; its quotient is T_n itself, with core n>b. The prior certificate allows no one-child internal node, so a trivial unary wrapper cannot evade that failure. Thus these are genuinely outside the prior bounded recursive quotient-core certificate class. ## 4. Global color permutations and the divisibility condition

The external obstruction-cycle premise is Shen's result as credited and reproved in Lemma 2.1 of Georgakopoulos and Sprüssel, *On 3-coloured tournaments*, arXiv:0904.1967v2: https://arxiv.org/pdf/0904.1967v2 . The recovered PDF and full extracted text were hash-checked, and the lemma's statement, proof, and surrounding general-minimal-counterexample hypotheses were read. The source gives uniqueness of the cycle with the failed-predecessor property, not uniqueness among all Hamilton cycles. Its quoted theorem is used as a dependency, not misidentified as an original result of this packet.

Let pred(v) be the unique distinct vertex unreachable monochromatically from v. A direction-preserving automorphism with one global color permutation preserves monochromatic reachability and its failure. Thus f(pred(v))=pred(f(v)). The centralizer of a full n-cycle in the symmetric group is its cyclic rotation group: specifying f at one vertex specifies it at each successive predecessor. Hence every such symmetry is a rotation. A subgroup of this rotation group has each orbit of size its own order. If it is transitive on n vertices, its order is n and it is the whole rotation group. In particular the single-step shift belongs to the group; it is not necessary to guess that an initially supplied generator is that shift.

For completeness, the color action is a genuine homomorphism on the colors actually used. For each used color choose an actual arc of that color: its image uniquely determines that color's image, and composition of vertex maps composes these images. Unused colors do not affect this homomorphism. Since the one-step shift g has order n, its induced permutation sigma on used colors has order dividing n. An even-order rotation has an involutive power swapping a pair of vertices and so is impossible; n is odd. A permutation of at most three used colors with odd order is identity or a three-cycle. If identity, the distinguished cycle's successive arc colors are all equal and following it supplies a forbidden path to each predecessor. Therefore sigma has order three. This forces all three colors to occur and forces strict period three on that distinguished cycle, with 3 dividing n. The source's additional incidence theorem is unnecessary for this deduction.

The kernel on the full rotation group is exactly <g^3>, has order n/3, and has the three residue classes modulo three as its vertex orbits. In particular the kernel is not transitive; applying the color-preserving transitivity theorem to that kernel on the whole tournament would be invalid. No such step occurs in the accepted proof.

Write n=3m and let h=g^m. It has order three and every orbit on vertices has three distinct members. The induced tournament on an orbit is invariant under a 3-cycle, so all vertices have one outneighbor there and the orbit is an actual directed cyclic triangle, in one of its two orientations. If 3 does not divide m, sigma^m is a 3-cycle; the three actual arcs of that triangle are successive images of one arc and have three distinct colors. This contradicts the rainbow-free premise. Therefore 9 divides n.

When 9 divides n, sigma^m is identity and these specific triangles are monochromatic. The argument supplies no further factor of three. The separate accepted general theorem through nine excludes n=9; odd multiples of nine then begin at 27. The report establishing through-nine has hash `[verified undistributed artifact identity]`. It was inspected at its accepted scope; this audit does not rerun that enumeration and uses no provisional larger bound.

A strict periodic cycle word does not force the full tournament to admit the color-twisted shift. An independent nine-vertex fixture changes one noncycle chord color while preserving the cycle word, and rejects every possible global color permutation for the shift. Conversely, a rainbow three-cycle does admit a transitive color-twisted rotation but has no universal monochromatic source. These controls rule out both tempting overextensions. The accepted divisibility theorem concerns the fully color-permuting-transitive minimal-counterexample subclass only; arbitrary three-periodic obstruction cycles remain outside it.
