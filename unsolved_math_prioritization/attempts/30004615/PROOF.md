## Result and limits

The general descent question in Wang's Conjecture 5.2 remains undecided by this note. We prove its normal-subgroup case, including the passage from a reflection on the type-IV domain to a pseudoreflection on suitable homogeneous algebra generators. We also give:

1. a finite reflection-group example showing that a polynomial invariant ring for a nonnormal subgroup does **not** imply polynomiality for its normal core; and
2. a source-dependent arithmetic example showing that replacing actual linear reflections by projective reflections makes the proposed conclusion false.

The first theorem is a consequence of classical invariant theory and the standard affine-cone realization of modular varieties. No novelty claim is made. The general nonnormal case is neither proved nor disproved. None of the arguments assumes that the lattice splits two hyperbolic planes or that the groups contain its discriminant kernel.

## 1. Exact conventions and dependencies

Let M be an even integral lattice of signature (n,2), where n >= 3. Choose one component D of its type-IV domain, and let L be the corresponding affine null cone with its vertex removed. Thus L has complex dimension d = n+1. We write O+(M) for the component-preserving integral orthogonal group.

For an arithmetic subgroup H, put

\[
A_H=M_*(H)=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}M_k(H).
\]

Here every weight k is an integer, the character is trivial, and a form is a holomorphic function on L satisfying

\[
F(tZ)=t^{-k}F(Z),\qquad F(hZ)=F(Z).
\]

These are Wang's Definition 2.1, with the quadratic form's sign reversed. In this dimension Koecher's principle supplies the boundary holomorphy. A_0 is C, the weight grading is nonnegative, and positive-weight generators have strictly positive degrees.

An actual reflection is

\[
\sigma_r(x)=x-\frac{2(r,x)}{(r,r)}r,
\]

with a mirror meeting D. This is precisely Wang's equation (2.8), §2.4, PDF page 6. With our sign convention r has positive norm. The element sigma_r fixes L intersect r-perp pointwise. Its negative generally does not: on that cone, -sigma_r sends Z to -Z. We never identify those two cone actions.

We use the following named dependencies.

**D1: The affine-cone realization.** For an arithmetic H, the evaluation map identifies L/H with the corresponding open part of the affine modular-form spectrum. In the polynomial case A_H = C[f_1,...,f_d], its explicit map is

\[
q_H:L\longrightarrow L/H\hookrightarrow
\operatorname{Spec}A_H\setminus\{0\}=\mathbb A^d\setminus\{0\},
\quad Z\longmapsto(f_1(Z),...,f_d(Z)).
\]

The quotient map is locally a quotient by a finite stabilizer. This is the precise geometric statement invoked in Wang's proof of Theorem 3.5(2), PDF page 10, from Baily--Borel theory. We use the affine statement, not merely the identification of Proj with a weighted projective space. In particular, the image under q_H of a local hypersurface in L has dimension d-1. We do not reprove the Baily--Borel theorem.

**D2: Chevalley--Shephard--Todd, characteristic zero.** If a finite subgroup of GL(V) is generated by pseudoreflections, then C[V] invariant under that group is a polynomial algebra. A pseudoreflection is a nonidentity linear transformation whose fixed subspace has codimension one. This classical theorem is explicitly stated and referenced in the introduction of Wang's paper; the original references are listed below. We use its sufficient direction.

**D3: Reflection generation for a free modular-form algebra.** If A_H is polynomial, H is generated by actual reflections. This is Wang's Proposition 3.3. It is used only for the optional normalizer-iteration corollary in §4, not in the main theorem.

The following proofs do not use the classification of 26 groups in Wang's Theorem 4.4, the two-hyperbolic-plane hypothesis, or the classification of simple lattices.

## 2. Two elementary graded-algebra lemmas

### Lemma 2.1: equivariant homogeneous generators

Let A be a polynomial C-algebra with a connected positive grading and finite-dimensional graded pieces. Let a finite group G act on A by grading-preserving algebra automorphisms. Then A has a homogeneous system of polynomial generators whose C-linear span is G-stable.

**Proof.** Let m be the ideal of positive-degree elements. The graded space V = m/m^2 is finite dimensional. In each degree appearing in V choose a linear section of m -> V. Average the sections over G. This produces a homogeneous G-equivariant section s:V -> m. Put W=s(V).

We claim W generates A. Induct on the positive degree k. For a homogeneous a in A_k, subtract a linear combination of the degree-k elements of W having the same image in V. The difference lies in (m^2)_k. Every homogeneous product appearing there has factors of positive degree strictly below k, hence lies in the algebra generated by W by induction. This proves the claim.

Write d=dim A. Because A is a polynomial ring and m is a maximal ideal with residue field C, dim_C m/m^2=d. Consequently Sym(W) -> A is a surjection between domains of the same dimension d. Its prime kernel has height zero and must be zero, since Sym(W) is a polynomial domain. Hence it is an isomorphism. It is graded and G-equivariant by construction. In these generators the action is linear. This proof also shows that its kernel on W is exactly its kernel on A. □

### Lemma 2.2: regularity gives generators of the original weights

Let B be a finitely generated connected positively graded C-domain. If the local ring at the homogeneous maximal ideal n=B_{>0} is regular of dimension d, then B is a polynomial algebra on d homogeneous generators.

**Proof.** Choose homogeneous lifts of a basis of n/n^2, which has dimension d by regularity. The same induction on positive degree used above shows that these lifts generate B. The resulting surjection from a polynomial algebra on d variables has prime kernel of height zero and is therefore injective. □

This lemma prevents a grading gap: the ordinary polynomial algebra supplied by D2 also has free generators homogeneous in the **modular weight** grading, even when the original generators have different weights.

## 3. Normal descent theorem

### Theorem 3.1

Suppose Gamma is a finite-index subgroup of O+(M), generated by actual reflections. Let H be a finite-index **normal** subgroup of Gamma. If A_H is polynomial, then A_Gamma is polynomial on n+1 homogeneous generators of positive integral weights.

**Proof.** Set A=A_H and d=n+1. Normality makes

\[
(g\cdot F)(Z)=F(g^{-1}Z)
\]

a grading-preserving action of G=Gamma/H on A. Indeed, for h in H the point g^{-1}hZ equals (g^{-1}hg)g^{-1}Z with g^{-1}hg in H. Conversely, invariance under all g in Gamma is exactly the condition of being a trivial-character form for Gamma. Thus

\[
A^G=A_\Gamma.
\]

There is no change of weights or characters in this equality.

By Lemma 2.1 choose a G-stable homogeneous generator space W, so that A=Sym(W). Set X=Spec A=W^*. If some elements of G act trivially, replace G by its effective image G_eff in GL(W). This has no effect on the invariant algebra. In particular, we do not silently discard a central -Id unless it actually lies in the action's kernel.

Take any actual reflection sigma_r in Gamma. It fixes the nonempty smooth hypersurface

\[
E_r=L\cap r^\perp
\]

pointwise. This hypersurface has dimension d-1. The evaluation map q_H is equivariant for the G-action on X. Therefore the image q_H(E_r) lies in the fixed-point locus of the image of sigma_r on X.

We justify the dimension used here. Choose a sufficiently small neighborhood of a point of E_r on which the H quotient is a finite-stabilizer quotient. A finite holomorphic quotient preserves the dimension of the hypersurface germ. By D1 its image embeds as an analytic germ in X. Hence the fixed-point locus in X contains an analytic germ of dimension d-1. Since the action on X=W^* is linear, that fixed-point locus is a linear subspace. It has dimension at least d-1.

If the induced action of sigma_r is nonidentity, its fixed subspace is proper and so has dimension exactly d-1. It is a pseudoreflection. If its action is identity, it contributes nothing to G_eff. Because Gamma is generated by its actual reflections, G_eff is generated by the nonidentity pseudoreflections obtained this way.

D2 now implies A^G is a polynomial C-algebra. It is finitely generated, connected, and positively weight-graded. Its homogeneous maximal ideal is a smooth point of this polynomial affine variety, so Lemma 2.2 gives polynomial generators homogeneous in the original modular weights. Its dimension is d because A is integral over A^G for a finite group action: for a in A, the polynomial product over g in G of (T-g(a)) has invariant coefficients and annihilates a. This proves the result. □

**Precisely where normality was used.** Without H normal in Gamma, precomposition by g generally takes an H-form to a gHg^{-1}-form, rather than an H-form. There is then no Gamma/H group acting on A_H. The first line of the proof, not merely its last application of D2, ceases to be available.

**Precisely where cone geometry was used.** A fixed divisor in Proj A is not sufficient. We proved that an actual reflection fixes an (n-dimensional) germ in Spec A, using its pointwise-fixed cone hypersurface and D1. This supplies the missing pseudoreflection condition after graded linearization.

## 4. A restricted extension procedure

Suppose there is a finite chain

\[
H=H_0\triangleleft H_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft H_t=\Gamma
\]

such that every H_i for i>=1 is generated by actual reflections. If A_H is polynomial, repeated application of Theorem 3.1 proves that A_Gamma is polynomial. Merely assuming H is subnormal does not supply the reflection-generation hypothesis for every intermediate group.

One can form a particular chain without making unproved assumptions. Given a finite-index H_i with polynomial algebra, let

\[
H_{i+1}=\langle \sigma\in\Gamma:
\sigma\text{ is an actual reflection and }
\sigma H_i\sigma^{-1}=H_i\rangle.
\]

D3 ensures H_i is itself reflection-generated, so H_i is contained in H_{i+1}. By construction H_i is normal in H_{i+1}, and H_{i+1} is reflection-generated. Theorem 3.1 applies. Each strict inclusion decreases the finite integer [Gamma:H_i], so this procedure stabilizes. If it reaches Gamma, descent follows. Nothing here proves that it must reach Gamma; the next example shows why a general group-theoretic claim to that effect would be false.

## 5. A polynomial subgroup can have a nonpolynomial normal core

This example is a finite linear invariant-theory example, **not** an orthogonal modular counterexample to the conjecture.

Let P=C[x,y] and let the linear substitutions r,s be given on the column (x,y) by

\[
r=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
s=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

They satisfy r^6=s^2=1, srs=r^{-1}, and r^3=-I. The group G=<r,s> has order 12. It is generated by the reflections s and rs, and preserves the positive-definite integral form with Gram matrix

\[
Q=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Put H=<r^3,s>. It has order 4 and index 3 and is generated by s and -s, both reflections. In the coordinates u=x+y and v=x-y, these two reflections independently change the signs of v and u. Hence

\[
P^H=\mathbb C[u^2,v^2]
\]

is polynomial.

On the other hand,

\[
H=\{I,-I,s,-s\},\qquad
rHr^{-1}=\{I,-I,r^2s,r^5s\}.
\]

Their intersection is K={I,-I}. Because K is central, it equals the full normal core intersection over g in G of gHg^{-1}. It is not generated by pseudoreflections: its only nonidentity element fixes just the origin in C^2.

Its invariant ring has the presentation

\[
P^K=\mathbb C[x^2,xy,y^2]
\cong\mathbb C[A,B,C]/(AC-B^2).
\]

For completeness, invariance under -I means that every monomial has even total degree. Its two exponents consequently have the same parity, so it is uniquely of the form (x^2)^a(y^2)^c or (x^2)^a(xy)(y^2)^c. Modulo AC-B^2 every polynomial in A,B,C has a unique expression involving powers of B at most one; the displayed monomials have distinct images. This proves both generation and the asserted kernel.

This ring has dimension 2, but at its origin the maximal ideal has three linearly independent classes A,B,C modulo its square. The defining relation has no linear term. Thus its local ring is singular, and the ring cannot be polynomial. Adding any number d-2 of coordinates fixed by G gives the same obstruction in dimension d, in particular d=n+1>=4: the dimension becomes d and the cotangent dimension at the origin becomes d+1.

The normalizer is also N_G(H)=H. For a rotation r^a, conjugating s gives r^{2a}s, which lies in H exactly when 2a is 0 modulo 6, since 2a cannot be 3 modulo 6. Thus a is 0 or 3. An element r^a s has the same normalizing criterion, because s belongs to H. These give precisely H. Accordingly the reflection-normalizer iteration of §4 would stop immediately in this finite analogue.

There is no contradiction with descent here: G is itself a finite reflection group, so P^G is polynomial by D2. The obstruction is to the inference

\[
P^H\text{ polynomial}\ \Longrightarrow\ P^{\operatorname{core}_G(H)}\text{ polynomial}.
\]

The analogous inference for modular-form rings would require a genuinely arithmetic/geometric argument. It cannot be treated as a general property of invariant rings or reflection subgroups.

## 6. Why the projective-reflection variant is false

Here is a more concrete arithmetic warning, dependent on the stated theorems in Wang--Williams [WW]. We use their lattice L_0=U direct-sum U(3) direct-sum A_1, of signature (3,2), and their group R=O_r(L_0), generated by all actual reflections in O+(L_0).

[WW, Theorem 5.2] identifies the discriminant kernel with its reflection subgroup, so R has finite index in O+(L_0). [WW, Theorem 5.4, PDF page 26] supplies

\[
A_R=\mathbb C[f_1,f_3,f_4,g_6],
\]

where subscripts are weights. In the source, g_6 is denoted psi squared. This note does not recheck the Fourier-expansion computations underlying those theorems.

Let R^+=<R,-Id>. The nonzero odd-weight form f_1 shows that -Id is not in R. Since -Id is central, R is a normal subgroup of index two in R^+, and R^+ is still arithmetic and finite-index in O+(L_0). On the above polynomial algebra -Id acts by

\[
(f_1,f_3,f_4,g_6)\longmapsto(-f_1,-f_3,f_4,g_6).
\]

Therefore

\[
A_{R^+}=\mathbb C[f_1^2,f_1f_3,f_3^2,f_4,g_6]
\cong\mathbb C[A,B,C,D,E]/(AC-B^2).
\]

The weights of A,B,C,D,E are 2,4,6,4,6; the relation has weight 8. The ring has dimension 4 and cotangent dimension 5 at the origin, so it is not polynomial. This description is also consistent with [WW, Corollary 5.5 and its proof, PDF pages 26--27]; the argument here only needs the subgroup R^+, and does not assume it equals the full orthogonal group.

Nevertheless R^+ and R have exactly the same action image on the projective domain D, because -Id acts trivially there. That common projective image is generated by the images of reflections. Thus the version of descent asking only for projective reflection generation is false, even for a normal subgroup with a free algebra.

This is not a counterexample to Wang's actual conjecture: every actual reflection in R^+ already belongs to R, by the definition of R as the subgroup generated by **all** actual reflections in O+(L_0). The subgroup they generate is therefore R, strictly smaller than R^+. In particular, R^+ is not generated by actual reflections. The example also makes explicit why discarding odd weights or quotienting out the central -Id can alter freeness.

## 7. What is still missing

For an arbitrary finite-index, nonnormal H in Gamma, the polynomial ring A_H has no generally available action by Gamma. Passing to K=core_Gamma(H) does produce a finite action on A_K, with

\[
A_H=A_K^{H/K},\qquad A_\Gamma=A_K^{\Gamma/K}.
\]

But A_K is not known to be polynomial from the given hypotheses. Section 5 shows that this implication fails even for finite real reflection groups in ordinary invariant theory. The normal theorem therefore does not close the nonnormal case. Section 4 provides a usable sufficient condition, but not a universally terminating route to Gamma.

Any full solution along this line must supply additional arithmetic geometry that either controls this nonpolynomial normal-core algebra, bypasses it, or proves that a suitable reflection-normal enlargement reaches Gamma. No such argument is claimed here.
