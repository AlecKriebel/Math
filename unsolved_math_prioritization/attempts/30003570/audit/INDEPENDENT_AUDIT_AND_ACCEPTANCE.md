## Source question and ambient cone

The original contribution is Möller’s joint work with Bruinier on pp. 2439–2440 of Oberwolfach Report 39/2017. It asks whether the cone generated by primitive Heegner divisors is all of the K3 pseudo-effective cone or a proper subcone. Its broad use of “polarized” must be interpreted using the underlying modular quotient: Bruinier–Möller’s paper and the subsequent sources explicitly use primitively quasi-polarized K3 surfaces. The polarization is big and nef; restricting to the ample-polarization locus changes the problem.

The accepted setting is the full arithmetic quotient for the lattice

\[
U^{\oplus2}\oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus2}\oplus\langle-2d\rangle,
\]

with divisor classes in its finite-dimensional real Picard space modulo linear equivalence. The pseudo-effective cone is the closure of the cone of effective real divisor classes in that space. This matches the explicit conventions of the later sources. It is not automatically the numerical divisor cone of an arbitrary compactification. The original report’s opening reference to projective varieties, and the 2019 article’s introductory discussion of Baily–Borel compactifications, do not authorize that replacement.

Primitive-Heegner representatives may have positive multiplicities. These do not change rays, but replacing the reduced unigonal class by twice that class would change a slope coordinate. The accepted degree-two statement uses the reduced unigonal divisor throughout.

## Hodge class and the twisting argument

The Hodge class in the accepted slope and twisting arguments is the positive automorphic convention used by the later papers: a positive multiple extends as an ample class on the Baily–Borel model. Its pullback to a resolution or toroidal model is nef and can have degree zero on exceptional boundary curves. It must not be silently treated as ample on every such model.

There is a convention hazard in the 2019 article: its p. 504 labels its tautological convention anti-ample, while the report also cites later papers that explicitly place the positive Hodge ray in the NL-cone interior. Read the report’s λ consistently in the latter convention, also fixed by the cited degree-two slope definition. No source-independent conclusion is being drawn from the word “Hodge” alone. The report’s mathematics is correct with this convention; the audit records this clarification without altering its frozen bytes.

Lemma 1 is correct. Each nonzero defining functional is strictly positive on an interior point: if it vanished there, an arbitrarily small displacement with negative functional value would remain in the cone, a contradiction. Solving each inequality for the nonnegative twisting parameter yields exactly the displayed maximum. The quadrant example correctly shows why eventual membership after adding a positive interior vector says nothing about the original vector’s membership. No subtraction of an effective class from an effective class is treated as positivity preserving.

## Extremal rays and separating functionals

The three-dimensional counterexample is valid. The added vector is outside the first octant, and the sum-of-coordinates functional is positive on every generator. The three advertised supporting functionals vanish on exactly the respective original generating rays and are positive on the others. Thus strict enlargement can preserve all three old extremal rays. This refutes the proposed general inference, without making any assertion about the actual K3 cone.

The rank-two exception is also valid. A full-dimensional closed pointed planar cone has a compact interval as its normalized section by a strictly positive functional. Its two endpoints represent its two extremal rays. If both boundary rays of the smaller cone are those endpoints, the cones coincide. Pointedness and closedness are essential to that formulation.

Lemma 3 correctly handles both closure and prime components. A point outside a closed polyhedral cone violates one finite supporting inequality strictly. A sufficiently close finite effective sum still violates it, and at least one of the finitely many prime components in that sum must violate it. Conversely, nonnegativity on every prime extends to every finite effective sum and then to the closure by continuity. There is no assumption of a global finite list of prime divisors, and no passage to an infinite divisor sum. A pseudo-effective limiting class outside the NL cone therefore cannot be the only available kind of witness: an actual prime effective divisor outside it must exist.

## Compactifications and curve certificates

The restriction calculation is valid under the report’s stated normality, Q-factoriality and finite-dimensionality assumptions. On both varieties, rational Cartier classes identify with rational Weil divisor classes. Closing a divisor extends it, and the localization kernel is generated by the finitely many divisorial boundary components. Passing to real coefficients preserves the exact sequence. The calculation does not require the boundary classes to be independent.

The formula for the pseudo-effective cone correctly retains the outer closure of the image. Continuous linear maps need not take arbitrary closed cones to closed sets. Effective divisors lift by closure and restrict effectively, which proves the effective-cone image equality; continuity then gives precisely the stated closed-image formula, with no illicit limit lifting.

For a normal model whose boundary has codimension at least two, the automatic identification is of Weil class groups. It is not an assertion that all those classes are Cartier, or that they equal numerical classes. Thus the report correctly avoids using the Baily–Borel boundary’s small dimension as a replacement for Q-factoriality or a Picard-to-numerical comparison theorem.

Lemma 4 is a sound sufficient criterion. Its factorization hypothesis requires the numerical curve pairing to induce the selected functional on the Picard restriction quotient. Boundary annihilation makes lift changes harmless; alone it does not prove that a prescribed Picard functional is representable by a curve class. This stronger requirement is explicitly present.

For a prime other than the designated divisor, density of the covering family produces a complete curve not contained in its closure. A positive Cartier multiple of that effective closure restricts to a nonzero section on the normalization of the curve, giving nonnegative degree. For the designated prime itself, nonnegativity is supplied by the separate assumption that the functional is nonnegative on the designated cone. The proof therefore does not infer a false nonnegative self-intersection merely from a moving-curve family. Finite sums and subsequent closure are handled by Lemma 3. No claim is made that witnesses satisfying these hypotheses have actually been constructed for all supporting functionals.

## Degree-two slope equivalence

The author uses three compatible inputs: the two-dimensional Picard space and two NL rays, closed-cone extremality of the nonzero reduced unigonal ray, and the expression `p = Aλ − Bu` with positive coefficients and `A/B = 150/57`. The nodal representative may be positively rescaled without changing the ratio.

There is also an orientation check behind the phrase “extremal ray.” Choose an ample Cartier multiple of the positive Hodge class on the Baily–Borel model. Its degree against the closure of a prime divisor is positive. Because that boundary has codimension at least two, closure identifies Weil classes modulo rational equivalence, so this degree defines a linear functional on the open Picard space. It is nonnegative on the effective cone and, by continuity, on its closure; it is strictly positive on the unigonal class. Hence the negative unigonal ray cannot lie in the pseudo-effective cone. This rules out a hidden sign ambiguity if a source phrases extremality merely as proportionality of summands. It uses ample intersection on the Baily–Borel model and Weil cycles, not an unjustified identification with that model’s Cartier Picard group, or ampleness of the Hodge pullback on a resolution.

Here are the potentially delicate steps, independently checked:

1. A nonzero extremal ray, in the face sense, cannot coexist with nonzero lineality of the ambient cone: every face contains the lineality space. Hence the closed two-dimensional cone is pointed. The unigonal ray is a boundary ray with a supporting line.
2. In the basis `(λ,u)` that supporting line is `a = 0` for a class `aλ − bu`. Since the cone contains λ on the positive side, all its classes have `a ≥ 0`, and the intersection with that line is precisely the nonnegative unigonal ray.
3. Rewriting in the basis `(p,u)` gives coefficients `a/A` and `aB/A − b`. For `a > 0` and `b ≤ 0` both are nonnegative. For `a,b > 0`, membership is equivalent to `a/b ≥ A/B`. Classes with `a = 0` have already been accounted for.
4. The effective nodal representative attains the comparison slope, so the infimum is at most `150/57`. Equality of the infimum with that value forces every effective class into the NL cone; its closedness then includes all pseudo-effective limits. Conversely, equality of the cones imposes the same slope bound.
5. Under strict inclusion, Lemma 3 supplies a prime outside the NL cone. The coordinate test forces this prime to have positive `a,b` and slope strictly below `150/57`. This also eliminates any ambiguity between taking the infimum over integral, rational, or finite real effective divisors for the endpoint test: a violating class yields a prime witness. An unattained infimum is not a loophole.

These checks establish the conditional equivalence and prime-witness assertion exactly as claimed. They neither evaluate the infimum nor produce the missing prime divisor. The published/preprint interval is much weaker than the endpoint equality required to finish the original problem.
