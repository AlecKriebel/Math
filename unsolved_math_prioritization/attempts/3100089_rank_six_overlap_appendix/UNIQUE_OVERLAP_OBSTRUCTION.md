# A four-word obstruction to iterating unique-overlap moves

## Result and boundary

This is a separate continuation after the accepted short-neighbor theorem. The accepted first-round proof and its evidence are unchanged.

The eight-vertex matrix below has exactly four successful words. The graph generated by **all** contiguous substitutions whose source interval has uniquely pressable one-end root truncations consists of two disjoint edges. This remains true even if such substitutions have no edit-budget restriction.

Its actual four-deletion/four-insertion graph is a connected four-cycle. Thus the example refutes the proposed proof strategy of obtaining general connectivity merely by iterating the unique-overlap substitutions used in the short-neighbor proof. It does **not** refute the Bixby–Flint–Miklós conjecture, or classify all four-word instances.

No novelty or vertex-minimality claim is made. The discovery inspected an already completely verified two-vertex extension census; the complete certificate here is independently recomputed and does not depend on the census.

## 1. Precisely defined restricted moves

For a nonsingular symmetric binary matrix A, let S be a successful word and U its unit-upper-triangular instructional Cholesky root in S's order. Choose a consecutive interval I of word positions. A **unique-overlap substitution** may replace the letters in I by another successful order for

B=U[I,I]^T U[I,I]

if both one-end truncations of U[I,I] have uniquely pressable Gram matrices. The ordered letters outside I stay fixed. Join two words if such a substitution is possible from either of them.

This definition allows every successful replacement on an eligible interval, not just the particular explicit replacement constructed in the accepted theorem. It also imposes no bound on the interval length or on the LCS deficit. Therefore a disconnected graph for this enlarged restricted-move system also obstructs any strategy that merely repeats the accepted theorem's explicit minimal-interval moves.

The complete pressing-word graph instead joins any two words with LCS at least n-4, with no unique-overlap requirement. These two graphs must not be conflated.

## 3. The actual conjectured graph is a four-cycle

Put

P=(1,4), R=(2,3),
Q_0=(0,5,6,7), Q_1=(0,6,5,7).

Then S_i=P Q_i R and T_i=R Q_i P. The order of any two distinct blocks P,Q,R is reversed between an S-word and a T-word. A common subsequence across the two sides must therefore lie entirely in a single block.

The two Q-words have LCS three; each has length four. Hence

LCS(S_0,T_0)=LCS(S_1,T_1)=4,
LCS(S_0,T_1)=LCS(S_1,T_0)=3.

Within a side, swapping adjacent letters 5 and 6 gives

LCS(S_0,S_1)=LCS(T_0,T_1)=7.

The original threshold is 8-4=4. Its four edges are therefore

S_0--S_1, S_0--T_0, T_0--T_1, T_1--S_1.

The diagonal cross-pairs are nonedges. In particular, this four-cycle is connected. At deficit three the two sides would be disconnected, so the example also retains sharpness of the four-deletion budget.

## 4. The unique-overlap graph has exactly two components

A substitution from an S-word to a T-word changes both the first and last letters. Since letters outside the chosen contiguous interval must remain fixed, the interval would have to be the entire eight-letter word.

That whole interval is ineligible for **every** word in (1). The Gram matrix of its root after deleting the first root row and column is the residual after the first press. Its successful words correspond bijectively to full successful words beginning with that first letter. There are exactly two such words in (1).

Similarly, the Gram matrix of the leading seven-by-seven root block is the principal restriction obtained by deleting the current last vertex. Every successful word on that nonsingular restriction extends by the last vertex to a full successful word: every earlier principal pivot is one, and the final pivot is det(A)/det(A[restriction])=1. Conversely any full word ending at that vertex restricts to such a word. There are exactly two of those words in (1).

Thus both one-end root truncations have **two**, rather than one, successful words. No whole-word unique-overlap substitution is allowed, and no shorter interval could cross the two sides. This argument excludes every allowed interval, not just the intervals visited by one algorithm.

Within each side, the swap of 5 and 6 is allowed. After prefix (1,4,0), the principal matrix on {5,6} is the identity I_2. Its two single-coordinate truncations each have exactly one successful word. The interval (5,6) may therefore be reversed. The automorphism (1 2)(3 4) gives the same move on the other side.

Consequently the unique-overlap graph is exactly

{S_0,S_1} disjoint union {T_0,T_1},

with one edge inside each pair and none between the pairs.

## 5. Consequence for the next proof step

The accepted short-neighbor theorem detects and leaves no singleton component. Repeating its minimal unique-truncation exchanges need not grow a two-word component at all. In this example it can only alternate between the same two words, even though an actual allowed four-deletion edge exits that pair.

The missing extension must therefore handle a root whose two one-end truncations are already nonunique. A proposed induction cannot replace 'connected' by 'unique' in those truncations, or assume that arbitrary proper-interval connectivity supplies the needed crossing edge. The example does not rule out a more powerful argument that uses the two-word truncation languages and proves such a crossing edge.

## 6. Independent verification and scope

The verifier constructs the matrix directly from its displayed edges and black vertices. It checks:

- all legal full-matrix pressing branches, without a successful-word or node cap;
- all 256 principal determinants using the permutation expansion, independent of pressing or elimination;
- every permutation's principal-prefix criterion against the complete pressing language;
- the exact six pairwise LCS values;
- the instructional root of each word and both seven-vertex truncation languages;
- every interval of each word and every eligible replacement, establishing the exact restricted graph;
- the complete connected original-threshold graph.

The determinant-prefix criterion follows from the block determinant identity: appending a scalar pivot multiplies the current nonzero prefix determinant by that pivot. All arithmetic is binary. No third-party code or copied source text is needed for this certificate.
