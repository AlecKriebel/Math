## 1. Scope and conventions

Let F be an arbitrary field of characteristic p > 0, let L be an arbitrary Lie algebra over F, and put R = s(L) = S(L)/(v^p : v in L). Lie brackets are alternating, including in characteristic 2. The induced Poisson bracket is written { , }. No restricted structure or finite-dimensional assumption on L is imposed.

For a subspace U, products and brackets mean linear spans. Define

- gamma_1(R) = R and gamma_(n+1)(R) = {gamma_n(R), R};
- R^(0) = R and R^(n+1) = {R^(n), R} R;
- delta_0(R) = R and delta_(n+1)(R) = {delta_n(R), delta_n(R)};
- D_0(R) = R and D_(n+1)(R) = {D_n(R), D_n(R)} R.

The ordinary nilpotency class is the least c with gamma_(c+1) = 0; the strong class is the least c with R^(c) = 0. The ordinary and strong derived lengths are the least n with delta_n = 0 and D_n = 0. Thus an abelian unital R has both classes and both lengths equal to 1.

The primary article has an apparent typo in its prose definition of strong nilpotency on PDF page 7: it states two consecutive powers vanish where the earlier one should be nonzero. We use the least-vanishing convention above, consistent with its class-one discussion and proof of Corollary 7.4.

The two target questions remain distinct:

1. Must the two nilpotency classes agree if p = 2 or 3 and R is Lie nilpotent?
2. Must the two derived lengths agree if p > 2 and R is solvable?

## 2. Results established here

**Theorem A (small derived subalgebra).** Suppose L is nilpotent and dim L' <= 2, where L' = [L,L]. In every positive characteristic, the ordinary and strong nilpotency classes of s(L) agree. Their common value is:

- 1 if L' = 0;
- p if dim L' = 1;
- 2p - 1 if dim L' = 2 and [L',L] = 0;
- 3p - 2 if dim L' = 2 and [L',L] != 0.

In particular this proves Question 1 in this range, even for infinite-dimensional L.

**Theorem B (a broader class-two family).** Suppose L has nilpotency class two, m = dim L' is finite, and there is x in L with [x,L] = L'. Then the two nilpotency classes of s(L) are both 1 + (p-1)m. This includes the algebras with basis x, y_1,...,y_m, z_1,...,z_m and brackets [x,y_i] = z_i, together with arbitrary central directions. Equality is also preserved by direct sums of such nilpotent algebras, provided only finitely many nonabelian summands occur, and by adjoining an arbitrary-dimensional central abelian algebra.

**Theorem C (one-dimensional derived subalgebra).** Suppose dim L' <= 1 and p > 2. Then the two derived lengths of s(L) agree, with common value:

- 1 if L' = 0;
- ceil(log_2(p+1)) if dim L' = 1 and L' is central;
- 1 + ceil(log_2 p) if dim L' = 1 and L' is not central.

The last formula and equality of the actual derived series hold also in characteristic 2, although that characteristic is outside Question 2.

**Theorem D (finite counterexample reduction).** Fix a characteristic allowed in either target question. If that question has a counterexample over any field and any dimension, it has a counterexample of finite dimension over a finite field of the same characteristic. More precisely, the pair of unequal finite classes or lengths can be preserved. The finite field may be a nontrivial finite extension of the prime field; reduction to the prime field alone is not asserted.

These are mathematical proofs. The exact computations in Section 9 are independent finite checks, not a proof of either unrestricted equality.

## 3. Basic algebra and central direct summands

Choose a basis of L, of any cardinality. The monomials with finite support and exponents in {0,...,p-1} form a basis of s(L). Indeed in characteristic p the ideal generated by all v^p is generated by the pth powers of the basis vectors. Consequently inclusions H -> L of Lie subalgebras induce injective Poisson maps s(H) -> s(L).

If C is a central abelian direct summand, s(L direct-sum C) = s(L) tensor_F s(C), with the bracket acting only on the first factor. Every one of the four series in Section 1 is exactly its series in s(L), tensored with s(C). To prove this by induction, use

{u tensor a, v tensor b} = {u,v} tensor ab

and the fact that s(C) is unital: every c is c times 1. The same observation deals with the associative ideal products in the strong series. Tensoring with the nonzero F-vector space s(C) preserves nonvanishing. All four finite invariants are therefore unchanged, without a dimension bound on C.

## 4. Nilpotency in class two

Suppose L' is central of finite dimension m. Put I = L'R. Every bracket in R belongs to I, and because the generators of I are Poisson central,

{I^a R, I^b R} is contained in I^(a+b+1),

where I^0 = R. In particular R^(n) is contained in I^n. In a basis z_1,...,z_m of L', no nonzero monomial has z-degree greater than (p-1)m. Thus I^((p-1)m+1) = 0, giving the common upper bound 1+(p-1)m for both classes. This argument works when L itself is infinite-dimensional.

Now suppose there is x with [x,L] = L'. Choose y_i with z_i = [x,y_i] a basis of L'. For every central polynomial P in the z_i,

{xP, xy_i} = xP z_i.

Starting with x and appending xy_1 exactly p-1 times, then xy_2 exactly p-1 times, and so on, produces the left-normed commutator

x z_1^(p-1) ... z_m^(p-1).

It has 1+(p-1)m entries. It is nonzero: x is outside the central L', so x,z_1,...,z_m are independent and its monomial is in the truncated PBW basis. This attains the upper bound and proves Theorem B for a single summand.

The following elementary fact supplies the m=2 case of Theorem A.

**Breadth lemma.** If B: V x V -> W is alternating bilinear and dim span B(V,V) >= 2, some x has dim B(x,V) >= 2.

Proof. Suppose every B(x,V) has dimension at most one. Choose B(a,b)=z != 0. The images of a and b are Fz. If some u has an image not contained in Fz, then B(u,a)=B(u,b)=0: a nonzero one of these belongs to Fz and would force the whole image of u into Fz. Choose v with B(u,v) outside Fz. Then B(a+u,b)=z, whereas B(a+u,v)=B(a,v)+B(u,v) is outside Fz. This contradicts the one-dimensional image assumption. Hence all images are contained in Fz, contrary to the hypothesis. This proof applies over F_2 as well. QED.

Apply the lemma to B(x,y)=[x,y], whose full image span is L'. When dim L'=2, it gives [x,L]=L'. When dim L'=1 and L is nilpotent, L' is central: otherwise the lower central series would stabilize at its nonzero one-dimensional second term. A single nonzero bracket then gives the same construction with m=1.

## 5. The remaining two-dimensional-derived case

Suppose L is nilpotent, dim L'=2, and gamma_3(L) != 0. Strict descent of nonzero terms of the lower central series implies

 dim gamma_3(L)=1, gamma_4(L)=0.

Thus gamma_3 is central and L' is abelian, since [gamma_2,gamma_2] is contained in gamma_4. Fix w spanning gamma_3 and z in L' outside gamma_3. Write [t,z]=alpha(t)w. The functional alpha is nonzero, and [t,w]=0.

There exist u,v with z'=[u,v] outside gamma_3. If both u and v centralized L', the Jacobi identity would give [a,z']=0 for every a, contrary to noncentrality of z'. Thus, after interchanging them if necessary, [u,z'] != 0. The functional giving action on z' is a nonzero scalar multiple of alpha. Set x=u and replace v by y=v-alpha(v)alpha(u)^(-1)u. Then

[x,y]=z', [x,z']=w' != 0, [y,z']=0,

and w' is central. The four elements x,y,z',w' are independent. Indeed x,y are independent modulo L': a dependence modulo L' would force [x,y] into gamma_3, whereas z' is outside gamma_3. The independence of z',w' follows from their different lower-central layers. Hence their span is the four-dimensional filiform subalgebra with brackets [x,y]=z, [x,z]=w, after renaming z',w'.

Here is a maximal commutator valid in every positive characteristic. In s(H), let D be the derivation on F[y,z,w]/(y^p,z^p,w^p) defined by D(y)=z, D(z)=w, D(w)=0. For a polynomial f in this ring,

{x f, xy} = x(z f-yD(f)),    {x f,x} = -xD(f).

Starting with x, append the pair (xy,x) exactly p-1 times. Each pair takes xw^b to -xw^(b+1), so the result is (-1)^(p-1)xw^(p-1). Now append xy exactly p-1 times. Since w^p=0, each such operation takes xz^a w^(p-1) to xz^(a+1)w^(p-1). The resulting commutator is

(-1)^(p-1) x z^(p-1) w^(p-1) != 0.

There are 1+3(p-1)=3p-2 entries.

For the upper bound in all of L, extend z,w to a basis of L. Give z weight 1, w weight 2, and the remaining basis vectors weight 0. Every nonzero bracket has weight at least the sum of the input weights plus 1. By Leibniz the same holds for monomial brackets. Multiplication cannot decrease weight. Consequently R^(n) is contained in the span of monomials of weight at least n. The largest possible weight is (p-1)+2(p-1)=3(p-1), so R^(3p-2)=0. The preceding witness in the embedded s(H) attains this bound. This completes Theorem A.

## 6. Direct-sum closure for the nilpotency result

Let A,B be unital Poisson algebras with finite ordinary classes c,d. Choose nonzero left-normed commutators u={a_0,...,a_(c-1)} and v={b_0,...,b_(d-1)} of maximal lengths. In A tensor B, start with a_0 tensor b_0, append a_i tensor 1 for i=1,...,c-1, then 1 tensor b_j for j=1,...,d-1. The result is u tensor v != 0. Thus the ordinary class of A tensor B is at least c+d-1.

For truncated symmetric algebras of the nilpotent families above, the weight upper bounds add under direct sums: if the largest permitted weights are N_A,N_B, their sum is the largest permitted tensor weight. Hence the strong class is at most N_A+N_B+1. If c=N_A+1 and d=N_B+1, the lower bound is exactly N_A+N_B+1, proving equality. Iterate for finitely many nonabelian summands; arbitrarily many central abelian directions do not change the invariants by Section 3. This proves the closure statement in Theorem B. No closure claim for arbitrary tensor products of derived lengths is made.

## 7. Exact derived lengths when dim L' = 1

### 7.1 Central L'

Write L'=Fz and assume z is central. Some x,y satisfy [x,y]=z, after rescaling, and x,y,z are independent. Let I=zR. Induction gives

D_n(R) is contained in z^(2^n-1) R,

because the bracket adds one further factor of z and z is Poisson central. Therefore D_n=0 as soon as 2^n-1 >= p.

For the matching ordinary lower bound, work in the embedded Heisenberg subalgebra generated by x,y,z. Let V be the span of 1,x,y,x^2,xy,y^2. Because p>2 these are valid distinct truncated monomials. Temporarily denote the constant symplectic bracket on x,y by { , }_0, with {x,y}_0=1. Direct calculations give

1={x,y}_0; x=-{xy,x}_0; y={xy,y}_0;
x^2=-(1/2){xy,x^2}_0; y^2=(1/2){xy,y^2}_0;
xy=(1/4){x^2,y^2}_0.

The bracket of any two elements of V stays in V, so {V,V}_0=V. In the actual Poisson algebra {V,V}=zV. Since z is central,

{z^a V,z^b V}=z^(a+b+1)V.

It follows inductively that delta_n(R) contains z^(2^n-1)V. This is nonzero whenever 2^n-1<p, since in particular z^(2^n-1) is nonzero. The least zero index is therefore ceil(log_2(p+1)) for both series. The proof only uses three elements of L for its lower bound and a central ideal for its upper bound; it permits infinite-dimensional L and an alternating form of infinite rank.

### 7.2 Noncentral L'

Again write L'=Fz. Define alpha by [t,z]=alpha(t)z. Choose x with alpha(x)=1. Set A=ker alpha. For u,v in A write [u,v]=b z. Jacobi gives

[x,[u,v]]=[[x,u],v]+[u,[x,v]]=0,

because [x,u],[x,v] belong to Fz and u,v commute with z. The left side is bz, so b=0. Hence A is abelian. There is a functional beta:A->F with [x,a]=beta(a)z and beta(z)=1. Its kernel C is central in L. Thus

L = (Fx direct-sum Fz) direct-sum C,    [x,z]=z,

where C may have arbitrary dimension. By Section 3 it suffices to compute in

T = F[x,y]/(x^p,y^p),    {x,y}=y.

The monomial formula is

{x^a y^b,x^c y^d}=(ad-bc)x^(a+c-1)y^(b+d),

with any monomial of exponent at least p set to zero. First {T,T}=yT. For x^r y^s with s>=1 and r<=p-2 use {x^(r+1),y^s}/((r+1)s); for r=p-1 use {x,x^(p-1)y^s}/s.

For 1<=q<p let I_q=y^qT. The monomial formula gives {I_q,I_q} contained in I_(2q). If 2q>=p both are zero. Otherwise, for 2q<=s<p and r<=p-2,

{y^q,x^(r+1)y^(s-q)}=-q(r+1)x^r y^s.

For r=p-1 instead use

{xy^q,x^(p-1)y^(s-q)}=s x^(p-1)y^s.

The indicated coefficients are nonzero in F. Hence {I_q,I_q}=I_(2q) exactly. These spaces are already associative ideals, so ordinary and strong series coincide term by term:

delta_n(T)=D_n(T)=y^(2^(n-1))T for n>=1.

The least zero index is 1+ceil(log_2 p). This argument also works for p=2. Together with the abelian case and Section 7.1 it proves Theorem C.

## 8. Reduction to finite dimension and a genuinely finite field

This section is a reduction theorem, not a bounded search claim. It gives no bound on the needed dimension, field size, or unequal lengths.

### 8.1 Finite-dimensional localization

We use the primary structural theorems of Monteiro Alves–Petrogradsky: Lie nilpotence of s(L) implies L nilpotent and dim L'<infinity (Theorem 4.1); for p>2, solvability of s(L) implies L solvable and dim L'<infinity (Theorem 4.3). The same results give finiteness of the corresponding strong invariant. These source theorems are used as established inputs; this report does not reprove their necessity arguments.

Suppose the ordinary and strong invariants in the relevant question are finite numbers a and b. Choose a nonzero evaluation at the last nonzero stage of each series. Such evaluations exist because the series are spanned by their defining multilinear bracket/product expressions. Each evaluation uses finitely many elements of s(L), and every one of those elements involves finitely many basis vectors of L. Let S be the union of those finite supports, and H the Lie subalgebra generated by S.

The subspace span_F(S)+L' is finite-dimensional and closed under Lie brackets: every bracket in L belongs to L'. Thus H is finite-dimensional. The map s(H)->s(L) is injective by the PBW observation in Section 3. Both selected evaluations are still nonzero in s(H). Conversely, every vanishing identity in s(L) holds in its Poisson subalgebra s(H). Therefore both invariants of s(H) equal exactly a and b. If a<b, H is still a counterexample with the same pair.

This is stronger than merely observing that an individual word has finite support: finite-dimensionality of the Lie algebra it generates uses dim L'<infinity. That hypothesis is supplied by the source theorems, with p>2 retained in the solvability case.

### 8.2 Descent and specialization without losing witnesses

Now let H have finite dimension d over a field F of characteristic p, and let its relevant ordinary and strong invariants be a,b. Fix an F-basis e_1,...,e_d and its finitely many structure constants c_ij^k. The truncated monomials e_1^u1...e_d^ud (0<=u_i<p) give a fixed list of p^d basis vectors of s(H).

All multiplication and Poisson-bracket coefficients on this list are polynomials with coefficients in F_p in the c_ij^k. The same is true of every fixed iterated bracket/product evaluation on these monomials.

For clarity, strong-stage spanning expressions may be chosen recursively as

P_0(X)=X; P_(n+1)={P_n,X_(n+1)}Y_(n+1),

for upper Lie powers, and

Q_0(X)=X; Q_(n+1)={Q_n(left variables),Q_n(right variables)}Y,

with disjoint variables for the upper derived series. Ordinary lower/derived expressions omit the multipliers. These are multilinear in all inputs. Consequently a stage is zero if and only if all its evaluations on the finite truncated-monomial basis vanish. Although their number may be enormous, it is finite for every specified stage.

Since the stages immediately preceding a and b are nonzero, multilinearity permits choosing a nonzero basis-input evaluation at each of those stages, and a nonzero output coefficient f and g of those evaluations. (For the nilpotency class a the preceding nonzero stage is gamma_a; for strong class b it is R^(b-1). For derived length a or b it is the stage with index a-1 or b-1.) The coefficients f,g belong to

A_0 = F_p[c_ij^k] contained in F.

Let A=A_0[(fg)^(-1)]. This is a nonzero finitely generated F_p-algebra contained in F. The free A-module A^d with the same structure constants is a Lie algebra: alternation, skew-symmetry and Jacobi are polynomial equalities already zero in the subring A of F. Its truncated symmetric algebra is free over A on the same p^d monomials.

All basis-input evaluations of the two required zero-stage identities have coefficients equal to zero in F, hence equal to zero in A. They remain zero under every quotient of A. Meanwhile f and g are units in A, so their images under any field quotient are nonzero.

Choose a maximal ideal m of A. The field k=A/m is a finitely generated F_p-algebra which is a field. By Zariski's lemma, k is a finite algebraic extension of F_p. In particular k is a finite field F_(p^r) for some positive integer r. This conclusion is stronger than merely obtaining a finitely generated field extension.

Specialize all c_ij^k to k. Alternation and Jacobi survive, so this defines a d-dimensional Lie algebra H_k over k; its underlying module has the chosen d-element basis, so the dimension does not collapse. The fixed monomial basis of s(H_k) also has p^d elements. Universal vanishing survives because every relevant basis-input evaluation remains zero. Both last-nonzero witnesses survive because their selected coefficients were inverted before quotienting. Thus the ordinary and strong invariants are exactly a and b after specialization.

In particular nilpotence or solvability of s(H_k) is preserved, with the same ordinary and strong bounds. Nilpotence or solvability of H_k itself also follows by its embedding in s(H_k); alternatively its finitely many basis-evaluations of the corresponding Lie identities are polynomial zero equations and survive directly. If desired one may preserve the exact finite nilpotency class or derived length of H as well by inverting a selected nonzero coefficient of its last-nonzero Lie-stage evaluation, but this is not needed for the counterexample reduction.

Combining Sections 8.1 and 8.2 proves Theorem D. It does not prove that a counterexample exists, or that testing any fixed finite collection suffices. Nor does it claim preservation of an isomorphism type under specialization.

## 10. Exact boundaries and unresolved work

The unrestricted equalities remain open in this work. For Question 1, the proven low-derived-dimension range stops at dim L'=2; Theorem B and its direct-sum closure cover additional higher-dimensional-derived examples. General class-two bracket maps without a full-image adjoint element, and higher nilpotency classes with dim L'>=3, are not settled by these arguments. An example outside a sufficient criterion is not a counterexample to equality.

For Question 2, the proof covers dim L'<=1 for every p>2, not all solvable L with finite-dimensional L'. There is no assertion that the derived-length problem follows from nilpotency-class equality. There is no assertion for ordinary-versus-strong derived-length equality in characteristic 2.

The source's general p>3 nilpotency proof uses product-of-commutator inclusions. Its Lemma 8.3 obtains a multiple by 3, and Lemma 8.6 uses division by 2. The former route therefore does not establish the needed inclusion at p=3, and the latter is unavailable at p=2. These failures of that proof are not evidence that the desired equality is false. Our explicit words avoid those divisions in the families treated above.

The finite-field reduction identifies a precise search setting but leaves unbounded dimension and finite-extension degree. The computations do not range over all such algebras, fields, or characteristics. No novelty, global openness certificate, or completed resolution is claimed.

### Appendix: why the residue field in Section 8 is finite

Here is the algebraic lemma used there. If K is a field finitely generated as an algebra over a field k_0, then K is a finite algebraic extension of k_0. Choose a maximal algebraically independent subset t_1,...,t_r of a finite algebra-generating set. The remaining generators are algebraic over k_0(t_1,...,t_r). Invert a single nonzero polynomial h in the t_i clearing all denominators of the coefficients in their finitely many monic algebraic equations. Then K is integral over B=k_0[t_1,...,t_r,1/h]. If a field is integral over a subring B, that subring is a field: for 0!=b in B, a monic integral equation for b^(-1), multiplied by b^(n-1), expresses b^(-1) as an element of B. If r>0, however, B is not a field. There are infinitely many pairwise nonassociate irreducibles in k_0[t_1] (Euclid's argument), and one not dividing h is a nonunit even after adjoining t_2,...,t_r and inverting h. This is a contradiction. Thus r=0, and finitely many algebraic generators give a finite-degree extension. Applied to k_0=F_p, a degree-r extension has exactly p^r elements. This proves the precise finite-field assertion without replacing it by a merely finitely generated extension.
