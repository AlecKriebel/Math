# Attempt 1/5: remove the complete-class wrapper and try Hall theory

Date: 2026-10-03 UTC. Objective: prove the whole normal-intersection assertion from odd-order solvability and Hall theory. Outcome: rigorous reductions and a positive solvable-kernel case; the general problem remains open in this attempt.

## Exact target

For a nonempty subgroup-, quotient-, and extension-closed class X of finite odd-order groups, H maximal among X-subgroups of finite G and N normal G, show H∩N maximal among X-subgroups of N.

## 1. The class is exactly an odd-prime class

Put pi={p prime : C_p belongs to X}. Nonemptiness and subgroup closure give 1 in X. Every prime divisor p of the order of an X-group belongs to pi by Cauchy's theorem and subgroup closure. Thus X is contained in the class G_pi of finite pi-groups, and 2 is not in pi.

Conversely, let K be a finite pi-group. Its order is odd, so the Feit–Thompson theorem makes K solvable. Every composition factor of K is therefore cyclic of prime order C_p with p in pi. Starting from 1 and applying extension closure along a composition series gives K in X. Consequently X=G_pi. If pi is empty, X={1} and the problem is immediate. This argument uses the odd-order theorem explicitly; it does not assume that every arbitrary complete class is a prime class.

This also follows from the standard inclusions S_pi⊆X⊆G_pi in Guo–Revin–Vdovin, arXiv:1808.10107v2 §1.1, together with odd-order solvability. It is a known reduction, not a novelty claim.

## 2. Reduce the ambient group to HN

Replacing G by G0=HN keeps N normal and keeps H pi-maximal: an intermediate pi-subgroup H<L≤HN would already contradict maximality in G. The target intersection A=H∩N is unchanged. We can therefore assume G=HN. In this reduced configuration G/N≅H/A is a pi-group of odd order.

This restriction is important: a counterexample cannot be supplied merely by choosing an arbitrary nonmaximal odd subgroup of N. It must arise as the normal intersection of a globally maximal pi-subgroup in an odd pi-extension of N.

## 3. Hall theory proves the assertion if N is solvable

H has odd order, hence is solvable. If N is solvable, then HN is an extension of N by the solvable group H/(H∩N), so HN is solvable. Hall's theorem gives a pi-Hall subgroup P of HN containing H. Since H is pi-maximal in HN, P=H.

The usual index formula gives

[N:H∩N]=[HN:H].

The right side is a pi'-number because H is pi-Hall in HN. Thus A=H∩N is pi-Hall in N. If A≤B≤N is a pi-subgroup, then [B:A] is simultaneously a pi-number and a divisor of [N:A], a pi'-number. Hence B=A. This proves the requested maximality whenever N is solvable, with no solvability assumption on the original ambient G.

The same index argument works whenever H happens to be pi-Hall in HN (in particular, if H is pi-Hall in G). It does not work for general pi-maximal H: maximal-by-inclusion is strictly weaker than Hall.

## 4. The failed extension step

To finish by this method one would need every pi-maximal H in HN to be pi-Hall. That assertion is false for arbitrary nonsolvable HN. Even a simple group can have pi-maximal subgroups of differing orders, and Sylow theory does not combine arbitrary odd primes into Hall subgroups.

The exact unresolved step is: in a nonsolvable N, an A-containing pi-subgroup B can exist without an H-invariant such B. The group generated by H and B need not be a pi-group. Oddness of H and B separately says nothing sufficient about the order of their join.

## Result after this turn

The full question is equivalent to the pi-maximal normal-intersection question for arbitrary sets pi of odd primes. It is true for solvable N and for the Hall case. No universal proof or counterexample has been obtained. Budget used: 1/5 substantive attempts.
