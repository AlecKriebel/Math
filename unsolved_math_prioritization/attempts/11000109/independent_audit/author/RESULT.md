# A negative answer to the twice-punctured-torus extension question

Problem 11000109 / AMR-109-0109, Korkmaz Problem 2.4.

## Result and credit

The answer is **no**. The orientation-preserving mapping class group of a torus with two marked points has a subgroup of index 48 and an automorphism of that very subgroup which is not the restriction of any automorphism of the ambient group.

This is a reconstruction of the prior Behrstock–Margalit construction, with its applicability to Korkmaz's exact conventions and its obstruction to *all* ambient automorphisms made explicit. It is not a new-discovery or first-resolution claim. The argument uses the classical branched-cover identification and a standard sphere rigidity theorem; those substantial topological inputs are credited below rather than re-proved.

## 1. Conventions and notation

All surfaces here are connected and orientable. There is no boundary. A puncture can equivalently be treated as a distinguished marked point. Write Mod⁺(S) for orientation-preserving mapping classes allowing permutations of the marked points, Mod±(S) when orientation reversal is also allowed, and PMod⁺(S) for the orientation-preserving subgroup fixing every marked point separately.

The group in the original question is

G = Mod⁺(S₁,₂),

not a boundary-fixing mapping class group and not the pure or extended group. The extension sought in that question is an arbitrary abstract automorphism of G, not just an inner automorphism.

Let a sphere have five marked points p₀,p₁,p₂,p₃,p₄. Put

M = Mod±(S₀,₅),   M⁺ = Mod⁺(S₀,₅),
H = Stab_{M⁺}(p₀),   K = PMod⁺(S₀,₅).

The permutation map π:M⁺→S₅ is onto: half-twists realize transpositions, and these generate S₅. Thus K=ker π is normal in M⁺, H=π⁻¹(Stab_{S₅}(0)), and [H:K]=24. In fact K is also normal in M and [M:K]=240.

## 2. Precisely stated topological inputs

**Input A (branched-cover description).** There is an isomorphism

Θ:G → H × C₂,

where the C₂ factor is generated by the central hyperelliptic involution ι that exchanges the two marked points of the torus. Under Θ, the pure torus subgroup maps onto H×{0}.

This is Behrstock–Margalit, §4.2 and Proposition 9, with orientation restricted. An explicit later account with both orientation conventions is Bridson–Wade, Propositions 6.1 and 6.4. The quotient by ι has four branch points and the additional point p₀ coming from the exchanged torus points. For clarity about the restriction: the covering is orientation-preserving away from branch points, so a lift and its descended map have the same orientation sign; ι itself preserves orientation. The unique pure representative in each coset modulo ⟨ι⟩ therefore retains the original sign. Hence the extended version of the identification restricts to exactly G and H above.

**Input B (faithfulness of sphere commensurations).** The natural conjugation map

M → Comm(M)

is injective for S₀,₅. This is the kernel assertion of Behrstock–Margalit, Theorem 6, attributed there in this genus to Korkmaz. Only injectivity is needed here, not surjectivity or an automorphism classification for the torus.

**Consequence.** C_M(K)={1}. Indeed, an element centralizing K induces the identity commensuration because K has finite index in M; Input B then makes that element trivial. In particular Z(H)={1}, since K⊂H and every element of Z(H) centralizes K. Therefore

Z(H×C₂)={1}×C₂.

## 3. An automorphism of the same finite-index subgroup

Choose f∈M⁺ with permutation π(f)=(0 1), for instance the half-twist on an arc joining p₀ to p₁ and avoiding the other points. No finite-order assertion about f is required.

Define

Γ = Θ⁻¹(K×{0}).

Then Γ is a subgroup of G with

[G:Γ] = [H×C₂:K×{0}] = 24·2 = 48.

As K is normal in M⁺, conjugation by f sends K onto K, with inverse conjugation by f⁻¹. Consequently the formula

φ(Θ⁻¹(k,0)) = Θ⁻¹(f k f⁻¹,0),  k∈K,

defines an automorphism φ:Γ→Γ. In particular, domain and image are exactly the same specified subgroup. This is not merely an isomorphism between two unspecified commensurable subgroups.

## 4. No ambient automorphism can restrict to φ

Suppose, for contradiction, that α∈Aut(G) restricts to φ. Transport α by Θ to an automorphism A of H×C₂. Since the center is characteristic and is exactly {1}×C₂, A induces an automorphism β of the center quotient H. The prescribed restriction on Γ gives

β(k)=f k f⁻¹  for every k∈K.

For h∈H and k∈K, normality of K in H gives hkh⁻¹∈K. Applying β to this identity yields

β(h) f k f⁻¹ β(h)⁻¹ = f h k h⁻¹ f⁻¹.

Conjugating by f⁻¹ and rearranging shows that

h⁻¹ f⁻¹ β(h) f ∈ C_M(K).

The centralizer consequence of Input B makes this element the identity. Therefore

β(h)=f h f⁻¹  for every h∈H.

In particular fHf⁻¹⊂H (and surjectivity of β would give equality). This is impossible already at the permutation level. Choose h∈H with π(h)=(1 2), which exists by surjectivity of the permutation map on H. Then

π(f h f⁻¹)=(0 1)(1 2)(0 1)=(0 2),

which moves p₀; hence f h f⁻¹∉H. This contradicts β(h)∈H and proves the result.

## 5. Scope and pitfalls

- The proof handles every abstract ambient automorphism, including ones with a central transvection component. Passing to the characteristic center quotient removes that component. No assertion Aut(G)=Inn(G) is used.
- The invariant subgroup is explicit and normal in G. Conjugation by f preserves it because the pure sphere subgroup is normal in the entire sphere group.
- The 48 is a verified index for this example, not a minimal-index claim.
- The full torus group permits exchanging its two punctures; the pure subgroup used in the covering identification has index two. Confusing these groups would change the index and the target.
- The argument proves existence of a nonextendable automorphism, which completely refutes the universal assertion in Problem 2.4. It does not classify which automorphisms of arbitrary finite-index subgroups extend.
- The exceptional commensurator alone would not establish this conclusion. The normal subgroup construction and the all-automorphism obstruction above are essential.
