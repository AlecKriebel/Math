How a population is connected can change the chance that a beneficial mutation
spreads to everyone. A population network is called an amplifier when it makes
such fixation more likely than in a well-mixed population. We ask whether one
fixed finite weighted network can amplify every beneficial mutation under a
death–birth replacement rule. We prove that it cannot. When some replacement
links are missing, sufficiently strong selection eventually produces a
fixation deficit. When every directed link is present, unequal competition
weights create a deficit whose leading term is an explicit sum of nonnegative
squares. The only complete networks with no deficit are dynamically equivalent
to the well-mixed process, so they tie rather than amplify. We also give exact
all-beneficial-fitness classifications for every positive weighted triangle
and for two natural symmetric four-vertex families; every nonuniform example
is a suppressor. Exact symbolic programs rebuild the finite Markov chains and
replay the algebraic certificates. The theorem concerns each fixed finite
network. It leaves open a different question in which population size is
allowed to grow after the mutation's fitness is fixed.
