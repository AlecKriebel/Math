Original problem

    

    

      

        
The result

        
No finite nonempty placement of knights and rooks can make every knight attack exactly four rooks and no knights, while every rook attacks exactly two knights and no rooks.

        
This answers the N=4, R=2 cell in the Knights and Rooks table on Erich Friedman’s February 2007 Math Magic page. That cell still displayed a question mark when checked on 17 September 2026 UTC.

        

          
Stronger theorem.
 Let the knight and rook squares be finite, nonempty, disjoint subsets of the integer grid. If every rook sees exactly two knights and no rooks, then some knight attacks at most three rooks. Knight-to-knight attacks are permitted.

        

        
Rook visibility stops at the first occupied square in each orthogonal direction. Knights jump in the usual way. Attacks are directed: a knight attacking a rook does not imply that the rook attacks that knight.

      

      

        
Why the placement is impossible

        
A rook cannot lie farther right than every knight: it could then see a knight only to its west, giving at most one target. Translate a rightmost knight to the origin. Its four possible rook targets in the occupied half-plane are therefore forced.

        
Those rooks force intervening knights to block rook-to-rook attacks. Once a rook already sees two knights, its remaining rays must be empty. Following these local constraints forces another knight with at most three possible rook targets, contradicting the required four.

        
The argument covers every finite board size and every finite piece inventory. The full coordinate proof is available in the note and as

Scope and provenance

        
The result addresses only the N=4, R=2 knights-and-rooks cell. It does not resolve the other open questions on Friedman’s page. The finite, nonempty, ordinary integer-grid model is essential to the stated scope; no infinite or wrapped-board conclusion is claimed.
