                                        We show that every two-way deterministic finite automaton (2dfa) that solves one-way liveness on height h has
                                        Ω(h2 ) states. This implies a quadratic lower bound for converting one-way nondeterministic finite automata to 2dfas,
                                        which asymptotically matches Chrobak’s well-known lower bound for this conversion on unary languages. In contrast
                                        to Chrobak’s simple proof, which relies on a 2dfa’s inability to differentiate between any two sufficiently distant loca-
                                        tions in a unary input, our argument can be applied to inputs over any alphabet and is structured around a main lemma
                                        that is general enough to potentially be reused elsewhere.

                                        Keywords: two-way finite automata, size complexity, Sakoda-Sipser conjecture, minicomplexity




                                        1       Introduction
                                        Back in the 1970s, Sakoda and Sipser [22] (but also Seiferas [23]) asked a fundamental question, which
                                        to this day remains an important open problem in automata and complexity theory: Can every two-way
                                        nondeterministic finite automaton (2nfa) with some number of states s be simulated by a two-way deter-
                                        ministic finite automaton (2dfa) with a number of states f (s) which is polynomial in s?
                                           A standard way to approach this question is to study variant cases: cases where either the 2dfa is
                                        enhanced or the 2nfa is restricted, if we want to prove an upper bound for the respective variant f (s); or
                                        cases where either the 2dfa is restricted or the 2nfa is enhanced, if we want to prove a lower bound for
                                        the respective variant f (s).
                                           Indeed, a polynomial upper bound is known when the simulating 2dfa is enhanced into some restricted
                                        single-tape Turing machine: a weight-reducing one (i.e., one that overwrites symbols only by “lighter”
                                        ones) or a bounded & linear-time one (i.e., a linear-time one that writes only within the input) [9]. Likewise,
                                        a quasi-polynomial upper bound is known when the simulated 2nfa is restricted to unary (i.e., accepting
                                        only unary inputs) [8]; or letter-bounded (i.e., accepting only inputs of the form a∗1 a∗2 · · · a∗k for fixed k) [5];
                                        or outer-nondeterministic (i.e., using nondeterminism only on the endmarkers) [6].

    On the flip side, an exponential lower bound is known when the simulating 2dfa is restricted to single-
pass (i.e., halting upon reaching an endmarker) [23]; or sweeping (i.e., reversing only on the endmark-
ers) [25]; or (almost) oblivious (i.e., following sub-linearly many trajectories per input length) [11]; or
few-reversal (i.e., reversing sub-linearly often) [14]; or logical (i.e., satisfying some formula on “reacha-
bility variables” in each state) [10, 2, 21]; and an infinite lower bound is known when the 2dfa is a mole
(i.e., it only explores the configuration graph of the 2nfa) [13]. Likewise, an exponential lower bound is
known when the simulated 2nfa is enhanced into a single-pebble 2nfa (i.e., one equipped with a pebble
that it can drop on and lift from an input cell) [7]; or into a 1-limited automaton (i.e., a single-tape nonde-
terministic Turing machine that can overwrite an input cell when it first visits it) [18], even a once-marking
one (i.e., one that can only place a mark once on a single cell of the input) [19].
    In this landscape, a natural question is: What about lower bounds for unrestricted 2dfas?
    There are only two results of this kind and, quite counterintuitively, both are obtained in scenarios where
the simulated 2nfa is (not in any way enhanced, but instead) severely restricted!
    The first one is Chrobak’s well-known bound f (s) = Ω(s2 ) for when the simulated 2nfa is both unary
(as above) and one-way (i.e., moving its input head only forward; namely, a standard one-way nondeter-
ministic finite automaton or 1nfa) [4, Thm. 6.3] (cf. also [27]). Expectedly, the simple proof of this result
relies heavily on the inability of the simulating 2dfa to differentiate between distant locations on the unary
input. Moreover, a matching upper bound [4, Thm. 6.2] shows that no better lower bound is possible in
this special case.
    The second result is the bound f (s) = Ω(s2 / log s) of [15, Lemma 7] for the case where the simulated
2nfa is both one-way (again) and accepting only inputs of length 3.(i) The (counting) argument for this
second result is also quite simple. Moreover, here too, the possibility of a better lower bound is removed
by a (quite non-trivial) matching upper bound [15, Lemma 8].
    Our present study offers a third lower bound for unrestricted 2dfas. As in the two existing results, we
focus on 2nfas that are one-way, i.e., 1nfas; and, like Chrobak, we prove f (s) = Ω(s2 ). Unlike the two
existing results, however, our 1nfas work on alphabets of any size and inputs of any length, which forces
us into a much deeper analysis of the relevant computations.
    We express our argument in terms of one-way liveness (owl), the complete language (family) for the
conversion of 1nfas to 2dfas [22, Thm. 2.3]. Specifically, we prove that every 2dfa that solves owl on
height h has Ω(h2 ) states, which directly implies that f (s) = Ω(s2 ), since owl on height h is solved by
a 1nfa with h states [22, Thm. 2.3i].
    After some preliminaries (Sect. 2), we recall (from [13]) the basic definitions and facts about generic
strings (Sect. 3.1); and show how these force a 2dfa to use its states on their outer boundaries (Sect. 3.2).
We then prove our main lemma (Lemma 11), that a suitably selected pair of properties can force a 2dfa
to spend ≥ 1 state just to distinguish between them. Finally, we identify a quadratically long sequence of
properties where every two successive ones are as described in the lemma (Sect. 4). This then implies the
promised lower bound.

2      Preparation
For n ≥ 1, let [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n}. The set Σ ∗ consists of all finite strings over Σ. If z ∈ Σ ∗ , we let
|z|, zi , z i be its length, its ith symbol (i ∈ [|z|]), and the concatenation of i copies of itself (i ≥ 0). If
f : S → S, then f i is the composition of i copies of f (i ≥ 0). We let B := {0,1} be the set of Boolean
values and Bn×m be the set of all n × m Boolean matrices (n, m ≥ 1).

2.1      Two-way finite automata
A two-way deterministic finite automaton (2dfa) is a tuple of the form M = (Q, Σ, δ, qs , qa , qr ), where
Q is a set of states; Σ is an input alphabet; the states qs ,qa ,qr ∈ Q are the start, accept, and reject states,
respectively; and δ : Q × (Σ ∪ {⊢,⊣}) → Q × {l,r} is a total transition function, where ⊢,⊣ ̸∈ Σ are the
left and right endmarkers, and l,r are the two directions. An input z ∈ Σ ∗ is presented to M between the
endmarkers, as ⊢z⊣. The computation starts at qs on the left endmarker. In each step, the next state and
head move are derived from δ and the current state and symbol. Endmarkers are never violated, except
for ⊣ when the next state is qa or qr . Hence, the computation can either loop; or fall off ⊣ into one of qa ,qr .
   Formally, the computation of M from state p and “the jth symbol of string z” (where 0 ≤ j ≤ |z|+1),
denoted by compM,p,j (z), is the unique sequence c = (qt , jt )0≤t<1+m of state-position pairs, called
configurations, where 0 ≤ m ≤ ∞; q0 := p and j0 := j; for all 0 ≤ t < m, the t-th position satisfies
1 ≤ jt ≤ |z|; and every next state qt+1 and position jt+1 are derived from δ and the current state qt and
symbol zjt . If m = ∞, we say the computation loops. Otherwise, m is finite and the last position jm is
either |z|+1 or 0, in which cases we say the computation hits right or hits left into qm , respectively.(ii) If
j = 1 or j = |z|, we get the left computation and right computation of M from p on z, respectively:(iii)

                 lcompM,p (z) := compM,p,1 (z)                    and         rcompM,p (z) := compM,p,|z| (z) .

Then the computation of M on z is its left computation from qs on ⊢z⊣, in symbols

                                                compM (z) := lcompM,qs (⊢z⊣) ;

and M accepts z if this computation hits right into qa . As usual, M solves a language L ⊆ Σ ∗ if it accepts
exactly its strings: M accepts z ⇐⇒ z ∈ L.

2.2      One-way liveness
Let h ≥ 1. The alphabet Σh := P([h]×[h]) consists of every two-column directed graph with h nodes
per column and only rightward arrows (Fig. 1L). An n-long string z ∈ Σh∗ is naturally viewed as a graph
of n+1 columns, indexed from 0 to n, where edges connect successive columns and, for simplicity, are
undirected (Fig. 1M). If there exists an n-long path from some node of column 0 to some node of column n,
then z is live; otherwise, it is dead. Checking that a given z ∈ Σh∗ is live is the one-way liveness problem
for height h, denoted by owlh .

    Given any n-long z ∈ Σh∗ , its connectivity C(z) ∈ Bh×h is the h×h Boolean matrix where cell (i, j) is 1
iff z contains an n-long path from node i of column 0 to node j of column n (Fig. 1R). Easily, concatenation
of strings corresponds to Boolean multiplication of connectivities, namely C(xy) = C(x)C(y), where
every + and · on Boolean values is ∨ and ∧, respectively. So, equivalently, owlh is defined over the
alphabet Bh×h and consists in checking that a given string of Boolean matrices of dimension h × h has
non-zero Boolean product.
    The problem is important in studying how easily 2dfas can simulate one-way nondeterministic finite
automata (1nfas, as typically defined [20, 26]). Formally, the family owl = (owlh )h≥1 is complete, under
homomorphic reductions, for the class 1N of all language families L = (Lh )h≥1 that can be recognized
by 2dfa families M = (Mh )h≥1 where the number of states grows polynomially in h [22]. Intuitively,
owlh captures in its instances all computations of all h-state 1nfas. So, if a 2dfa can solve owlh with
≤ s(h) states, then every h-state 1nfa can be simulated by a 2dfa with ≤ s(h) states; and, conversely, if
every 2dfa for owlh needs ≥ s(h) states, then some h-state 1nfa requires ≥ s(h) states in every 2dfa that
simulates it.

2.3    Properties
A property (of the strings) over an alphabet Σ is any set P ⊆ Σ ∗ . If x ∈ P , we also say that “x has P ”.
We are interested in properties over Σh that are defined by connectivities:
Definition 1. For any C ∈ Bh×h , we let P (C) := {z ∈ Σh∗ | C(z) = C} be the property of all strings
over Σh that have connectivity C.
   A property P over an alphabet Σ is smooth if it is “closed under concatenation with infix”, meaning
that every two strings in the property have an infix over Σ that keeps their concatenation in the property,
namely: (∀x ∈ P )(∀z ∈ P )(∃y ∈ Σ ∗ )(xyz ∈ P ).
Example 1. For any i, j ∈ [h], let Ci,j ∈ Bh×h be the matrix where cell (i, j) is the only cell that contains 1.
Then property Pi,j := P (Ci,j ) is smooth, because any two strings x and z in it have the one-symbol infix
y := {(j, i)} that ensures that xyz is also in Pi,j .
   Given two properties, P and P ′ over an alphabet Σ, we say that P lr-extends to P ′ if every string in P
can be lr-extended into a string in P ′ , namely if (∀x ∈ P )(∃y ∈ Σ ∗ )(xy ∈ P ′ ).

Example 3. For any i, j ∈ [h], let Pi,∗ and Pi,j be as in Example 2. Then Pi,∗ (not only lr-extends to Pi,j ,

   In even more special cases, the suffix that lr-extends into P ′ all strings of P ∪ P ′ can also be chosen

Example 4. Let I, J ∈ Bh×h be the identity matrix and the matrix with 1s in all of the first row and
nowhere else, respectively. Then P := P (I) is the property of having h disjoint paths that connect every
leftmost node to the corresponding rightmost node; and P ′ := P (I + J) is like P except that the top
leftmost node also connects to all rightmost nodes. We argue that P has suffix of choice into P ′ . Indeed,
pick any v ∈ P . Choose u ∈ P ′ (any such u will do) and consider the suffix y := uv. Easily, for every
x ∈ P ∪ P ′ , appending y forces xy into P ′ .
   Symmetric variants of the above relations are also relevant: we say that P rl-extends to P ′ if (∀x ∈
P )(∃y ∈ Σ ∗ )(yx ∈ P ′ ); that P has a prefix into P ′ if (∃y ∈ Σ ∗ )(∀x ∈ P ∪ P ′ )(yx ∈ P ′ ); and that P
has a prefix of choice into P ′ if (∀v ∈ P )(∃u ∈ Σ ∗ )(∀x ∈ P ∪ P ′ )(vux ∈ P ′ ).
   Finally, we say that two properties P, P ′ ⊆ Σ ∗ are separated by a language L ⊆ Σ ∗ if every two of
their strings have a context where the choice between them affects membership in L:

                  (∀x ∈ P )(∀z ∈ P ′ )(∃u ∈ Σ ∗ )(∃v ∈ Σ ∗ )(uxv ∈ L ⇐⇒ uzv ̸∈ L) .

In particular, distinct connectivities define properties that are separated by owl:
Lemma 1. Let C, C ′ ∈ Bh×h . If C ̸= C ′ then P (C), P (C ′ ) are separated by owlh .

Proof: Since C ̸= C ′ , there exists cell (i, j) such that C(i, j) ̸= C ′ (i, j). Without loss of generality,
assume C(i, j) = 0 and C ′ (i, j) = 1. Now pick any x ∈ P (C) and z ∈ P (C ′ ). Then x has no path
from the ith leftmost node to the jth rightmost node, but z does. Consider the symbols u := {(1, i)}, v :=
{(j, 1)} ∈ Σh . Clearly, uxv is dead and uzv is live.


3     A lower-bound lemma
Let L be a language over some alphabet Σ. This section proves a general lemma for lower-bounding the
number of states in a 2dfa solving L. Section 4 uses this lemma for Σ = Σh and L = owlh to show a
lower bound of Ω(h2 ).
   So, fix a 2dfa M that solves L with states Q. (Since M is fixed, we drop unnecessary subscripts: e.g.,
for lcompM,p (z), we just write lcompp (z).)

3.1    Generic strings
Introduced in [24], generic strings continue to be the main building block that we have for constructing
hard inputs for 2dfas of various kinds [12, 13, 16, 14, 17]. Here, we are using the flavor of [13, Sect. 3.2].
This subsection briefly recalls concepts and facts from that work that we need in our arguments.
   For y ∈ Σ ∗ , consider the set of “exit states” that M hits right into at the end of a left computation on y:

                       Qlr (y) := {q ∈ Q | (∃p ∈ Q)(lcompp (y) hits right into q)} .
6                                                                     Kehinde Adeogun, Christos Kapoutsis

When we lr-extend y to some yz, the size of this set cannot possibly increase, because the function αy,z
that maps every q ∈ Qlr (y) to the state (if any) that is hit right by the computation compq,|y|+1 (yz) is
easily seen to be a partial surjection from Qlr (y) onto Qlr (yz).
Lemma 2 ([13, Fact 3]). For all y,z, the function αy,z partially surjects Qlr (y) onto Qlr (yz); hence
|Qlr (y)| ≥ |Qlr (yz)|.
  Consequently, if we start with a string y in a property P and keep lr-extending it inside P , we will at
some point have minimized the size of the set of exit states. When that happens, the string has become
“lr-generic”.
Definition 2 ([13, Def. 3]). A string y is lr-generic for property P if y ∈ P and, for all yz ∈ P , we have
|Qlr (y)| = |Qlr (yz)|.
Lemma 3 ([13, Lemma 3]). Every property P ̸= ∅ has lr-generic strings.

Proof: Pick y ∈ P . If y is lr-generic, we are done. Otherwise, there is yz1 ∈ P such that |Qlr (y)| >
|Qlr (yz1 )|. So, we replace y with yz1 and repeat: either yz1 is lr-generic and we are done; or there is
yz1 z2 ∈ P such that |Qlr (yz1 z2 )| is even smaller, and we replace yz1 with yz1 z2 and repeat. Clearly, this
eventually terminates, as M has finitely many states and every step decreases |Qlr ( · )|.
  In addition, every further lr-extension of a lr-generic string inside the property continues to be lr-
generic for the property:
Lemma 4. If y is lr-generic for P , then so is every yz ∈ P .

Proof: Towards a contradiction, say y is lr-generic for P , but some yz ∈ P is not. Then |Qlr (y)| =
|Qlr (yz)| (since y is lr-generic and yz ∈ P ) but there exists yzz ′ ∈ P such that |Qlr (yz)| > |Qlr (yzz ′ )|
(since yz is not lr-generic). Then z̃ := zz ′ is such that yz̃ ∈ P but |Qlr (y)| > |Qlr (yz̃)|, contrary to the
lr-genericity of y.
   Finally, the size |Qlr (yz)| is upper bounded, not only by the respective size |Qlr (y)| for the prefix y
(the “prefix-monotonicity” given by Lemma 2), but also by the respective size |Qlr (z)| for the suffix z (a
“suffix-monotonicity”).
Lemma 5 ([13, Fact 4]). For all y,z: Qlr (yz) ⊆ Qlr (z); so |Qlr (yz)| ≤ |Qlr (z)|.
   Symmetrically to the above, we can also define the set Qrl (y) of exit states that M hits left into after
right computations on y; note the partial surjection βz,y : Qrl (y) → Qrl (zy), mapping every q to the
state (if any) hit left by compq,|z| (zy); conclude that |Qrl (y)| cannot increase when we rl-extend y (suffix-
monotonicity); define rl-generic strings and show that they exist for every P ̸= ∅; show that rl-extensions
preserve rl-genericity; and argue that Qrl (z) ⊇ Qrl (zy) and thus prefix-monotonicity also holds.
   In the end, we are interested in strings that are generic in both directions, and we are guaranteed to have
them for non-empty properties that are also smooth.
Definition 3 ([13, Def. 3]). A string is generic if it is both lr- and rl-generic.
Lemma 6 ([13, Lemma 3]). Every smooth property P ̸= ∅ has generic strings.

Proof: Since P ̸= ∅, there exist x, z ∈ P that are respectively lr- and rl-generic for P (by Lemma 3 and
its symmetric variant). Since P is smooth, there exists y such that w := xyz ∈ P . Then w is both lr- and

rl-generic for P , as a lr-extension of x and a rl-extension of z (Lemma 4 and its symmetric variant).
Therefore, w is generic for P .

3.2    Exit sizes
The previous section studied how the size of the set Qlr (y) changes as y ∈ P is lr-extended within P .
Call this number a(y) := |Qlr (y)| the “lr-size” of y. We now study how this number changes when y is
replaced by other strings.
  We first note that switching between lr-generic strings for P preserves the lr-size, provided that P is
smooth. Hence, this number is a feature of P .
Lemma 7. Let x,z be lr-generic strings for a smooth P . Then a(x) = a(z).

Proof: Suppose P is smooth and strings x, z are lr-generic for it. Since P is smooth, there exists y such
that xyz ∈ P . Then a(x) = |Qlr (x)| = |Qlr (xyz)|, by the definition of a(x) and since xyz ∈ P lr-
extends the lr-generic x. But also |Qlr (xyz)| ≤ |Qlr (z)| = a(z), by suffix monotonicity (Lemma 5) and
the definition of a(z). Overall, a(x) ≤ a(z). Finally, a(z) ≤ a(x) is also true, symmetrically.

Definition 4. The lr-size a(P ) of a smooth P ̸= ∅ is the common lr-size of all lr-generic strings of P .
  Moreover, a(P ) is a lower bound for the lr-size of any string in P , and is reached by exactly the lr-
generic strings of P .
Lemma 8. Let P ̸= ∅ be smooth. Every x ∈ P satisfies a(x) ≥ a(P ); and the equality holds iff x is
lr-generic for P .

Proof: Let x ∈ P . As in the proof of Lemma 3, x can be lr-extended to some lr-generic string xz for P .
Then a(x) = |Qlr (x)| ≥ |Qlr (xz)| = a(xz) = a(P ) (by Lemma 2 and the definitions of a( · ), a(P )),
hence a(x) ≥ a(P ).
  Now let x be lr-generic for P . Then a(x) = a(P ) (Def. 4). Conversely, assume a(x) = a(P ).
Pick any lr-extension xz ∈ P . We know |Qlr (x)| ≥ |Qlr (xz)|, by Lemma 2. But we also know that
|Qlr (x)| ≤ |Qlr (xz)|, because |Qlr (x)| = a(x) = a(P ) (by definition of a(x) and our assumption) and
a(P ) ≤ |Qlr (xz)| (by the first part of our proof, and since xz ∈ P ). Overall, |Qlr (x)| = |Qlr (xz)| for
the arbitrary lr-extension of x in P . So, x is lr-generic for P .
  Finally, the lr-sizes of distinct smooth properties can be compared, if one lr-extends to the other:
Lemma 9. If P, P ′ ̸= ∅ are smooth and P lr-extends to P ,′ then a(P ) ≥ a(P ′ ).

Proof: Pick any lr-generic string x for P . Since P lr-extends to P ′ , there exists y such that xy ∈ P ′ . Then
a(P ) = a(x) = |Qlr (x)| ≥ |Qlr (xy)| ≥ a(P ′ ), where the two equalities are by the definitions of a(P )
and a(x) (and since x is lr-generic) and the two inequalities hold by Lemmas 2 and 8, respectively.
   Symmetrically to the above, we can also define the rl-size b(y) := |Qrl (y)| of a string y; prove that it
is common for all rl-generic strings of a smooth P ; define it as the rl-size b(P ) of P ; show that it lower
bounds the rl-size of all x ∈ P and is met iff x is rl-generic; and prove b(P ′ ) ≤ b(P ) when a smooth P
rl-extends to a smooth P ′ . We then also prove the following.
Lemma 10. If P, P ′ ̸= ∅ are smooth and P has suffix of choice into P ′ , then both a(P ) ≥ a(P ′ ) and
b(P ) ≥ b(P ′ ).

U +1 ≤ t ≤ N −1 (stage 2); or on all cells, if t = N (stage 3).

Lemma 17. For all t = 1, . . . , N , property Pt−1 has suffix of choice into Pt .

Proof: Pick any v ∈ Pt−1 . Let u be arbitrary in Pt . Pick any x ∈ Pt−1 ∪ Pt . We compute:

                          C(xuv) = C(x)C(u)C(v) = C(x)Ct Ct−1 = C(x)Ct ,

where the last equality uses Lemma 14. Now, since x is in Pt−1 or Pt , the connectivity C(x)Ct is either
Ct−1 Ct or Ct2 ; both of which equal Ct , again by Lemma 14. Therefore, xuv ∈ Pt .

Theorem 1. Let h ≥ 1. Let M be any 2dfa that solves owlh . Then M has at least 14 h(h + 1) states.

5    Conclusion
We proved that every 2dfa solving owlh needs Ω(h2 ) states and thus, in general, the conversion of 1nfas
to 2dfas incurs a quadratic blow-up in the number of states. Although this simply matches the already
known lower bound by Chrobak [4, 27] and only slightly exceeds that of [15], it results from an analysis
of 2dfa computations which are much richer than the ones realized on unary or on 3-long inputs.
   We conjecture that the sequence of smooth properties constructed in Sect. 4 is of maximum possible
length. Namely that, in every sequence ∅ ̸= P0 , P1 , . . . , Pm ⊆ Σh∗ , if every two successive properties
Pt−1 , Pt satisfy the conditions of our Main Lemma (Lemma 11), then m ≤ h+1
                                                                                    
                                                                                 2 .
   If this is indeed the case, then our lemma in its present form seems not to be helpful in discovering
super-quadratic lower bounds for 2dfas against owl. Still, we are hopeful that our Main Lemma might
help bound the size of 2dfas in other settings. The lack of general lower-bounding tools is a well-known
major deficiency in the study of 2dfa computations. In this work, we deliberately tried to extract from our
syllogisms a lemma that is general enough to potentially serve as such a tool.
