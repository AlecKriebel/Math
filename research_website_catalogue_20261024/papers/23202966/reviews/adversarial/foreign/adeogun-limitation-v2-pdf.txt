                                                Abstract. A recent result by the present authors established a quadratic
                                                lower bound, in the worst case, for the increase in the number of states
                                                when a one-way nondeterministic finite automaton is converted to a two-
                                                way deterministic finite automaton. Although this simply matched a well-
                                                known pre-existing quadratic lower bound by Chrobak, it used a distinct
                                                proof method. We show that, much like Chrobak’s, this new method is
                                                also unable to deliver any lower bound strictly greater than quadratic.

                                                Keywords: Sakoda-Sipser conjecture, descriptional complexity


                                         1    Introduction

                                         An important open question in automata and complexity theory concerns the
                                         conversion of one-way nondeterministic finite automata (1nfas) to two-way de-
                                         terministic finite automata (2dfas). By an old result [1], this conversion is always
                                         possible, as we can convert the given 1nfa N to an equivalent one-way deter-
                                         ministic finite automaton M , i.e., a 2dfa that does not use its ability to reverse
                                         its input head. However, this method is known to incur an exponential cost in
                                         the number of states: if N has s states, then M may well have (and, for some N ,
                                         necessarily has) 2s states [2]. A natural question then is whether M can decrease
                                         its number of states to only a polynomial in s, if it uses its bidirectionality.
                                             First posed by Seiferas [3], this question is still open. Sakoda and Sipser [4] re-
                                         vealed its importance by viewing it as a special case of the respective question for
                                         the more general conversion of two-way nondeterministic finite automata (2nfas)
                                         to 2dfas; and, in turn, by viewing this latter conversion as a miniature variant
                                         of our big open questions on the power of nondeterminism (e.g., P vs. NP). In
                                         the spirit of this analogy, they also introduced a decision problem, now called
                                         one-way liveness (owl), that is “complete” for the conversion, in that: for all h,
                                         a 2dfa with s(h) states can decide owlh (i.e., owl on instances of “height” h) iff
                                         every h-state 1nfa has an equivalent 2dfa with s(h) states. Hence, the original
                                         question became: Is owlh decidable by a 2dfa with poly(h) states?
                                             Sakoda and Sipser conjectured a negative answer, namely that 2dfas need
                                         super-polynomially many states to decide owlh ; and thus (equivalently) to sim-
                                         ulate h-state 1nfas (and thus to simulate h-state 2nfas—this latter, weaker
                                         claim being what is now known as the “Sakoda-Sipser conjecture”). Their first
                                         attempts at confirming this conjecture were followed by many others, which

    In 1986, Chrobak [7] studied the conversion in the special case where the
given 1nfa is unary (i.e., over a single-letter input alphabet). For every h, he

note, he also proved that every h-state unary 1nfa has an equivalent 2dfa with
O(h2 ) states, so his approach was unable to deliver any greater lower bound.
    In 2018, Kapoutsis [9] studied owlh in the special case of instances of
length 3. He proved that every 2dfa deciding such instances needs Ω(h2 / log h)
states. On a pessimistic note, he also proved that O(h2 / log h) states are enough,
so his approach was also unable to deliver any greater lower bound.

bound was achieved even on severely restricted instances of owl (strings over
only one symbol; or of length 3). However, as mentioned above, the 2dfa assumed
to be working on these restricted instances was completely unrestricted.

Properties. A property (of the strings) over an alphabet Σ is any P ⊆ Σ ∗ . We
call P smooth, if every two strings in it have an infix that keeps their concate-
nation in the property: (∀x,z ∈ P )(∃y)(xyz ∈ P ). Properties P, P ′ are separated

    We say that P lr-extends to P ′ if every string in it can be lr-extended
into P ′ : (∀x ∈ P )(∃y)(xy ∈ P ′ ); that P has a suffix into P ′ , if a single suffix y
can push into P ′ every x from P or P ′ : (∃y)(∀x ∈ P ∪ P ′ )(xy ∈ P ′ ); and that
P has suffix of choice into P ′ , if that suffix can be chosen to end in any way
v ∈ P that we want: (∀v ∈ P )(∃u)(∀x ∈ P ∪ P ′ )(xuv ∈ P ′ ).

One-way liveness. Let h ≥ 1. The alphabet Σh := P([h]×[h]) consists of every
two-column directed graph with h nodes per column and only rightward arrows
(Fig. 1). An n-long string z ∈ Σh∗ is naturally viewed as a graph of n+1 columns,
indexed from 0 to n, where edges connect successive columns and, for simplicity,
are undirected. If there exists an n-long path from some node of column 0 to
some node of column n, then z is live; otherwise, it is dead. Checking that a given
z ∈ Σh∗ is live is the one-way liveness problem for height h, denoted by owlh .
    The problem helps us study how easily 2dfas can simulate 1nfas [4]. For-
mally, the family owl = (owlh )h≥1 is complete, under homomorphic reduc-
tions, for the class 1N of all language families L = (Lh )h≥1 that are recognized
4       K. Adeogun, C. A. Kapoutsis

by 2dfa families M = (Mh )h≥1 where the number of states grows polynomially
in h. Intuitively, the strings of Σh∗ are all computations of all h-state 1nfas. So,
if a 2dfa solves owlh with ≤ s(h) states, then every h-state 1nfa can be simu-
lated by a 2dfa with ≤ s(h) states; and, conversely, if every 2dfa for owlh needs
≥ s(h) states, then some h-state 1nfa needs ≥ s(h) states in each 2dfa simulator.

4.1   Regularity
A connectivity C ′ is regular on a connectivity C if there is connectivity J such
that C ′ = CJC. Easily, this implies a surjective homomorphism inside V (C).
Lemma 16. If C ′ is regular on C then there exists a surjective homomorphism
α : V (C) → V (C ′ ); and V (C ′ ) ⊆ V (C).

C ′ = C(JC) says that α := αC,JC is a surjective homomorphism from V (C)
onto V (C ′ ). The same lemma for C ′ = (CJ)C also says that V (C ′ ) ⊆ V (C). □

5    Properties

The connectivity C(z) of an string z ∈ Σh∗ is the connectivity C ∈ Bh×h that
records all connections between the two outer columns of z, in that cij = 1
iff z has a one-way path from node i of the leftmost column to node j of the
rightmost column (Fig. 1(R)). Easily, z is live iff its connectivity is non-zero:
z ∈ owlh ⇐⇒ C(z) ̸= 0; and concatenation of strings corresponds to Boolean
multiplication of connectivities, namely C(xy) = C(x)C(y).
     Given C ∈ Bh×h , we let P (C) := {z ∈ Σh∗ | C(z) = C} be all strings
with connectivity C. A property P ⊆ Σh∗ is a connectivity property if P =
P (C) for some C. Easily, connectivity properties are non-empty: P (C) ̸= ∅, for
all C. Moreover, when properties are connectivity properties, some qualities and
relations of properties correspond to qualities and relations of connectivities.

Lemma 19. Let C, C ′ ∈ Bh×h be any two connectivities. Let P := P (C) and
P ′ := P (C ′ ) be the corresponding connectivity properties. Then:(17)

(a) P is smooth iff C is regular.
(b) P has suffix of choice into P ′ iff C ′ is forward-regular on C.
(c) P, P ′ are separated by owlh iff C ̸= C ′ .

6    Conclusion
In [5], we proved a lower bound of 21 N for any 2dfa for owlh , where N = h+1
                                                                                        
                                                                                      2 .
At the heart of our proof lied a sequence of connectivity properties P0 , P1 , . . . , PN
meeting certain specifications. It was clear that a longer such sequence would
imply a greater lower bound. So, we asked: Could a better design produce a longer
sequence of connectivity properties, and thus a greater lower bound? Here, we
answered negatively, i.e., that the sequence designed in [5] was longest (Th. 2).
    Importantly, our proof would have also worked with arbitrary properties;
we used connectivity ones only because our design took us there. So, one may
wonder: What if we allow arbitrary properties? Is there a longer sequence then?
The answer is again negative (Th. 1), but requires a longer presentation, which
we are reserving for the full version of this report.
