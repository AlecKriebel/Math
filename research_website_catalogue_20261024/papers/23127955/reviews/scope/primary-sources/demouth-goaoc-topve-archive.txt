                                              Abstract
    We analyse the topological changes occuring in the apparent contour of a collection of disjoint
convex sets from a moving viewpoint, and relate them to the classical visual event surfaces, swept
by tritangent rays and limiting bitangent rays. Specifically, we show that all topological changes
occur as the viewpoint crosses these surfaces or the plane containing some flat object, and that for
a subset of these surfaces, such crossings do trigger a topological change in the apparent contour.
We also show that these so-called visual event surfaces are likely to be two-dimensional, in the
sense that their closure has empty interior. Last, we sketch how this characterization of visual event
surfaces led to improved algorithms for the computation of the boundaries of soft shadows induced
by area-light sources.
    Convexity and disjointedness allow elementary proofs and getting rid of the classical distinction
between the smooth and polyhedral realms: our results hold for scenes mixing sets that are flat,
smooth, piecewise smooth, have infinitely many singularities, are in arbitrarily degenerate position...

1      Introduction
The idea that the way a moving observer sees his environment changes qualitatively only as he reaches
certain critical positions underlies many approaches to visibility questions in Computer Vision, Com-
puter Graphics and Computational Geometry. Several data structures were proposed to encode these
critical positions, for instance the aspect graph [17] or the visibility skeleton [11, 13], and many algo-
rithms exploit them directly [2, 3, 7] or indirectly [14]. The locus of critical points, usually referred to
as visual event surfaces, depends on the precise meaning attached to "view" and "qualitative change".
Different definitions capture different phenomena and lend themselves more or less easily to analysis, so
it is not surprising that various formalizations developped over the last decades. The two main settings
deal with respectively smooth manifolds and polyhedra:
      • For smooth manifolds, the view is defined as a map from the object observed onto some screen and
        two views are declared equivalent if the functions are equal up to smooth changes of coordinates
        (both in the screen and on the object) [18]. When the object is a sufficiently generic smooth
        manifold, the visual event surfaces can be described, using techniques from singularity theory,
        as surfaces generated by sets of lines with prescribed contacts with the manifold [1, 22]. This
        approach extends, albeit with restrictions, to piecewise smooth objects [24].
      • For polyhedral objects, the view is defined as the abstract graph obtained from the projection of
        visible portions of the polyhedra’s edges onto the screen, each element being labelled with the
        geometric elements it is the projection of, and two views are declared equivalent if the graphs
        are isomorphic [15]. Simple combinatorial arguments yield a characterization of the visual event
        surfaces [15]; specifically, they are generated by lines either through a vertex and an edge (EV) or
        through three edges (EEE).
    In application areas, the objects at hands are often polyhedral approximations of “natural objects”.
This raises the question whether the visual event surfaces of an object can be estimated using approxima-
tions of that object. In the above settings, the answer is negative: the (discrete) visual event surfaces of a
polyhedral approximation of a smooth manifold do not approximate the (smooth) visual event surfaces
of that manifold. For instance, a sphere induces no visual event surfaces whereas any convex polyhe-
dron induces one visual event surface of type EV for each of its faces. This shows that although these
two lines of work may appear as the smooth and discrete counterparts of the same principle, they are
not. What choice of “view” and “view-equivalence” could be used to bring both settings under the same
umbrella, and what visual event surfaces would these notions define? This is the question we discuss in
this paper.

1.1     Problem statement and results
We study the visual event surfaces that correspond to topological changes in the apparent contour of a
finite collection of (pairwise) disjoint compact convex sets in R3 . The apparent contour from a point p
is the set of directions of rays originating from p whose first intersection with the scene is tangential –
where a ray is tangent to a convex set if it is contained in one of its support planes. An apparent contour
is thus a subset of the space S2 of directions (see Figure 1); we declare the apparent contours from two
points equivalent if they are ambiant-homeomorphic, that is one can be mapped to the other through a
homeomorphism of S2 into itself. Our results are two-fold:
      • We give a near-characterization of the “visual event surfaces” of a collection of pairwise disjoint
        compact convex sets with respect to changes in the topology of the apparent contour (Theorem 3).
        Specifically, we show that the set of points where such visual events occur are the origin of tritan-
        gent rays and limiting bitangent rays, with the possible exclusion of origins of limiting bitangent
        rays corresponding to tangential crossings of the apparent contours of two objects (see Figure 8).

      • We prove that in this context, the “visual event surfaces” are akin to surfaces, in the sense that they

We also present (in Section 6) an application of these results to the problem of computing the boundaries
of regions of umbra, penumbra and full light in the presence of extended light sources [9].

1.2     Significance and relation to previous work
Previous attempts at unifying the smooth and polyhedral approaches of visual events focused on extend-
ing the smooth setting to piecewise smooth manifolds. Positive results were obtained in certain cases,
e.g. unions of smooth surfaces intersecting pairwise transversally and having isolated triple points [24],
but they seem difficult to generalize [24]. This approach is also unlikely to solve the approximation
problem mentionned earlier. Indeed, requiring that the change of coordinates between the projection
functions be smooth implies, if the object is a polyhedron, that the structure of its visible edges is pre-
served, leading to the same problem as the existing polyhedral setting, whereas allowing nonsmooth
changes of coordinates makes the whole approach less amenable to singularity theory techniques.
    Our definitions of view and view-equivalence lead, for smooth disjoint convex sets, to the same
visual event surfaces as obtained in the smooth setting. This is not surprising as the apparent contour
coincide with the set of critical values of the projection function, a structure that is preserved under
smooth changes of coordinates. It should be noted that most results for the smooth setting hold under
certain restriction, namely that the manifold be “projection generic”, which de facto excludes most
surfaces one usually compute with, which then had to be treated separately (e.g. algebraic objects [21]
or solids of revolution [19]). By making no assumption on the objects’ description complexity and
allowing sets in arbitrary position, we treat all scenes of convex sets in a single stroke.
    In the polyhedral setting, the differences between our and the classical approach are twofold. First,
the apparent contour of a polyhedral object consists of the projection of the visible parts of silhouette
edges, and thus ignores all internal edges. Second, by allowing nonsmooth change of coordinates, we
ignore visual events that are, in some sense, related to the meshing of the polyhedra, e.g. changes in
the number of silhouette edges. As a result, our definitions yield a subset of the visual event surfaces
obtained by the graph-isomorphism approach: essentially the EV surfaces defined by an edge and a
vertex that span a plane tangent to their respective objects, and the EEE surfaces.
    Finally, our approach uses only simple tools. In particular, we give an elementary proof of the
following local-global equivalence: “the apparent contour changes, up to ambiant homeomorphism if
and only if it changes in a neighborhood of some direction”. This equivalence is often invoqued in the
classical litterature; in the generic smooth setting, it follows from deep classical results due to Mather
and we are not aware of any published proof accessible to the nonspecialist.

2    Preliminaries
The lemmas of this section are proven in Appendix A and B. In the remaining of this paper we let C be a
finite collection of (pairwise) disjoint compact convex sets in R3 . We identify vectors in R3 with points
in S2 , the unit sphere centered at the origin, and write them in boldface. We denote by B(p, r) the open
ball of radius r centered in p, and by B(u, r) the directions, in S2 , making angle less than r with u.

Lemma 1. If A and B are two subsets of R3 such that A is closed and both have empty interiors, then
A ∪ B has empty interior.

     We denote the space of rays in R3 by R3 × S2 , and use (p, u) to denote the ray with origin p and
direction u. We say that a ray that intersects X ∈ C without being tangent to it meets X transversally,
and that a ray sees X ∈ C if that ray does not intersect transversally any member of C in-between its
origin and its first intersection with X. A ray that sees tangentially at least 2 (resp. 3) objects is a
bitangent (resp. tritangent) ray; if it is contained in a common support plane of these objects we say that
it is limiting. If two rays with the same origin intersect two convex sets in different orders, then these
two sets must intersect. As a consequence, the system of rays originating from a given point p induces a
partial ordering on C, which we denote by ≺p . Two such orderings are compatible if they are consistent
for all pairs of elements that are comparable for both orderings.

Lemma 2. In a neighborhood of a point p all points induce orderings compatible with ≺p .

    We use hXi to denote the affine space spanned by X, or its affine hull, so hp, qi is the line through
p and q and hp, u, vi is the plane spanned by p and vectors u and v. The dimension of a convex set
is defined as the dimension of its affine hull. In the remaining of this paper, all convex sets considered
are of dimension at least 2. Let ∆ be the apparent contour of C from a point p. There are two types of
points in ∆: regular points, in the neighborhood of which ∆ is rectifiable (i.e. homeomorphic to R), and
branching points. If a branching point u can be isolated from other branching points of ∆, its degree is
defined as the number of connected components of ∆ ∩ U for sufficiently small neighborhood U of u.
A face of the apparent contour is the closure of a connected component of S2 \ ∆.

Lemma 3. Let X be a convex set with dim(X) ≥ 2 and p a point; assume that p is not in the affine hull
of X if dim(X) = 2. There exists a neighborhood N of p and a continuous map φ : N × S1 → S2 such
that for any q ∈ N , φq is an homeomorphism between S1 and the apparent contour of X from q.

     We call a point degenerate (with respect to C) if it lies in the affine hull of a flat object it can see or if
it is the origin of a limiting bitangent or tritangent ray. A degenerate point that is the origin of a limiting
bitangent ray whose direction is a branching point point of degree three of the apparent contour of C is
called highly degenerate; a degenerate point that is not highly degenerate is mildly degenerate.

Lemma 4. The set of points degenerate with respect to C is closed.

We often use the term viewpoint to denote a point from which we consider the apparent contour of C.


3    Structure and local stability of the apparent contour
The apparent contour of C from an arbitrary point can have infinitely many branching points, branching
points of arbitrary degree and non-manifold faces (see Figure 2). The situation is much simpler when
the viewpoint is required to be nondegenerate.

Lemma 5. The apparent contour of C from a nondegenerate viewpoint contains finitely many branching
points, each of degree 3, and its faces are 2-dimensional manifolds with boundaries.

Before we prove this statement, we formalize, for apparent contours, the following observation: as the
intersection points of a convex curve with a moving line merge, the line supports the curve.

Lemma 6. Let X be a convex set of dimension at least 2, (pi )i∈N a sequence of points and (ni )i∈N a
sequence of directions. Assume that (pi ) converges to a point p, which does not belong to the affine hull
of X if X is 2-dimensional, that ni converges to a limit n and that the great circle ni ⊥ cuts the apparent
contour of X from pi into two distinct points ui and vi that converge to one and same direction u. Then,
the great circle n⊥ is support to the apparent contour of X from p in u.

As an immediate consequence (proven in Appendix C), we get:

Corollary 7. If there are infinitely many rays originating from p and tangent to two compact convex sets
X and Y then p is the source of a limiting bitangent ray to X and Y .

We can now return to the proof that apparent contours from nondegenerate points have a simple structure.

Proof of Lemma 5. Let p be a nondegenerate viewpoint and ∆ the apparent contour of C from p. Assume
that ∆ contains infinitely many branching points. Each branching point is the direction of a ray from
p seeing tangentially at least two objects in C. Since C is finite, there is an infinite set B of branching
points corresponding to rays seeing tangentially the same pair of objects, and by Corollary 7, there is
a limiting bitangent ray to this pair that originates from p. This contradicts the assumption that p was
nondegenerate, and so ∆ has only finitely many branching points.
    Let u ∈ ∆ be a branching point and X denote the set of objects intersected by the ray (p, u).
Since ∆ has only finitely many branching points other than u, we can isolate u from them by some
neighborhood N ; since the objects in C are compact, making N small enough ensures that any ray from
p with direction in N only intersects objects in X. Now observe that any arc in ∆ ∩ N incident to u is
contained in the transparent contour of an object that (p, u) sees tangentially. By genericity of p, there
are exactly two such objects, say X1 and X2 , u is a regular point of their transparent contour ∂Xi , and
the ∂Xi meet nontangentially in u. Since (p, u) sees the Xi tangentially, and no other object, they are
minimal for ≺p in X: X1 ≺p X2 ≺p Y for any Y ∈ X \ {X1 , X2 }. It follows that precisely two arcs
of ∂X1 and one arc of ∂X2 are incident to u in the apparent contour from p. Thus, u has degree three.
    Let F be a face of ∆ and x a point of F. Either x is interior to F, in which case F is homeomorphic
to a plane locally around x, or x ∈ ∆. If x is a regular point of ∆ then, locally around x, ∆ is a convex
curve and F is homeomorphic to a halfplane. If x is a branching point of ∆ then it has degree 3 and cuts
the sphere into three sectors locally around x and, by definition of F, at least one sector is contained in
F. If all three sectors are contained in F, then the face is homeomorphic to a plane locally around x; if
two or fewer sectors are in F then the face is homeomorphic to a halfplane locally around x. Altogether,


                                                    5
F is homeomorphic to either a plane or a halfplane in a neighborhood of any of its point, and is therefore
a 2-dimensional manifold, possibly with boundaries.

The next lemma, proven in Appendix D, will be instrumental in the next sections.

Lemma 8. Let p be a nondegenerate viewpoint and u1 , . . . , uk the branching points of the apparent
contour of C from p. There exist neighborhoods N of p and U1 , . . . , Uk of u1 , . . . , uk such that (i) the
Ui are pairwise disjoint and (ii) the apparent contour of C from any point q ∈ N has a unique branching
point in each Ui and no other.


4    Geometry of the set of degenerate points
In this section we prove that the locus of degenerate points is nowhere dense, that is, its closure has
empty interior. We handle each type of degenerate point separately, starting with the origins of limiting
bitangent rays.

Lemma 9. The set of origins of limiting bitangent rays to two disjoint compact convex sets is closed and
has empty interior.

Given the space limitation, the proof is given in Appendix E. To analyze the case of origins of tritangent
rays, we introduce some terminology. An oriented plane is a plane together with an orthogonal frame,
that is, a point and two orthogonal vectors. A ray (p, u) is tangent to a compact convex set X from above
in the plane hp, u, ni if hp, u, ni does not support X and if, in that plane, n points in the open halfplane
bounded by the line hp, ui that is disjoint from X.

Lemma 10. Let X be a compact convex set and (p, u) a ray tangent to X from above in a plane
hp, u, ni. There exists  > 0 such that for any ray (q, v) ∈ B(p, ) × B(u, ) tangent to X and any
vector m ∈ B(n, ) normal to v, (q, v) is tangent to X from above in the plane hp, v, mi.

We proceed with a local analysis of tritangent rays that are not limiting bitangent.

Lemma 11. For any sufficiently small neighborhood U of u, there exists an open ball B arbitrarily
close to p such that no ray in B × U is tritangent to the same objects as (p, u).

support planes to, respectively, X, Y and Z that contain (p, u). Since (p, u) is not a limiting bitangent

limiting bitangent ray. For each sector, we mark the object minimal with respect to ≺p that intersects
that sector; there are, up to symmetries, four distinct configurations (see Figure 3).

Lemma 12. The set of origins of rays tangent to three disjoint compact convex sets is nowhere dense.
Proof. Let X, Y and Z be three disjoint compact convex sets, T the set of origin of their tritangent rays,
and T ∗ the set origin of their tritangent rays that are not limiting bitangents to any two of them.
    We first argue that T ∗ has empty interior. Assume by contradiction that T ∗ contains some open ball
B. A point in B is the origin of only finitely many rays tangent to two objects, as otherwise it would
be the origin of a limiting bitangent ray, by Corollary 7. Moreover, the local stability of intersections
between the silhouettes (Lemma 8), ensures that we can choose B so that the number of rays tangent to
X and Y is the same from any point in B. We pick a point p1 in B, let u1 , . . . , uk be the directions of
rays from p1 tangent to X and Y and let Vi be pairwise disjoint neighborhoods of the vi . By Lemma 8,
there exists a neighborhood P such that from any point of P there is a unique direction of ray tangent
to X and Y in each Vi , and no other. By Lemma 11, up to reducing V1 there exists an open ball B1
contained in B such that no ray in B1 × V1 is tangent to X, Y and Z. We iterate this process: picking
a point pi in Bi and letting Bi+1 be a ball in Bi such that no ray in Bi+1 × Vi+1 is tangent toSX, Y and
Z. Since Bk is contained in Bi for all i ≤ k, there is no ray tangent to X, Y and Z in Bk × 1≤i≤k Vi ;
thus, there is no tritangent ray from Bk , which, since Bk ⊂ B, contradicts the assumption that B ⊂ T ∗ .
    Let B denote the set of origin of limiting bitangent rays to two among the three sets. Since B is
closed and has empty interior (Lemmas 9 and 1), Lemma 1 implies that B ∪ T ∗ has empty interior. Since
T ⊂ B ∪ T ∗ , it follows that T also has empty interior. Since T is closed, the statement follows.

We can now state the main result of this section:
Theorem 1. The set of points degenerate with respect to a finite collection of pairwise disjoint compact
convex sets is nowhere dense.
Proof. The affine hull of a convex set of dimension at most 2 is a plane, which is closed and has empty
interior. Together with Lemmas 9 and 12, this implies that if C is finite then its locus of degenerate
points is the union of a finite collection of closed sets with empty interiors, which is also closed and, by
Lemma 1, has empty interior; it is thus nowhere dense.

In some cases, this implies that the visual event surfaces are indeed surfaces in the classical sense:
Theorem 2. If C consists of disjoint compact convex semi-algebraic sets of finite description complexity,
then the set of degenerate points with respect to C is contained in a finite union of algebraic 2-manifolds.
Proof. The conditions that a ray be tangent to a given set, does or does not intersect a given set and
has a common tangent plane with two given sets all become semi-algebraic in R3 × S2 if the sets are
semi-algebraic object in R3 (given an adequate representation of S2 ). The set of points degenerate with
respect to C is thus also semi-algebraic. Since it has empty interior by Theorem 1, it is contained in the
union of finitely many algebraic hypersurfaces, which implies the statement.

5     Near-characterization of visual event surfaces
A point p is topologically stable (with respect to C) if the apparent contour of C from any point in a
neighborhood of p is ambiant-homeomorphic to the apparent contour of C from p, and topologically
unstable otherwise. The main result of this section is the following theorem.
Theorem 3. Any nondegenerate point is topologically stable and any degenerate but not highly degen-
erate point is topologically unstable.
    If we denote by D and H, respectively, the sets of points that are degenerate and highly degenerate
with respect to C, Theorem 3 asserts that the visual event surfaces that correspond to topological changes
in the apparent contour of C are sandwiched between D and D \ H. We first establish the topological
stability of nondegenerate viewpoints (Lemma 14) and then prove the topological instability of mildly
degenerate viewpoints (Lemma 15). Note that as a a highly degenerate viewpoint can be topologically
stable (Figure 8-left) or unstable (Figure 8-right), Theorem 3 is optimal.

5.1   Nondegenerate viewpoints
Let p be a nondegenerate point. By Lemma 8, there is a natural bijection between the branching points
of the apparent contours from p and any point q sufficiently close to p. Using Lemma 3, we extend this
bijection into a a (3D) isotopy of the whole apparent contour near p.
Lemma 13. Let p be a nondegenerate viewpoint and let ∆p denote the apparent contour from p. For
any sufficiently small neighborhood P of p, there exists a continuous map φ : P × ∆p → S2 , such that
for any q ∈ P the map φ(q, ·) is an homeomorphism between the apparent contours from p and q.
Proof. By Lemma 5, the apparent contour from p contains a finite number of branching points. Let
u1 , . . . , uk be those branching points. For each ui , consider a small enough neighborhood Ui isolating
ui from the other branching points. By Lemma 8, for any i, there exists a neighborhood Pi of p such
that the apparent contour ∆q from any q ∈ Pi contains a unique branching point in Ui . The intersection
P of the Pi is a neighborhood of p. The map ϕ restrained to P × {u1 , . . . , uk } is the bijection which,
for any pair (q, i), sends the branching point in ∆p ∩ Ui to the branching point in ∆q ∩ Ui .
     We show how to extand ϕ to arcs. Consider the arcs of ∆p limited by two branching points. Let α
be the arc of extreme points ui and uj and let Ui and Uj be, respectively, the neighborhoods of these two

                                                    9
branching points. For any point q ∈ P , the map ϕ sends ui and vi to ϕ(q, ui ) ∈ Ui and ϕ(q, uj ) ∈ Uj .
Now, possibly restreigning each of the Uk , we can suppose that the arc α intersects the boundary of none
of the Uk distinct from Ui and Uj . It follows, from the continuity of the transparent contour (Lemma 3)
and possibly restreigning P , that we can ensure that α, when the viewpoint is moving in P , keep on
intersecting none of the boundaries of the Uk . Thus, the arc α links the branching points ϕ(q, ui ) and
ϕ(q, uj ) for any q ∈ P . The map ϕ can be extended continuously to simple arcs of the apparent
contour. Finally, for closed contours (containing no branching point) the extension of ϕ follows from
the continuous change in the transparent contours.
The next Lemma (proven in Appendix G) shows that in this isotopy, every homeomorphism between
two contours can be extended into an ambiant homeomorphism.
Lemma 14. Let p be a nondegenerate viewpoint. There exists a neighborhood P of p such that for any
q ∈ P there exists an homeomorphism of S2 into itself that maps the apparent contour from p to the
apparent contour from q.
As a consequence, we get the first implication of Theorem 3: any point that is nondegenerate with
respect to a finite collection of compact convex sets is topologically stable.

5.2   Mildly degenerate viewpoints
The next lemma, together with Lemma 14, implies Theorem 3.
Lemma 15. The apparent contour from any mildly degenerate viewpoint is topologically unstable.
Proof. Let p be a mildly degenerate viewpoint and ∆ the apparent contour of C from p. If p is topologi-
cally stable then for any point q sufficiently close to p, there is a bijection between the branching points
of ∆p and those of the apparent contours from q that respect the degrees (when defined). It then follows
from Theorem 1 and Lemma 5 that if ∆p contains infinitely many branching points or a branching point
of degree other than 3 then p is topologically unstable. Since p is only mildly degenerate, we can thus
assume that ∆p contains only finitely many branching points, each of degree 3 and corresponding to a
ray tangent to 2 or more object and not limiting bitangent to any two of them.

6    Application to shadow boundaries computation
In the last decades, much efforts was devoted in Computer Graphics to improve the accuracy of shadow
rendering due to their significance for the understanding of shape from images [23]. Examples include
the developpement of the discontinuity mesh [16, 20] to improve radiosity methods [6, 25] or the use of
shadow volumes [7], in particular for real-time rendering [2, 3]. All these methods compute the shadows
boundaries geometrically, that is, a priori to the illumination simulation. While these boundaries can be
easily retrieved for point light sources, they proved elusive in the case of extended light sources. Since a
shadow boundary is the locus of point on an object from which the visibility of the light source changes
(from visible to partially visible, to invisible), these curves are the trace of certain visual event surfaces.
So far, the EEE and EV visual event surfaces of Gigus and Malik [15, 26] has been the basis for most
computer-graphic methods [2, 3, 11, 12, 10].




Figure 10: Left: a scene of disjoint convex polyhedra, two of which are (area) light sources. Right:
boundaries between full light and penumbra (green) and penumbra and umbra (red) computed using the
topological changes of the apparent contour.

    In a companion paper [9], we show that the topological changes of the apparent contour do capture
the shadow boundaries cast by area light sources. We also give an exact characterization of the visual
event surfaces that induce the boundaries between full-light and penumbra and between penumbra and
umbra, respectively. We tested this approach on scenes of disjoint convex polyhedra (Figure 10). In our
experiments, the exact shadow boundaries that we compute are 4 to 10 time smaller than the superset
usually obtained by the visual events of Gigus and Malik [15, 26]. As far as we know, this is the first
method that computes shadow boundaries for direct extended light sources exactly.


7    Concluding remarks

    An obvious question is whether our work extends to a more general setting. Promising results in that

events corresponding to unilocal rays, that is, rays that intersect a single component of visible portion of
the scene. We also plan to explore how the visual event surfaces corresponding to topological changes
of the apparent contour behave with respect to approximation.

Lemma 1. If A and B are two subsets of R3 such that A is closed and both have empty interiors, then
A ∪ B has empty interior.

Proof. Assume that A ∪ B contains an open ball B(p, r) with r > 0. Since A has empty interior, there
is a point q ∈ B(p, r) that is not in A. Since A is closed, the distance δ from q to A is positive, and there
exists ρ > 0 such that the ball B(q, ρ) is both contained in B(p, r) and disjoint from A. It follows that
B(q, ρ) is contained in B, which contradicts the assumption that B has empty interior. It follows that
A ∪ B contains no open ball, and thus has empty interior.

Lemma 2. In a neighborhood of a point p all points induce orderings compatible with ≺p .

Proof. Assume that there is a ray from a point p that intersects set X before set Y . Since X and Y
are convex and compact, they can be strictly separated by some plane ΠXY , in the sense that they are
contained in distinct open halfspaces defined by ΠXY . Since there is a ray from p that intersects X
before Y , p lies on X’s side of ΠXY , and admits a neighborhood NXY that lies in the same halfspace;
it follows that any ray originating from NXY that intersects both X and Y meets X before Y . The
intersection of the NXY for all pairs comparable for ≺p yields a neighborhood of p in which all orderings
are compatible with ≺p .

Lemma 4. The set of points that are degenerate with respect to C is closed.

Furthermore, the continuity of q 7→ uq implies the continuity of ψ −1 . Thus, the map ψ is a homeomor-

parted in at most a countable number of intervals. The union of lines in Dκ (bitangent lines intersecting
κ in a regular point) is the union of at most a countable number of sets with empty interiors. It has, thus,
an empty interior.
