                       to Byrnes’s diﬀerential-poset bound

                                               Abstract
         For every ordinary unweighted r-diﬀerential poset and n ≥ 2, we subtract an explicit non-
     negative term from Byrnes’s cumulative upper bound for the nth rank size. The term is the
     cycle rank of the preceding incidence subgraph supported on elements with a singleton succes-
     sor. Singleton witnesses transfer these cycles into the next incidence graph by replacing stars
     with trees having the same terminals. The correction can be positive and attained on standard
     reﬂected Young-preﬁx examples, and its coeﬃcient one is sharp. A singleton-successor condition
     gives the conjectured local recurrence; two such successors give a conditional equality charac-
     terization. The result answers the printed request for an improvement of Byrnes’s inequality in
     an incidence-dependent form. It does not prove the general local recurrence, improve the global
     Fibonacci envelope, or give a combinatorial proof of strict rank growth.


1    The improvement question and attribution
Majumder’s problem in Oberwolfach Report 23/2018, printed p. 1452 [3], asks whether Byrnes’s
bound

can be improved toward Stanley’s proposed local inequality

                                         pn ≤ rpn−1 + pn−2 .                                           (2)

The same contribution separately asks for a combinatorial strict-growth proof. Our claim concerns
the improvement request only. The correction uses incidences of the preceding ranks; no rank-
number-only restriction is imposed in the printed request.
    The operator framework, common-cover restrictions and reﬂection construction are classical
[1]. Byrnes proves (1) on the way to the global Fibonacci envelope and states (2) separately [2,
Chapter 5]. Incidence and hypergraph dimension sums already appear in [4, Remark 8]; the cycle
identities below repackage these counts. Cycle-space monotonicity and terminal-preserving tree
replacement are elementary graph facts. The substantive assertion proved here is the simultaneous
transfer of the singleton-supported cycle rank, not a new general graph method.

2    Incidence graphs and exact slack
An ordinary r-diﬀerential poset is a locally ﬁnite N-graded poset P with a unique minimum b 0, ﬁnite
ranks Pj , and ordinary unweighted up and down operators over Q satisfying DU − U D = rI, where
r is a positive integer. Write pj = |Pj |, and let d(x) and u(x) count the lower and upper covers of
x. Equivalently, the integer incidence counts satisfy

                  u(x) = d(x) + r,          | pred(x) ∩ pred(y)| = | succ(x) ∩ succ(y)|             (3)

for distinct elements in a common rank. We do not interpret this identity only modulo a charac-
teristic. The terminal equality to one in the OWR common-cover axiom is a typographical error:
zero common covers are allowed under the standard deﬁnition [1, 5].

Lemma 2.1. Distinct same-rank elements have at most one common lower cover and at most one
common upper cover. Every consecutive-rank incidence graph is connected.

Proof. If a pair has two common upper covers, (3) gives two common lower covers. This lower pair
has the original pair as common upper covers. Iterating down the ranks contradicts the unique
rank-zero element. The common lower count is equal to the upper count.
   Let Bj be the bipartite incidence graph on Pj−1 ⊔Pj . The graph B1 is a star with r leaves. If Bj is
connected, its projection on Pj , joining elements with a common predecessor, is connected (including
the one-vertex case). By (3) each such adjacency has a common successor, so all Pj vertices lie in
one component of Bj+1 . Every vertex of Pj+1 has a lower cover, proving the induction.

   For a ﬁnite graph H, retaining isolated vertices, deﬁne β(H) = |E(H)| − |V (H)| + c(H), where
c(H) is its component count. This is its cycle-space dimension over any ﬁxed ﬁeld; it is nonnegative
and monotone under subgraph inclusion. Put bj = β(Bj ) for j ≥ 1.

3    The supported-cycle theorem
Fix n ≥ 2 and set Z = Pn−2 , X = Pn−1 . Deﬁne

                           S = {z ∈ Z : some a ∈ X has pred(a) = {z}}.

Such an a is a singleton successor of z; it does not mean that z has only one successor. Let HS
retain all vertices of X, precisely S on the other side of Bn−1 , and all corresponding incidence edges.

Theorem 3.1. For every ordinary r-diﬀerential poset and every n ≥ 2,

                                     0 ≤ qn ≤ bn−1 ,        b n ≥ qn .                              (8)

Consequently

                                             X
                                             n−2
                                pn ≤ 1 + r         pk + (r − 1)pn−1 − qn ,                          (9)
                                             k=0
                                pn ≤ rpn−1 + pn−2 + bn−1 − qn .                                    (10)

The correction is determined by the two preceding ranks, before Pn is constructed, with no further
hypothesis on the poset.

Proof. The ﬁrst two inequalities follow from HS ⊆ Bn−1 . For each z ∈ S, choose az ∈ X with
predecessor set {z} and put Nz = succ(z) ⊆ X. For every y ∈ Nz \ {az }, the pair az , y has unique
common predecessor z, hence unique common successor t ∈ Pn . Every lower cover w of t lies in Nz :
if w ̸= az , its common successor with az forces a common predecessor, which must be z.
    Take every nonsingleton upper vertex t covering az , with all its incidence edges, and all terminals
Nz . Two diﬀerent such t cannot share another terminal, by Lemma 2.1. Each terminal other than
az occurs exactly once. Thus these stars, joined at az , form a tree Tz connecting precisely Nz . If
|Nz | = 1, take the one-vertex tree. A rank-n internal vertex cannot belong to both Tz and Tw for
z ̸= w: it would cover the witnesses az , aw , whose predecessor sets are disjoint, contrary to (3).
Diﬀerent trees can share only X terminals.
    Let K be their union together with every vertex of X, including isolates. Replace each star of
HS centered at z by Tz . If Tz has a internal vertices, it has |Nz | + a − 1 edges. The replacement
changes both vertex and edge counts by a − 1 and preserves the connections among terminals. In-
ternal vertices from diﬀerent replacements are disjoint, so components and cycle rank are preserved:
β(K) = β(HS ) = qn . Since K ⊆ Bn , cycle-space inclusion gives bn ≥ qn . Equations (5) and (4)
now prove the bounds.

4    When the local bound and equality follow
Corollary 4.1. If every z ∈ Pn−2 has a singleton successor in Pn−1 , then pn ≤ rpn−1 + pn−2 .
In particular this holds for every admissible next extension whenever rank n − 1 was formed by
reﬂection-extension.

Proof. Here S = Z, so qn = bn−1 . Reﬂection creating rank n − 1 adds r singleton successors of
every rank-(n − 2) element; see the construction in Section 5. The extension to rank n may be
arbitrary.

Theorem 4.2. Suppose n ≥ 3 and every z ∈ Pn−2 has at least two distinct singleton successors in
Pn−1 . Equality in the local bound holds if and only if the extension to rank n is reﬂection-extension,
up to relabeling that rank.

Proof. Choose one witness per z in the main proof. Now K is connected and contains all X
vertices. Each unused upper vertex t attaches by its complete star to X, adding d(t) − 1 to cycle
rank. Equality means bn = bn−1 = β(K), so every unused upper vertex is singleton.
    For a ﬁxed z, take distinct singleton successors a = az , b. Every nonsingleton upper vertex
covering b belongs to K and speciﬁcally to Tz : if it belonged to Tw , its common predecessor with
the witness aw would force w = z. Every internal vertex of Tz covers a. Let h be the unique
common successor of a, b. For each y ∈ Nz \ {b}, its common successor with b is nonsingleton, lies
in Tz , and covers a. Uniqueness for a, b forces it to be h. Hence h covers all Nz , and no second
nonsingleton internal vertex can share a pair of terminals.
    Since n ≥ 3, each z has a predecessor and u(z) ≥ r + 1 ≥ 2. There is therefore precisely one
nonsingleton vertex hz over each Nz . They are distinct by the witness argument. Each x ∈ X
belongs to exactly d(x) of these neighborhoods; the degree identity leaves exactly r singleton
successors over x. This is reﬂection-extension. Conversely reﬂection has |Z| + r|X| upper vertices
and gives equality.

  The following established lemma is [5, Proposition 3.4]; its short counting proof is included to
make the consequence self-contained.

Lemma 4.3 (Gaetz–Venkataramana). Every rank-one element has at least two singleton succes-
sors.

Proof. There are r rank-one elements, each with r + 1 successors. At a ﬁxed z, nonsingleton
successors use disjoint nonempty subsets of the other r − 1 rank-one elements; otherwise a pair
would have two common successors. There are at most r − 1 nonsingleton successors, leaving at
least two.

    Thus, for every r ≥ 1, p3 ≤ rp2 + r, with equality precisely for reﬂection from rank two. This
is a consequence of the transfer/equality theorem and the credited lemma, not a claim that the
lemma is new.

5    Standard examples giving strictness and sharpness
We recall Wagner reﬂection, as presented in [1, Proposition 6.1 and the following paragraph]. Given
a valid preﬁx through rank m ≥ 1, add one vertex over the entire successor set in Pm of each element
of Pm−1 , and add r distinct singleton successors over each element of Pm . Each current x gets
d(x) + r successors; a current pair gets one common new successor for each common predecessor.
Every new vertex has a predecessor and the rank is ﬁnite. Thus the preﬁx prolongs indeﬁnitely to
an ordinary diﬀerential poset. Its next rank has pm−1 + rpm elements. The constructions below
use this classical operation and classical Young preﬁxes, rather than new poset families.
    For r = 3, take rank one {a, b, c}. Put one rank-two element over each of {a, b}, {a, c}, {b, c} and
two singleton successors over each rank-one element. Each rank-one element has four successors
and every pair has one. This is the preﬁx of Y 3 through rank two, with ranks (1, 3, 9). Reﬂect to
rank three and continue reﬂecting. Then

               (p0 , p1 , p2 , p3 ) = (1, 3, 9, 30),     e2 = 12,      e3 = 39,   b2 = b3 = q3 = 1.

Byrnes’s rank-three bound is 31 and (9) gives the attained bound 30.
    For r = 1, use Young’s lattice Y through rank ﬁve and reﬂect twice, then continue reﬂecting.
Young’s lattice consists of integer partitions, ordered by addition of one box. Its ﬁrst six rank sizes
are (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7), so the extended ranks are

                                                (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 19).
           P
Here e6 = 5k=0 pk = 19 and p5 + p6 = 19, hence b6 = 1. Reﬂection at rank six gives a singleton
successor for each rank-ﬁve element, so q7 = b6 = 1; reﬂection at rank seven gives b7 = 1. Byrnes’s
bound 20 is reduced to the attained bound 19. The standard reﬂection argument proves inﬁnite
realization in both cases.
    In either example pn = Un − qn with qn = 1. Replacing the coeﬃcient one in (9) by any c > 1
would yield a false bound pn ≤ Un − cqn < pn . This proves coeﬃcient sharpness, not optimality of
every possible use of the preceding incidence data.

Boundary cases and the exact remaining gap
No b0 is needed: b1 = 0. At n = 2, Z = {b 0} and every rank-one element is a singleton successor, so
q2 = 0 and p2 ≤ r2 + 1. If also r = 1, the replacement tree has one terminal and no internal vertex.
For empty S, HS is the isolated vertex set X and qn = 0.
    Always Un − (rpn−1 + pn−2 ) = bn−1 , so the reﬁned right side lies between Byrnes’s and the
proposed local right sides. The unremoved allowance is exactly bn−1 − qn . It can be positive:
Young’s lattice at n = 6 has b5 = 1, q6 = 0, reﬁned bound 13, local bound 12, and actual p6 = 11.
The general recurrence still requires bn ≥ bn−1 . No combinatorial strict-growth argument follows
here; the standard strict-growth statement also has the exception r = n = 1, when p0 = p1 = 1.

6    Veriﬁcation, related work, and research status
The companion archive contains the complete LATEX source, all eight unchanged submitted artifacts,
the independently written standard-library exact checker, complete expected outputs, a portable
oﬄine reproduction runner, source/attribution records and content manifests. No third-party full
text, ledger data or private coordination is included.
    The checker reconstructs predecessor sets, veriﬁes every integer commutator, connectivity, exact
cycle identities and the actual witness-tree replacements. Its edge-clique partition enumeration gives
labeled preﬁx counts 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 7, 60 for r = 1 through rank seven, 1, 1, 4 for r = 2 through rank
three, and 1, 2, 288 for r = 3 through rank three. These are not isomorphism counts. It checks
reﬂected preﬁxes for 1 ≤ r ≤ 5 through rank ﬁve, Young’s lattice through rank twelve, and both
sharp examples. The 5764 witness selections use ﬁrst and last choices in every reﬂection-only run,
irrespective of product size. Enumerated-preﬁx, Young-lattice and separate sharp-example runs
exhaust products of at most 2000 choices and use ﬁrst/last above 2000. The proof establishes
arbitrary witness choice.
    Negative controls reject deletion of a top vertex and a false minimum degree, and refute subtract-
ing 2qn in the sharp examples. The relaxed layer sizes (1, 3, 3, 7) satisfy the two middle commutators
for r = 1 but violate the rank-zero commutator; they do not form a diﬀerential-poset preﬁx. Their
pair-block projection has a triangle and their bipartite incidence graph a six-cycle. Normal, -O,
and -OO runs agree byte for byte. These ﬁnite computations support implementation and example
correctness; the universal theorem and inﬁnite realization rest on the written proofs.
    Singleton and crown techniques have earlier antecedents [6, 7]; the exact rank-one two-singleton
lemma used here is [5, Proposition 3.4]. Reﬂection of arbitrary Young preﬁxes is already cov-
ered by [1]. Related upper-extremal investigations include Qing’s 2008 thesis and Wright’s 2019
slides/abstract [8, 9]; their global-envelope or restricted-preﬁx scope is distinct from the supported-
cycle assertion. Byrnes also uses cycle arguments under other hypotheses, so this note asserts no
new general use of cycles in the subject.
    A dated bounded primary-source comparison, completed 22 October 2026, found no earlier
equivalent supported-cycle upper-bound theorem in the inspected material. Historical ﬁrstness, ﬁrst
application and present worldwide openness are not certiﬁed. Source versions, inspected portions
and access limits are recorded in the companion attribution dossier. The ﬁnal publisher bodies
of Gaetz–Venkataramana and Miller were not obtained; their versioned primary preprints were
inspected. No subsequent full proof corresponding to Wright’s talk was located, and subscription
indexing and unpublished work remain coverage limits. The classiﬁcation of this result concerns
the literal printed improvement request, not every stronger rank question.
