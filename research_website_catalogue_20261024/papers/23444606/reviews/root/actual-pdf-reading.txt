Abstract
Every discrete initial ordered additive subgroup of the surreal numbers is order-group-
isomorphic to a subgroup of the omnific integers that is initial in the surreal numbers. This
holds for sets and proper classes in NBG with global choice and answers the discrete-group ques-
tion posed by Kaplan and Ehrlich. An explicit operation on ordinal-length sign sequences moves
the least exponent to zero without creating a right-simplicity-predecessor obligation. Relabeling
the actual normal-form supports and normalizing only the bottom coeﬀicient group then gives
the required image. An appendix supplies a detailed suﬀiciency proof of the initial-group char-
acterization used in the argument, addressing support exhaustion and the legality of limit-cut
options.
1 Question, conventions and established inputs
An initial subclass of No contains every proper sign-sequence prefix of each of its members. This
is closure under simplicity, not numerical convexity. We write u ≺s v for a proper prefix, u ⪯s v
when equality is also allowed, P (v) = {u : u ≺s v}, and Rs(v) = {u ∈ P (v) : u > v }. All orders
and group operations are inherited from No. A nontrivial ordered group is discrete if it has a least
positive element; the zero group is treated separately.
The following is the full additive-group question, with no multiplicative closure hypothesis.
Theorem 1. Let G ⊆ No be a discrete initial ordered additive subgroup. There is an order-group
isomorphism T : G − →H, where H ⊆ Oz is initial in No. The assertion holds when G is a set
and when it is a proper class. The zero group also has the stated property.
The target occurs in Kaplan’s April 2015 honors thesis [ 1, §7.1, Question 1, p. 47], in his joint-
work contribution with Ehrlich [ 2, Question 1, p. 3356], and in Ehrlich–Kaplan [ 3, Question 9.1
in the author version]. The numbering in arXiv:1512.04001v1 is Question 2. The isomorphism in
Theorem 1 is not required to preserve simplicity.

2 An exponent map with the required prefix control
Lemma 2. If G is nontrivial, discrete and initial, its exponent class Γ has minimum m = −α for
a set ordinal α, and Rm = 2 −nZ for some n ∈ N0. Its least positive element is 2−nωm.
Proof. Let g > 0 be least, with leading term rωm, r > 0. Truncation closure puts this term and the
residual tail in G. A nonzero tail has absolute value strictly between zero and g, a contradiction.
Thus g = rωm. The group Rm has least positive element r, so item 2 gives Rm = 2 −nZ and r = 2 −n.
If y ∈ Γ were below m, cross sectionality would give 0 < ω y < g . Hence m = min Γ. A plus sign in
m would make the prefix before that sign a member of Γ strictly below m. Thus m is the all-minus
sequence (−)α, namely −α.
The least-positive-element form is already present in [ 3, Proposition 5.2]; the negative-ordinal
minimum observation also appears in [ 4, Lemma 3.2]. The proof records their precise role here.
Concatenation of sign sequences is denoted by juxtaposition, and e is the empty sequence.
Concatenation is the ordered sum of blocks; no cancellation of ordinal lengths is intended.

4 Attribution, verification and limits
Exponent relabeling and positive coeﬀicient scaling are established Hahn-group mechanisms; an
explicit later account is [ 5, Proposition 3.9 and Construction 3.10]. General order/simplicity sub-
structures and partition constructions [ 6] do not by themselves guarantee the additive compatibility
and initial-image property needed here. The specific bridge proved above is the sign operation ( 2),
its right-prefix control, and its use in the actual subgroup. No claim of first discovery of the general
transport machinery is made.

A A detailed proof of the initial-group suﬀiciency criterion
This appendix proves the suﬀiciency assertion used above. The characterization is credited to [ 3,
Theorem 5.1], not claimed as new. In the inspected author proof two literal steps require care: the
union of bounded exponent stages need not exhaust cofinal supports, and dyadic perturbations of
a series need not belong to the given coeﬀicient groups. The proof below avoids both steps. This
concerns proof presentation, not a counterexample to the theorem.
Proposition 4. Let A be a truncation-closed, cross-sectional additive subgroup of normal forms
over an initial exponent class Γ. Suppose every Ry is initial in R and Z[1/2] ⊆ Ry for y ∈ Rs(x),
x, y ∈ Γ. Then the corresponding subgroup of No is initial.
We use standard recursive addition and negation, the simplicity theorem, normal-form unique-

A.1 Initial sums and adjoining a cut
If q = {L | R} and z ≺s q, then z is a prefix of or equal to an option on its side. Indeed, if z < q
and every l ∈ L were below z, then L < z < R , contrary to simplicity of q. Thus z ≤ l < q for some
l ∈ L. A sign sequence lying between a sequence and one of its extensions must extend the shorter
sequence, by the first differing sign comparison. Consequently z ⪯s l. For z > q use a member
r ∈ R with q < r ≤ z. In particular, if all options belong to an initial set M , all proper prefixes of
q belong to M .
If S, U are initial sets, then S + U is initial. For each pair (s, u), induct on the natural (Hes-
senberg) sum of their ordinal birthdays. Every recursive addition option sL + u, s + uL, sR + u or
s + uR has a smaller operand measure. It belongs to S + U and, by induction, so do all its prefixes.
The preceding option-covering argument for the defining cut of s + u now puts all prefixes of s + u
in S + U . The induction is on operand birthdays, not on the birthday of the sum.
Negation reverses all signs and preserves prefixes. Hence the additive subgroup generated by
any initial set containing zero is initial: take finite sums of its union with its negative, and then
their union. It follows that, for an initial set-sized subgroup M and a surreal b simplest between
sets of options from M , the group M + Zb is initial. This directly supplies the additive adjunction
needed here; no general module-adjunction theorem is assumed. If desired, when b /∈ M , its small
options extend to the partition M<b, M>b: b realizes it, and any realization of the full partition
realizes the small cut, so b is still simplest.
A.2 Finite-support elements first
First suppose Γ = E is a set. Then A is a set, since all its series are encoded in the set of real-
coeﬀicient functions on subsets of E. Well order E by nondecreasing exponent birthday, breaking
ties by choice. All proper prefixes of an exponent precede it. Starting with {0}, build initial
subgroups by adjoining actual pure terms.
At exponent y, first adjoin ωy if necessary. All options in ( 6) are in the previously generated
group: earlier exponent monomials give the left options, and Z[1/2] ⊆ RyR gives the right ones. The
additive adjunction proves initiality and supplies all integer multiples. Next adjoin the noninteger
rωy, r ∈ Ry, in nondecreasing real birthday. Initiality of Ry puts its real predecessors in Ry;
their multiples have already appeared (integer multiples are already present). For nondyadic r, or
noninteger dyadic r with no left prefix of y, the pure cut uses just rLωy and rRωy. In the other

or it is already finite. Each option in ( 7) has length at most µ + 1 < λ, so belongs to the previous
subgroup. Adjunction applies. At limit stages take unions. The same δ + j decomposition proves
that the resulting initial group is all of A. This includes supports cofinal in the entire exponent
enumeration; no bounded-stage union is used.
