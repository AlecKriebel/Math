# Checkable original-only deductions and boundary controls

These arguments were reconstructed after reading only the original. Statements about the central nonamenability input and cited classical interfaces remain conditional. No scientific code or imported primary text was used.

## Countability with finite generation retained

The dyadic rationals and integer slope exponents form countable sets. A finite piecewise-linear description uses a finite sequence of such data; the union over finite lengths is countable. This gives countability of standard F. For T, include the dyadic image of a chosen dyadic basepoint and the finite dyadic subdivision/slopes; this is again a countable union of finite products of countable sets.

If a countable H is generated by d<∞ elements, evaluation on those generators injects Aut(H) into H^d. Thus Aut(H) is countable. This applies to F conditional on its cited finite generation; having a countable underlying group alone would not justify the injection into a finite power.

For index n, each subgroup K is a point stabilizer under a coset action H→Sym(n). There are at most (n!)^d possible homomorphisms and at most n stabilizers per homomorphism, so only finitely many index-n subgroups. Summing over n gives countably many finite-index subgroups. A direct Schreier argument gives finite generation of each: choose finitely many coset representatives r, and use the finitely many elements r x (representative of r x)^{-1} for the original finite generating set and its inverses. These generate the subgroup by telescoping a word through its successive cosets.

For each ordered pair K,L of finite-index subgroups, a homomorphism K→L is determined by finitely many images in the countable L. Hence the set of isomorphisms is at most countable. A countable union of such sets, followed by the commensurator equivalence quotient, is countable. No assumption that every subgroup of a finitely generated group is finitely generated has been made; only finite-index subgroups are used.

## Canonical trace and the conditional uniqueness step

For a finite group-algebra sum a=∑a_g λ_g, τ_G(a)=a_e. For two finite sums, τ_G(ab)=∑_g a_g b_{g^{-1}}=τ_G(ba). The vector state is positive since τ_G(a* a)=||aδ_e||²≥0 and has value1 on the unit. Its norm is1; the trace identity extends from the dense group algebra by norm continuity.

Suppose the cited BKKO support theorem holds and R_a(G)={e}. Any tracial state σ then has σ(λ_g)=0 for g≠e and σ(1)=1. On every finite sum, σ(a)=a_e=τ_G(a). Both states are bounded, so density gives σ=τ_G on C*_r(G). This proves only the final uniqueness implication from the exact support theorem. Neither simplicity⇒trivial radical nor the support theorem has been re-proved or accepted without inspection.

## Full group algebra is a different target

For any nontrivial discrete G, let u_g denote its canonical universal unitaries. The trivial representation gives the augmentation character χ(u_g)=1. The regular representation gives the quotient π:C*(G)→C*_r(G), and τ_G∘π is another tracial state. For g≠e, their values on u_g are1 and0. Also u_g−1 belongs to kerχ and is nonzero, because (τ_G∘π)(u_g−1)=−1. Since χ(1)=1, its kernel is proper and nonzero. Thus the full algebra is not simple. This independently tests the paper's reduced/full boundary without any assumption about amenability of F.

## A concrete control against arbitrary abstract overgroups

Take K=F×C₂ and write z for the nonidentity element of the second factor. This K is countable and contains an abstract copy of F regardless of the truth of P. In C*_r(K), λ_z is a central self-adjoint unitary. The elements p_±=(1±λ_z)/2 are central orthogonal projections summing to1. Both are nonzero because p_±δ_e=(δ_e±δ_z)/2. Their ideals are proper and nonzero, so C*_r(K) is not simple.

There are at least two distinct traces: σ_±(a)=2τ_K(p_±a). Positivity follows from centrality of p_±, normalization from τ_K(p_±)=1/2, and traciality from that of τ_K. Their values on λ_z are respectively+1 and−1. Thus a countable abstract overgroup assertion would fail both claimed conclusions, even if F were nonamenable. This control does not contradict the stated standard-action theorem; its action hypothesis must do real work.

## Standard circle action and local copies

The quotient [0,1]/(0∼1) is a circle. Every f∈F fixes both endpoints and preserves the interior, so it descends. If its descended map is the identity, each x∈(0,1) has the same quotient image as f(x); the quotient is injective on the interior, hence f(x)=x there and at the endpoints. Thus the standard circle action is faithful.

Given a nonempty open arc, choose a smaller arc avoiding the identified endpoint, then a dyadic interval I=[a,a+2^{-m}] inside it. Conjugating f∈F affinely to I and setting it equal to the identity outside I gives a standard F element: transformed breakpoints remain dyadic, its slopes are the original powers of2, and endpoint continuity holds. This realizes an injective copy of F inside the rigid stabilizer of the arc. Conditional on P and subgroup heredity of amenability, each such rigid stabilizer is nonamenable. The exact cited C*-simplicity criterion still needs inspection; this local construction alone is not a proof of all its hypotheses.

The centralizer of the standard F action in Homeo(S¹) is trivial. If h moves some x, choose an arc U around x with U∩h(U)=∅. Choose a nonidentity standard-F element supported in a dyadic interval inside U. Its h-conjugate is supported in h(U), so it cannot equal the original nonidentity element. Therefore h does not centralize standard F. In particular, the central C₂ in the abstract-overgroup control cannot act faithfully while its F restriction is exactly the standard action. This explains concretely why that control lies outside the manuscript's action-level hypothesis.
