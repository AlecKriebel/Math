                                                   Abstract. We introduce a density model for random quotients of a free prod-
                                                   uct of finitely generated groups. We prove that a random quotient in this model
                                                   has the following properties with overwhelming probability: if the density is
                                                   below 1/2, the free factors embed into the random quotient and the random
                                                   quotient is hyperbolic relative to the free factors. Further, there is a phase
                                                   transition at 1/2, with the random quotient being a finite group above this
                                                   density. If the density is below 1/6, the random quotient is cubulated relative
                                                   to the free factors. Moreover, if the free factors are cubulated, then so is the
                                                   random quotient.




                                                                               1. Introduction
                                               Gromov’s density model of random groups is an oft-used tool for studying
                                           ‘generic’ properties of groups. The model is defined as follows: Let n ≥ 2, ℓ >
                                           0, 0 < d < 1. A Gromov random group (see [Gro93]) is given by a presentation
                                           ⟨S∣R⟩, where ∣S∣ = n and R is a collection of (2n − 1)dℓ cyclically reduced words
                                           on the alphabet S of length ℓ, chosen uniformly at random with replacement. The
                                           value d is called the density. Traditionally, we fix a density d and a number of
                                           generators n, and say that a property P holds with overwhelming probability if the
                                           probability of a random group satisfying P tends to 1 as ℓ → ∞.
                                               When d < 1/2, with overwhelming probability a Gromov random group is torsion-
                                           free non-elementary hyperbolic; on the other hand, when d > 1/2, with overwhelm-
                                           ing probability a random group is either trivial or Z/2Z [Gro93; OW11]. Other
                                           properties of random groups also exhibit a phase transition phenomenon, that is,
                                           there is a threshold density d0 above and below which some property is either satis-
                                           fied or not satisfied with overwhelming probability. These properties include small
                                           cancellation conditions [Oll04b; Tsa22; Tsa24], satisfying Greendlinger’s lemma
                                           [Oll04b], a Freiheitssatz-type property [Tsa23], and Property (T) (folklore). For
                                           the last property, the threshold density is currently unknown, though it is known
                                           to be at most 1/3 [Żuk03; KK13] and no less than 1/4 [Ash22]. It is unknown if
                                           there is a threshold density for cubulation (that is, acting geometrically on a CAT(0)
                                           cube complex). Ollivier–Wise showed in [OW11] that for d < 1/6, Gromov random
                                           groups are, with overwhelming probability, cubulated, and Mackay–Przytycki and
                                           Montee [MP15; Mon23] extended these results to cocompact, but not necessarily
                                           proper, actions on CAT(0) cube complexes for d < 3/14.
                                           1.1. The free product density model. In this paper, we initiate the study of
                                           random quotients of free products of groups and obtain a few analogous results.
                                           We do so by defining a density model for random quotients that uses the action of

a free product on a suitable Bass–Serre tree T , see [Ser77] or [BH99, Chapter III.Γ,
Section 6.3]. This is directly inspired by Gromov’s model: words of length ℓ in a
free group correspond to the finite set of elements that have translation length ℓ on
its Cayley graph. Note that, in our case, T is not locally finite whenever one of the
free factors is infinite, so the set of all loxodromic elements of a fixed translation
length is infinite. For this reason, we choose a density d random set of relators from
a natural finite subset of loxodromic elements of fixed translation length.
Definition 1.1. Let n ≥ 2, m ≥ 1, ℓ > 0, 0 < d < 1. Let G = (G1 , . . . , Gn ) be an n-
tuple of nontrivial finitely generated groups, that we refer to as the factor groups.
Moreover, we suppose that if n = 2, then G1 or G2 is not isomorphic to Z/2Z, so
that G1 ∗ G2 is not virtually cyclic. Let Si denote a finite generating set for Gi .
Let Bi (m) ⊆ Gi be the set of nontrivial elements in the ball of radius m in the
word metric with respect to Si . Let Sℓ be the set of words w = s1 s2 ⋯sℓ such that
each syllable sj ∈ ⋃ Bi (m) and such that no two consecutive syllables sj and sj+1
are in the same factor group. In addition, we assume that s1 and sℓ are not in the
same factor group. Let R ⊂ Sℓ be a subset chosen by selecting ∣Sℓ ∣d words from Sℓ
uniformly at random, with replacement. Then the group G = G1 ∗ ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ ∗ Gn /⟪R⟫ is
a random group in the free product density model of random groups on G1 , . . . , Gn .
We write G ∼ FPD(G; d, m, ℓ) to indicate that G is a random group in this model.
We say that a group G ∼ F PD(G; d, m, ℓ) satisfies a property P with overwhelming
probability if limℓ→∞ P(G satisfies P ) = 1.
   Note that Sℓ is a finite set of elements of G1 ∗ ⋯ ∗ Gn of free product length = ℓ
represented in normal form. For readers familiar with the notion of angle in this
context, note that the set Sℓ consists of the elements of translation length ℓ in the
Bass–Serre tree T that subtend an angle at most m at each turn. We note that the
model depends on the choice of a finite generating set for each group Gi .
   As the hyperbolic geometry of a free group is reflected by its action on its Cayley
graph, so too is the relative hyperbolic geometry of a free product reflected by its
action on the Bass–Serre tree. From this point of view, our model is a natural
generalization of the Gromov model to quotients of free products. The use of the
Bass–Serre tree allows us to push many of the methods that have been used to
study Gromov random groups to this setting.
   Our first theorem below shows that there is a threshold density ( 21 ) below which
the factor groups of the free product embed into G, and G itself is non-elementary
relatively hyperbolic with respect to the factor groups.
Theorem 1.2. Let G ∼ FPD(G; d, m, ℓ).
  (1) If d < 12 , then, with overwhelming probability, the groups G1 , G2 , . . ., Gn
      embed into G and G is hyperbolic relative to {G1 , . . . , Gn }.
  (2) If d > 21 and n ≥ 3, then, with overwhelming probability, G has order at
      most 2. If d > 1/2 and n = 2, with overwhelming probability G is equal to a
      finite dihedral group or the free product G1 ∗ ⋅ ⋅ ⋅ ∗ Gn .
Remark 1.3. If n = 2, then Sℓ is empty for all odd ℓ. Therefore G = G1 ∗ G2 in this
case.
   When each free factor is a free group, our model gives a random quotient of a free
group. However, our quotients behave differently than Gromov random quotients.

Example 1.4. Let us consider the free product Gk = Fk ∗ Z of a non-abelian free
group on k generators and an infinite cyclic group. We may consider Gk ≅ Fk+1
and ask how random quotients of Gk taken in the free product density model relate
to random quotients in the Gromov density model. By Theorem 1.2, Fk embeds
in the random free product quotient of Gk for all values of k, whenever d < 1/2.
In the Gromov density model, however, Tsai shows in [Tsa23, Theorem 1] that
there exists a function ε ∶ N → (0, 21 ] which tends quickly to 0 as k → ∞ so that: if
d > ε(k) then the factor group Fk does not embed into a random quotient, and if
d < ε(k) then Fk embeds. Nevertheless, Gromov random groups at density d < 1/2
are hyperbolic (see [Gro93; Oll04b]), and thus hyperbolic relative to the image of
Fk .
1.2. Actions on CAT(0) cube complexes. We also extend the cubulation
result of [OW11] for Gromov random groups to the free product density model. A
group is cubulated if it acts properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex.
In a similar vein, a group is relatively cubulated if it admits a relatively geometric
action on a CAT(0) cube complex (see Definition 6.1).
Theorem 1.5. Let G ∼ FPD(G; d, m, ℓ). If d < 16 , then G is, with overwhelming
probability, cubulated relative to {G1 , . . . , Gn }. If moreover each Gi is a cubulated
group, then, with overwhelming probability, G is cubulated.
    We actually prove a generalization of Theorem 1.5, see Theorem 7.5. This theo-
rem applies to a free product of relatively cubulated groups. Each of these groups
is cubulated relative to a set of subgroups called the peripheral subgroups. The
theorem then asserts a relative cubulation for the random quotient relative to the
collection of the peripheral subgroups of the factor groups. The first part of Theo-
rem 1.5 follows from this, and the second part is proven in Section 8.
    In the case of d < 1/12, Theorem 4.30 implies that the random quotient G sat-
isfies, with overwhelming probability, the C ′ (1/6)-condition over the free product.
In this case Theorem 1.5 follows from [MS17], see also [JW22] in the case that
d < 1/40, and [EN21]. The idea of the proof is to extend these papers and [OW11]
to the setting of the free product density model.
Remark 1.6. If a group G is cubulated relative to peripheral subgroups G1 , . . . , Gn ,
this can have powerful consequences even if the peripherals are not themselves
cubulated. For instance, if all the peripheral subgroups Gi are residually finite,
then G itself is residually finite and all its full relatively quasiconvex subgroups are
separable [EG22, Corollary 1.7], see also [GM22, Theorem 4.7].
Remark 1.7. As a consequence of Theorem 1.5, we obtain a partial answer to
[FW24, Problem 7.2]. Let G1 and G2 act properly and cocompactly on CAT(0)
cube complexes X1 , X2 , respectively. In this case, let us view the free product
G = G1 ∗G2 as the fundamental group of the space X∗ , which is composed of X1 , X2
and a segment s whose endpoints are glued to X1 and X2 , respectively. Then G
is cubulated by Theorem 1.5, and the density of cubulation given by Theorem 1.5
does not depend on the length of s. Note, however, that [FW24, Problem 7.2] does
not specify a model of a random quotient of a free product. It also remains open
whether the density of Theorem 1.5 is optimal. In fact, to our knowledge the only
model for random groups with a known sharp optimal upper bound for cubulation
is the 6-gonal model for random groups [Odr25].

   Having unbounded orbits on a CAT(0) cube complex and Property (T) are
mutually exclusive group properties. In the case that d < 16 , Theorem 1.5 implies
that there is such an action on a CAT(0) cube complex, so G ∼ FPD(G; d, m, ℓ)
does not have Property (T) regardless of the choice of factor groups. However, in
the free product density model the following is open.

Question 1.9. Let G ∼ FPD(G; d, m, ℓ). If d > 13 , does the random group G have
Property (T) with overwhelming probability?

   These questions are motivated by the best-known densities in Gromov’s model
for random groups (see [Mon23], [Ash22], [Żuk03]).
   A strategy to answer Question 1.9 in the affirmative is the following. Let G1 =
⟨X1 , R1 ⟩ and G2 = ⟨X2 , R2 ⟩, and let G = ⟨X1 ∪ X2 , R1 ∪ R2 ∪ R⟩. Note that G′ =
⟨X1 ∪ X2 , R⟩ is a random quotient in the free product density model of two free
groups. Moreover, if G′ = ⟨X1 ∪ X2 , R⟩ has Property (T), then so does G. Thus,
Question 1.9 can be reduced to whether the group G′ has Property (T).
   It is also unclear how the group properties of groups in the free product density
model compare to those in Gromov’s model for random groups in the case that
each factor group is infinite cyclic. The referee suggested the following question.

Question 1.10. If we consider a d-random quotient, with any m ≥ 1, of the free
product of k ≥ 2 infinite cyclic groups Z ∗ Z ∗ ⋯ ∗ Z, it does not coincide with
the Gromov density model with k generators at density d. Are there any group
properties that differ?

1.3. Outline. In Section 2, we provide constructions of model spaces XR and
XR (Z) for random quotients of free products that we use throughout the paper.
The first space, XR , witnesses the geometry relative to the free factors while XR (Z)
is often quasi-isometric to the group itself. The main results of Section 4 are
Theorem 4.30, a local isoperimetric inequality for XR , and Lemma 4.33, a non-
planar version of Greendlinger’s Lemma. In Section 5, we use Theorem 4.30 to
prove a global isoperimetric inequality, Theorem 5.1. We then examine how the
geometry of XR reflects the relative hyperbolicity of a random quotient relative to
the factors when d < 21 , and prove Theorem 1.2. In Section 6, we establish results
for density d < 16 about the geometry of codimension–1 subspaces of our model
spaces called hyperstructures.
   These results are used in Section 7 and Section 8 to prove Theorem 1.5. The-
orem 7.5 is the main result of Section 7, where we prove that random quotients
are relatively geometric cubulated with overwhelming probability at d < 16 when
the factors are (possibly trivially) relatively geometrically cubulated. In addition
to the results from Section 6, the proof of Theorem 7.5 uses a relative cubulation
criterion from [EN21]. Finally, in Section 8, we prove that if the factor groups are
properly and cocompactly cubulated, then a random quotient at density d < 61 will
be properly and cocompactly cubulated with overwhelming probability. For this,

we use a boundary criterion of Bergeron–Wise [BW12, Theorem 5.1] for proper-

Example 2.4. Let G1 = ⟨a⟩ be an infinite cyclic group, and let G2 = ⟨b, c⟩ be a free
group. Let Z1 and Z2 be their respective Cayley graphs. Let R = {a(bc)10 , (bc)−10 a},

G = G1 ∗ G2 /⟪R⟫. Thus the projection map from T (Z) to XR (Z) does not restrict
to an embedding of Z1 or Z2 .

Example 2.5. Assume that R satisfies the C ′ (1/6) small cancellation condition over

a central vertex, and otherwise we call the vertex a factor vertex. By the definition

and central vertices.

Lemma 2.6. The quotient G = G∗ /⟪R⟫ acts on the complex XR with trivial edge
stabilizers. Moreover, the stabilizer of a vertex of XR satisfies the following: if
the vertex is central, then the stabilizer is trivial. Otherwise, it is conjugate to the
image of one of the groups Gi in G.
Proof. Let x be an edge or a vertex of XR and let g ∈ G∗ /⟪R⟫ be the image of an
element h =/ 1 in G∗ such that h x = x. Let x be a pre-image of x. If hx /= x, then
h ∈ ⟪R⟫ and h = 1 in G. This settles the case where x is an edge. Otherwise, x
and x are vertices, and h is in the stabilizer of x. If x ∈ {g{1} ∣ g ∈ G}, then the
stabilizer of x is trivial and h = 1. Thus the stabilizer of x is trivial in this case.
Otherwise, the stabilizer of x is conjugate to one of the groups Gi . This yields the

    We end this section by introducing the following important notation.
Definition 2.7. An edge triple in T is an ordered triple (e1 , v, e2 ) so that e1 , e2
are edges in T containing a factor vertex v. We assign a label to each edge triple in
T , named a rotation element, as follows: Given an edge triple (e1 , v, e2 ), where e1 =
(w1 {1}, w2 Gi ), e2 = (w2 Gi , w3 {1}), the rotation element from e1 to e2 is w1−1 w3 ∈
Gi .
Remark 2.8. The rotation element w1−1 w3 is indeed in Gi as case w1 = w2 w1′ and
w3 = w2 w3′ , where w1′ , w3′ ∈ Gi . Rotation elements of edge triples in T are G∗ -
invariant, that is, (e1 , v, e2 ) and g(e1 , v, e2 ) have the same rotation element for any
g ∈ G∗ .
    By Remark 2.8 the following is well-defined.
Definition 2.9. An edge triple in XR is an ordered triple (e1 , v, e2 ) so that e1 ,
e2 are edges in XR containing a factor vertex v. Let (e′1 , v ′ , e′2 ) be a pre-image
of (e1 , v, e2 ) in T . The rotation element of (e1 , v, e2 ) is the image of the rotation
element of (e′1 , v ′ , e′2 ) under the quotient map q ∶ G∗ → G. Moreover, in this case
the rotation element of (e′1 , v ′ , e′2 ) in G∗ is called a geometric representative of the
rotation element of (e1 , v, e2 ).
Remark 2.10. The rotation element of an edge triple in XR is well-defined in G
by Remark 2.8, but may have multiple geometric representatives in Gi , for some
factor group Gi of G∗ . This happens if and only if Gi does not embed in G.
For instance, let G be the group of Example 2.4 and let (e′1 , v ′ , e′2 ) be an edge
triple in T with rotation element a2 . Then the rotation element of the image of
(e′1 , v ′ , e′2 ) in XR is the identity element, and both the identity element and a2
are geometric representatives of this rotation element in G∗ . Similarly, rotation
elements about a given factor vertex with rotation elements a and a3 are also
geometric representatives of the same edge triple in XR . This is related to the fact
that a complex of group is developable if and only if the vertex groups embed, see
[BH99, Chapter III.C Theorem 2.13 and Proposition 3.14].

its cardinality. By a disk diagram in a 2-complex X, we mean a combinatorial map

In particular, a disk diagram D may have a spur so need not be a topological disk.

    (1) the boundary of every 2-cell has length = 2ℓ,
    (2) there is a partition of the vertices into factor vertices and central vertices

        to factor vertices, and central vertices to central vertices.
An edge triple in Y is a triple (e1 , v, e2 ), where v is a factor vertex and e1 and e2
are two edges of Y at v.

4.1. Decorated abstract diagrams. From now on, let G = (G1 , . . . , Gn ) be an

Definition 4.4. An abstract diagram is a finite connected 2-complex. A decorated
abstract diagram is a finite connected 2-complex with bipartite 1-skeleton in which
every 2-cell is a 2ℓ-gon, along with the following decoration:
    (1) a distinguished vertex on the boundary of each 2-cell such that all distin-

    (2) an orientation in each 2-cell, and
    (3) a partition of the faces of the 2-complex.

Definition 3.2, we refer to the elements of the part containing the distinguished
vertices as factor vertices. All other vertices are referred to as central vertices.
A triple (e1 , v, e2 ) in a decorated abstract diagram is an edge triple if e1 and e2
are distinct edges containing a factor vertex v. The area of a decorated abstract
diagram Y , denoted Area(Y ), is the number of 2-cells in Y .

Finally, for every k > 0, one adds a k-cube if the (k − 1)-skeleton of a cube appears.
Remark 8.11. As the hyperstructure extending disjoint hyperplanes of Y may in-
tersect in EXbal , the cube complex Y is not isomorphic to Cr∗ (Y ). However, Y is
a subcomplex of Cr∗ (Y ).
    The cube complex C(U) is isomorphic to the cube complex dual to the wallspace
U ′ on EXbal defined by removing all those halfspaces from U such that only one
halfspace has non-empty intersection with Y [HW14, Remark 3.22]. Note that in
our context these are halfspaces whose corresponding EXbal –hyperstructures are
disjoint from Y . This cube complex is denoted by C(U ′ ). Note that U ∖ U ′ is
contained in every 0-cube of C(U ).
Remark 8.12. The cube complex C(U ′ ) embeds in C(G) with image C(U), where
the embedding is defined as follows: a 0-cell c′ of C(U ′ ) is sent to the 0-cell c′ ∪
(U ∖ U ′ ) of C(U). This extends to the higher dimensional cubes of C(U ′ ) in a
natural way. The action of P on EXbal induces an action of P on C(U ′ ), or C(U),
respectively. Also observe that P stabilizes the set of walls in U ′ and the set of
walls in U ∖ U ′ . By definition, the embedding of C(U ′ ) is P -equivariant.
Proposition 8.13. The group P acts cocompactly on C(U).
    By Remark 8.12 it is sufficient to prove that P acts cocompactly on C(U ′ ). We
first prove the following. Recall that Y is an essential CAT(0) cube complex, on
which P acts properly and cocompactly.
Lemma 8.14. Let W1 and W2 be two walls of U ′ that intersect in EXbal . Then
either they intersect in Y , or the intersection of the carriers of p(W1 ) and p(W2 )
contains an edge of Xbal at vP , the vertex of Xbal fixed by P .
Proof. Suppose that W1 and W2 do not intersect in Y . If p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) intersect
in the interior of a 2-cell containing vP we are done.
   Otherwise, p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) collar a diagram of Xbal . Let D be a minimal such
diagram containing vP . Recall that a corner of D is a 2-cell c of D such that p(W1 )
and p(W2 ) intersect in the interior of c. Note that a 2-cell is a corner if and only
if both p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) have non-empty intersection with its interior. Indeed,
suppose p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) both have non-empty intersection with the interior of a
2-cell c of Xbal and let c̃ be the pre-image of c in EXbal . Both W1 and W2 connect
opposite edges of c̃. Thus, W1 and W2 intersect in the interior of c̃, hence, p(W1 )
and p(W2 ) intersect in the interior of c.
   As p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) do not intersect in the interior of a 2-cell containing vP ,
D has at most one corner. By Lemma 6.32 every ε-supershell of D has to be a
corner. Thus, by Lemma 4.33 the diagram D has at most two 2-cells. Both must
contain vP , as the interior of one contains a segment of p(W1 ) and the interior of
the other contains a segment of p(W2 ). Also p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) have to intersect

in a point of the intersection of these two 2-cells that is distinct from vP . Hence,
the intersection of the carriers of p(W1 ) and p(W2 ) contains an edge at vP .     □
Proof of Proposition 8.13. Let W1 , W2 be walls in U ′ that intersect in EXbal . Let
hi be the hyperplane of Y such that Wi ∩ Y = hi .
   Recall the constant τ from Remark 6.12. Let R ∶= 2τ . Observe that R ≥
2 max{∣∣h∣∣Y ∣ h ∈ BP (m)}, where ∣∣h∣∣Y denotes the translation length of h on
Y and BP (m) is the ball of radius m in the Cayley graph of P .
   We denote by NR (h) the cubical R-neighbourhood of h and claim that NR (h1 ) ∩
NR (h2 ) is non-empty. Indeed, if W1 and W2 intersect in Y , then the intersection
of h1 and h2 is non-empty. Otherwise, the carriers of the projected hypergraphs of
W1 and W2 in Xbal intersect in an edge of Xbal at vP . By construction of EXbal ,
this implies that d(h1 , h2 ) ⩽ R, hence, the claim.
   Now let W1 , W2 , . . . , Wk be a set of walls of U ′ that pairwise intersect in EXbal .
Then NR (h1 ), NR (h2 ), . . . , NR (hk ) pairwise intersect. Note that the spaces NR (hi )
are convex in Y . By Helly’s theorem (see Lemma 13.13 of [HW08], for instance)
there is a point y ∈ Y in the intersection of the sets NR (hi ). Thus, there is a radius
R′ > R such that each of the hyperplanes hi intersects the finite ball BR′ (y). Since
P acts cocompactly on Y , up to the group action of P on U ′ there are at most
finitely many families of walls of U ′ that pairwise intersect in EXbal .
   We conclude that P acts cocompactly on C(U ′ ). See for instance [HW14, Lemma
7.2]. This finishes the proof.                                                          □
