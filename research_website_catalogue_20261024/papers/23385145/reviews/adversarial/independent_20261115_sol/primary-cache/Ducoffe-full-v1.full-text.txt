                                               Abstract. The Weighted Center problem takes as its input a graph G = (V, E) together
                                               with a profile π such that every vertex v is mapped to some nonnegative multiplicative weight
                                               π(v). Its output must be some vertex c minimizing max{π(v)dG (c, v) : v ∈ V }. The classic
                                               Center problem corresponds to the case where π(v) = 1 for every vertex v. In the literature,
                                               various almost linear-time algorithms have been proposed for the Center problem on some
                                               well-structured classes of graphs. By contrast, similarly efficient algorithms for the Weighted
                                               Center problem have been scarce. We investigate how the Gromov hyperbolicity, alone or in




arXiv:2607.04287v1 [cs.DS] 5 Jul 2026
                                               combination with other metric and geometric properties on graphs, can be used in the design of
                                               exact and approximate almost linear-time algorithms for the Weighted Center problem. In
                                               particular, we derive almost optimal algorithms for the following well-studied classes of graphs:
                                               chordal graphs, distance-hereditary graphs (both in O(m) time), dually chordal graphs and
                                               chordal bipartite graphs (both in O(m log n) time).




                                                                                    1. Introduction
                                           Problem considered. We refer to [6] for basics in Graph Theory. Unless stated otherwise,
                                        graphs considered throughout the paper will be simple, undirected, unweighted and connected.
                                        As usual, let dG denote the shortest-path metric of a graph G = (V, E), i.e., for any vertices u
                                        and v their distance dG (u, v) is equal to the minimum number of edges on a (u, v)-path. The
                                        following facility location problem on graphs is considered:
                                         Weighted Center
                                         Input: A graph G = (V, E); a profile π : V 7→ R≥0 .
                                         Output: A vertex c ∈ V s.t. rπ (c) = max{π(v)dG (c, v) : v ∈ V } is minimized.
                                           Roughly, π(v) represents the importance for vertex v to be close to the new facility. For
                                        example, π(v) can be the expected material flux between v and a new factory, the number of
                                        children in a building at v who will attend to a new neighborhood school, etc. Furthermore, if
                                        we set π(v ′ ) = 0 for some vertices v ′ , then we can account for the plausible situation where the
                                        placement of a new facility is only relevant for a subset of the vertices. Note that we cannot
                                        simply remove the zero-weight vertices for it could change the distances in the graph.
                                           In line with existing literature [36], the function rπ is called a radius function. The solution
                                        vertex c is called a center. The Weighted Center problem can be defined more generally for
                                        any metric space. In particular, a linear-time algorithm for the Weighted Center problem on
                                        Euclidean spaces of constant dimension was presented in [31]. For graphs, the existing literature
                                        is mostly focused on the following restricted variant:
                                         Center
                                         Input: A graph G = (V, E).
                                         Output: A vertex c ∈ V s.t. ecc(c) = max{dG (c, v) : v ∈ V } is minimized.
                                           The radius function ecc is also called the eccentricity function of G. More generally, if π(v) ∈
                                        {0, 1} for every vertex v, then π is called a binary profile, and rπ is called a binary radius
                                        function. The special case of binary profiles on graphs has received some attention in the
                                        literature, see [10, 15, 22]. As a rule of thumb, most of the techniques for the Center problem
                                        can be also applied for binary profiles. However, such is not the case for the more general
                                        Weighted Center problem.

Related work. The Weighted Center problem can be reduced to All-Pairs Shortest-
Paths. For n-vertex m-edge graphs, this straightforward reduction leads to an O(nm)-time
algorithm. In [1], the Hitting Sets Conjecture was introduced to prove a conditional lower
bound in Ω(n2−o(1) ) for the Center problem on n-vertex graphs, even if the number of edges
is at most in n1+o(1) . Therefore, breaking the quadratic barrier for the Center problem, and
even more so for the Weighted Center problem, is likely to require additional restrictions
on the classes of graphs we consider. There is a plethora of subquadratic-time algorithms, and
even better, of almost linear-time algorithms, for the Center problem on some well-structured
classes of graphs; we refer to [1, 12, 20, 28] for a small sample of such results. By comparison,
the only published linear-time algorithm for the Weighted Center problem, to the best of
our knowledge, is that of Megiddo for the class of trees [42]. Recently, a parameterized almost
linear-time algorithm for the Weighted Center problem on the graphs of bounded clique-
width was presented in [28], using orthogonal range queries and the so-called convex hull trick.
In particular, for the distance-hereditary graphs, the clique-width of which is at most three [32],
this result leads to an O(m logc n)-time algorithm for the Weighted Center problem, for some
unspecified constant c > 1. An O(m)-time algorithm for the Center problem was presented
much earlier, in [23].
    The authors in [11] introduced a different approach for the Weighted Center problem
that is based on a metric property, hereafter called Gp -unimodality (see also [29] for the case
p = 1). Roughly, a radius function rπ on a graph G is called Gp -unimodal if any vertex that
minimizes rπ within its ball of radius p is a center. It was proved in [11] that for many classes of
graphs studied in Metric Graph Theory, all    √ their radius  functions are G2 -unimodal. The latter
was used in the design of randomized Õ(m n)-time1 local-search algorithms for the Weighted
Center problem on these classes of graphs. Although the algorithmic properties of the classes
studied in Metric Graph Theory have insofar received limited attention (the results in [2, 4, 24]
count among the few exceptions), we want to stress here that they are natural generalizations of
better-studied classes, including: chordal graphs, chordal
                                                       √        bipartite graphs, and dually chordal
graphs. In particular, before this work, the Õ(m n)-time algorithms from [11, 29] were the
best ones known for the Weighted Center problem on chordal (bipartite) graphs, and dually
chordal graphs. By comparison, O(m)-time algorithms for the Center problem can be found
in [7, 13, 27].
    One common property of chordal graphs and their relatives, but also of distance-hereditary
graphs, AT-free graphs, and several other well-studied classes of graphs (e.g., see [15,47]) is that
they have bounded Gromov hyperbolicity. Roughly, the hyperbolicity of a graph represents how
close, locally, its shortest-path metric is to a tree metric (see Sec. 2 for a formal definition). The
introduction of this parameter by Gromov, see [33], has revolutionized Geometric Group Theory.
Later on, extensive experiments have evidenced that many complex networks also have bounded
hyperbolicity [37]. There is now a rich literature of approximation algorithms on graphs whose
performances depend on the hyperbolicity value [35, 40]. Of special interest to us is the main
result from [15], which states that on any δ-hyperbolic graph G, for every binary profile π on G,
a vertex that is at distance at most O(δ) to the center can be computed in O(m) time. Note that
it leads to an approximation algorithm for the radius: defined as radπ (G) = min{rπ (v) : v ∈ V },
with additive one-sided error in O(δ). Furthermore, this result was combined with the local-
search techniques from√ [11] to refine the runtime of their exact algorithms (only for binary
profiles) from O(m n) to O(δm). The motivation for our paper was to generalize the result
from [15] to arbitrary profiles.

centers. In what follows, the ball of center v and radius ρ is denoted by Bρ (v). We provide the
following two main results for the Weighted Center problem on δ-hyperbolic graphs.

    Our result for chordal graphs builds upon the local-search techniques from [11]. Indeed, in
this special case a vertex c1 that is at distance O(1) to the center can be computed in O(m)
time (the latter is a refinement of Theorem A(1) for this class of graphs). Then, starting from
c1 , an improving sequence (v0 = c1 , v1 , . . . , vℓ ) can be computed in O(ℓm) time, such that
rπ (vi ) > rπ (vi+1 ) for every i such that 0 ≤ i < ℓ, and vℓ is a center. If π is a binary profile, then
as our starting vertex c1 is at distance O(1) to the center, we must have ℓ = O(1) no matter
how we choose vi+1 from vi . However, as already noted in [11], this does not hold anymore for
arbitrary profiles. We introduce a new selection mechanism for the next improving vertex vi+1
that ensures that the bound ℓ = O(1) still holds with arbitrary profiles.
    For distance-hereditary graphs, we use a similar local-search approach as for chordal graphs.
For that, we prove that all their radius functions are also G2 -unimodal. We also design an O(m)-
time procedure for computing the next improving vertex vi+1 from vi in a distance-hereditary
graph. The latter is based on injective hulls, which are important objects in the study of metric
spaces and their optimal realizations, see [26]. By doing so, we contribute to the recent line of
studies of the properties of injective hulls of graphs [34].
    For chordal bipartite graphs and dually chordal graphs, we adapt a technique from [24].
Roughly, it allows us to compute, in some ball of constant radius, all the vertices v such that
rπ (v) is less than a fixed threshold τ . By doing so, all the centers can be computed with O(log n)
calls to this technique, using a nontrivial variation of binary search. At this point, we should
mention that it is not that obvious that we can efficiently reduce the Weighted Center
problem to its decision version, using binary search, because the set of possible values for the
radius is {k × π(v) : 0 < k < n, v ∈ V }, which has size in O(n2 ). To apply a naive binary-
search strategy to this set would require first to sort all the values. The latter would result in

Results on the Weighted Center problem. Let G = (V, E) be a graph, and let π :
V 7→ R≥0 be a profile. The support of π, hereafter denoted by Supp(π), is the set of all vertices
v such that π(v) > 0. Recall that rπ : u ∈ V 7→ max{π(v)d(u, v) : v ∈ V } is called a radius
function. Furthermore, for every vertex u, let Fπ (u) = {v ∈ V : rπ (u) = π(v)d(u, v)}. Recall
that we define the (π-)radius as radπ (G) = min{rπ (u) : u ∈ V }. In the same way, let the center
Cπ (G) contain all the vertices c such that rπ (c) is minimized; each vertex c ∈ Cπ (G) is also
called a center. As a side contribution of the paper, we present a reduction from the Weighted
Center problem to its decision version. For that, although the search space for radπ (G) is in
O(n2 ), we can use a strategy inspired by the median-of-medians algorithm [5] to quickly reduce
the size of this space.
Lemma 2.2. Let G = (V, E) be a graph, and let π be an arbitrary profile on G. If the problem
of either computing a vertex c such that rπ (c) ≤ τ , or asserting that no such vertex exists, can be
solved in O(T (n, m)) time for any value τ , then the Weighted Center problem can be solved
in O ((T (n, m) + n) log n) time on G, π.
Proof. If Supp(π) = ∅, then Cπ (G) = V . Furthermore, if Supp(π) is a singleton, then Cπ (G) =
Supp(π). Thus, let us assume from now on | Supp(π)| > 1. We maintain a set S ⊆ V × N × N
with the property that for some (v, av , bv ) ∈ S, there exists some k such that av ≤ k ≤ bv ,
and radπ (G) = π(v)k. Initially, we set S = {(v, 1, n − 1) : v ∈ Supp(π)}. Then, our algorithm
iteratively performs some procedure, detailed next, until the following halting condition, (HC), is
met: for every (v, av , bv ) ∈ S, av = bv . Note that once (HC) holds, we are left with O(|S|) = O(n)
distinct possibilities for radπ (G). We can then sort all these values in O(n log n) time, remove
duplicates, and compute a center in O(T (n, m) log n) time by using a standard binary search.
   Assume for what follows that (HC) does not hold. For every v ∈ Supp(π), we define its
weight ω(v) as follows: if there is someP triple (v, av , bv ) in S, then ω(v) = bv − av , and otherwise
ω(v) = 0. Furthermore, let ω(S) = {ω(v) : v ∈ Supp(π)}. As (HC) does not hold, ω(S) > 0.

δ-hyperbolic graphs and their properties. For every nodes u, v, x, y in a tree T , it holds
dT (u, v) + dT (x, y) ≤ max{dT (u, x) + dT (v, y), dT (u, y) + dT (v, x)}. The definition of Gromov
hyperbolicity can be regarded as a relaxation of this four-point condition. A graph G = (V, E)
is called δ-hyperbolic if for every u, v, x, y ∈ V , d(u, v) + d(x, y) ≤ max{d(u, x) + d(v, y), d(u, y) +
d(v, x)} + 2δ holds. Alternatively, if d(u, y) + d(v, x) ≤ d(u, x) + d(v, y) ≤ d(u, v) + d(x, y), then
d(u, v) + d(x, y) − d(u, x) − d(v, y) ≤ 2δ. The hyperbolicity of G is the smallest δ such that it is
δ-hyperbolic. In what follows, we gather some properties of δ-hyperbolic graphs.
   The first result is a relaxation of a well-known Helly property for trees: every family of
pairwise intersecting subtrees (and so, every family of pairwise intersecting balls in a tree) has
a nonempty common intersection.
Lemma 2.3 (see Lemma 1 in [17]). If G isTδ-hyperbolic, then for any family of pairwise inter-
secting balls Bρ1 (v1 ), Bρ2 (v2 ), . . . , Bρk (vk ), {Bρi +2δ (vi ) : 1 ≤ i ≤ k} ̸= ∅.
  The next result is a special case of Morse lemma [45], of which we give a direct proof. Recall
that in a tree, there exists a unique path between every two nodes. Roughly, we relax this
property for a δ-hyperbolic graph such that almost shortest-paths between every two vertices
must stay close to each other.
Lemma 2.4. Let u, v, x, y be vertices in a δ-hyperbolic graph G. If u ∈ I(x, y), d(x, v)+d(v, y) ≤
d(x, y) + λ, and d(v, x) ≥ d(u, x), d(v, y) ≥ d(u, y), then d(u, v) ≤ λ + 2δ.
Proof. Since G is δ-hyperbolic, d(u, v) + d(x, y) ≤ max{d(u, x) + d(v, y), d(u, y) + d(v, x)} + 2δ.
Therefore, d(u, v) + d(x, y) ≤ max{d(u, x), d(v, x)} + max{d(u, y), d(v, y)} + 2δ ≤ d(v, x) +
d(v, y) + 2δ ≤ d(x, y) + λ + 2δ. The latter implies as claimed that d(u, v) ≤ λ + 2δ.            □
   Finally, observe that for any subtree H in a tree (in particular, for any ball in a tree), the nodes
out of H can be partitioned in rooted subtrees with their roots being taken in the boundary of
H. In particular, every node y out of H has a unique closest node v in H (namely, the root of
its subtree), and the unique path from y to any node of H goes by v. We prove a relaxation of
this simple property for balls in a δ-hyperbolic graph, namely:

                                                                                  / Br (u), then
for any v ∈ Sr (u, y), d(x, v) + d(v, y) ≤ d(x, y) + 2δ.
Proof. Consider the three sums A = d(u, y) + d(v, x), B = d(u, v) + d(x, y), and C = d(u, x) +
d(v, y). Since v ∈ Sr (u, y), we get that d(u, v) = r ≥ d(u, x). Furthermore, d(y, z) ≥ d(y, v)

                                      4. Exact algorithms
   We present (almost) linear-time algorithms for the Weighted Center problem. With the
notable exception of Sec. 4.5, all the results in what follows are based on our Theorem A, used
in combination with other properties of the classes of graphs considered. Furthermore, for the
classes of graphs with unbounded hyperbolicity, we present parameterized algorithms, where the
parameter is the hyperbolicity value.
4.1. Bounded-degree graphs. The growth function of a graph G = (V, E) is defined as γG :
ρ ∈ N 7→ max{|Bρ (v)| : v ∈ V }. Note that γG (ρ) = O(∆ρ ) if G has maximum degree ∆.
A graph G of polynomial growth is one such that γG (ρ) = O(ρα ), for some fixed exponent α.
See [43], and the references therein, for previous studies on the graphs of polynomial growth.
Finally, a graph G has doubling dimension β if every ball in G of positive radius is included in
the union of at most 2β balls with half-radius. Then, γG (ρ) = O(ρβ ).
Theorem 4.1. For any δ-hyperbolic graph G, and for any profile π, the Weighted Center
problem can be solved in O((γG (O(δ)) + δ log2 n)m) time, with γG the growth function. In
particular, this algorithm runs

4.2. Chordal graphs, and weakly bridged graphs. Recall that a chordal graph is one with
no induced cycle of length more than three. A graph G = (V, E) is called weakly bridged if it
has convex balls and if any of its induced cycles of length five is contained in the neighborhood
of some vertex. Note that chordal graphs are weakly bridged. However, chordal graphs are
1-hyperbolic [9], whereas the hyperbolicity of weakly bridged graphs is unbounded [39]. The
properties of weakly bridged graphs were studied in [18]. In particular, they satisfy the following
Triangle Condition:
      • (TC) If u ∼ v and d(u, w) = d(v, w), for some u, v, w ∈ V , then S1 (u, w) ∩ S1 (v, w) ̸= ∅.
We will need the following characterization of graphs with convex balls:
Lemma 4.2 (see Theorem 2 in [46]). All balls Bk (v) (v ∈ V , k ≥ 1) of a graph G are convex if
and only if G does not contain isometric cycles of length ℓ > 5, and for any two vertices x, y of
G the slice S1 (x, y) is a clique.

precise statements than the authors in [11], based on a careful analysis of their proofs. Let
G = (V, E) be weakly bridged, and let π be an arbitrary profile:

      • (WB.2) for any v ∈ V such that v is a local minimum of rπ in G, but v ∈             / Cπ (G), a

Lemma 4.3. Let G = (V, E) be weakly bridged, let π be an arbitrary profile, and let v ∈ V . If

4.3. (Bipartite) Helly graphs. We obtain almost optimal algorithms for the Weighted
Center problem on dually chordal graphs, and chordal bipartite graphs. A graph is dually
chordal if it is the intersection graph of the maximal cliques of some chordal graph. The same
as chordal graphs can be characterized by the existence of a perfect elimination ordering, the
dually chordal graphs can be also characterized by the existence of a special type of dismantling
      BEYOND TREES: THE WEIGHTED CENTER PROBLEM ON GROMOV HYPERBOLIC GRAPHS                        13

ordering [7]. A chordal bipartite graph is a bipartite graph with no induced cycle of length more
than four. Both the dually chordal graphs and the chordal bipartite graphs are 1-hyperbolic.
   We also consider some of their metric generalizations. Namely, a graph is called Helly if
every family of pairwise intersecting balls has a nonempty common intersection. A half-ball in a
bipartite graph is the intersection of a ball with one of its two partite sets. A bipartite graph is
called bipartite Helly if and only if every family of pairwise intersecting half-balls has a nonempty
common intersection. Interestingly, every (bipartite) graph G can be isometrically embedded in
a smallest (bipartite) Helly graph H(G), which is called its injective hull. Furthermore, dually
chordal graphs and chordal bipartite graphs are Helly and bipartite Helly, respectively.
Theorem 4.8. For a (bipartite) Helly graph, the Weighted Center problem can be solved
  (1) in O(δ 3 m log2 n) time, if it is δ-hyperbolic;

4.4. Distance-hereditary graphs. A graph G is called distance-hereditary if every induced
path is a shortest-path. Note that the distance-hereditary graphs are 1-hyperbolic [47].
Theorem 4.16. The Weighted Center problem can be solved in O(m) time for distance-
hereditary graphs.
   We prove new metric properties of distance-hereditary graphs, which we use in the design
of our O(m)-time algorithm. In particular, radius functions are 2-weakly peakless. Therefore,
a local-search algorithm for computing a center can be derived. To implement each step of
the local search in O(m) time, we use the injective hull. In fact, we need the result from [34]
that the injective hull of a distance-hereditary graph is both Helly and distance-hereditary.
Furthermore, [34] also provides an O(m)-time algorithm for computing the injective hull of a
distance-hereditary graph.

d(v0 , c) ≥ 3. We consider the largest index k such that d(vk′ , c) ≥ 3 for each k ′ between
0 and k. By Claim 4.25, d(·, c) is monotonically decreasing on the sub-path [v0 , v1 , . . . , vk ];
moreover, for any k ′ such that 0 ≤ k ′ ≤ k, c is also a closest center to vk′ . It implies
k = d(v0 , c) − d(vk , c) ≤ d(v0 , c) − 3. As before, we further assume k < s (for else, we
are done). By Claim 4.25, d(vk+1 , c) < d(vk , c) holds. The maximality of k implies that
d(vk+1 , c) = d(vk , c) − 1 = 2. If c is not a closest center to vk+1 , then, d(vk+1 , c′′ ) ≤ 1 holds
for some other c′′ ∈ Cπ (G), and so, by Claim 4.27, s ≤ k + 2 ≤ d(v0 , c) − 1. Otherwise, by
using the same arguments as above (where we replace the start vertex v0 with vk+1 ), we get
s − (k + 1) ≤ 6. Hence, s = k + 1 + (s − k − 1) ≤ (d(v0 , c) − 3) + 1 + 6 = d(v0 , c) + 4.          □
4.5. Planar graphs.
Theorem 4.28. There is an 2O(δ) n log10 n-time algorithm for the Weighted Center problem
on δ-hyperbolic planar graphs.
   Recall that a separator of a graph G is a subset S such that G \ S is disconnected. The
balance of a separator S is the largest α such that every connected component of G \ S contains
at most (1 − α)n vertices. Finally, a separator S is called isometric if it induces an isometric
(or distance-preserving) subgraph of G. Note that if S is an isometric separator, and C is any
connected component of G \ S, then C ∪ S also induces an isometric subgraph.
Lemma 4.29 (see Theorem 1 in [38]). If G is a planar δ-hyperbolic graph, then we can compute
in O(δ 2 n log4 n) time an isometric separator S such that
   (1) either S is a path of length O(δ 2 log n), and it has constant balance;
   (2) or S is a cycle of length O(δ), and it has balance at least 2O(δ)1log n .
   As it can be expected, our strategy for proving Theorem 4.28 consists in recursively discon-
necting a planar hyperbolic graph using Lemma 4.29. After each separation, for any connected
component C, we need to compute, for each vertex of C, its maximum weighted distance to the
vertices in the other connected components. For that, we use the algorithmic toolkit from [41],
which is based on VC-theory. In what follows, for any graph G, the boundary of an induced
subgraph H is defined as the subset of all the vertices of H with at least one neighbor in
V (G) \ V (H). We denote the boundary of H by ∂H. Furthermore, we assume for what follows
22                                                G. DUCOFFE

that ∂H is totally ordered: ∂H = ⟨s0 , s1 , . . . , s|∂H|−1 ⟩ (the ordering is arbitrary). Then, for any
vertex v, the pattern of v with respect to ∂H is the |∂H|-dimensional array pv such that, for
each i, pv [i] = d(v, si ) − d(v, s0 ). The following result is proved in [41] for Kh -minor-free graphs,
but we restate it only for planar graphs:

Lemma 4.30 (see Lemma 4 in [41]). Let H be a connected induced graph of some planar graph
G. The number of distinct patterns with respect to ∂H is in O(|∂H||V (H)|4 ).

  Since we are dealing with arbitrary profiles (not necessarily binary), in what follows we will
need to combine Lemma 4.30 with the following convex hull trick:

Lemma 4.31 (see Lemma 4.2 in [28]). Let F be a set of n linear functions fi : t 7→ ai · t + bi ,
where ai , bi ≥ 0. Then after an O(n log n)-time pre-processing, for any x ≥ 0 we can compute
max{ai · x + bi : 1 ≤ i ≤ n} in O(log n) time.

Proof of Theorem 4.28. Assume for what follows n ≥ 2Θ(δ) (otherwise, we solve the Weighted
Center problem by brute-force in O(n2 ) = 2O(δ) time). We apply Lemma 4.29 to compute an
isometric separator S in O(δ 2 n log4 n) time. Furthermore, as |S| = O(δ 2 log n), we can afford
to compute rπ (s), for each s ∈ S; it takes O(δ 2 n log n) time in total. For the other vertices,
let us consider the connected components C1 , C2 , . . . , Cq , q ≥ 2, of G \ S. For each i such
that 1 ≤ i ≤ q, let πi denote the restriction of π to Ci ∪ S. Then, for any vertex v of Ci ,
rπ (v) = max{rπi (v)} ∪ {π(u)d(v, u) : u ∈ / Ci ∪ S}. As Ci ∪ S induces an isometric subgraph
Gi of G, the graph Gi is also planar and δ-hyperbolic. In particular, all the values rπi (v), for
v ∈ Ci , can be computed by applying our algorithm recursively on Gi .
   For each i such that 1 ≤ i ≤ q, for each v ∈ Ci , define λ(v) = max{π(u)d(v, u) : u ∈
                                                                                       / Ci ∪ S}.
By the above, rπ (v) = max{rπi (v), λ(v)}. So, we are left to compute the values λ(v), for each
v ∈ V \ S. For that, for each j with 1 ≤ j ≤ ⌈log q⌉, let Xj , Yj be such that:
      • Xj contains all components Ci such that the j th bit of i is equal to 0;
      • Yj contains all components Ci such that the j th bit of i is equal to 1.
Note that for each pair of vertices u, v that are in different components of G \ S, there is
at least one j such that either u ∈ Xj , v ∈ Yj or u ∈ Yj , v ∈ Xj . Therefore, to compute
all values λ(v), it suffices to compute the following values λj (v) for any j: if v ∈ Xj , then
λj (v) = max{π(u)d(v, u) : u ∈ Yj }; and if v ∈ Yj , then λj (v) = max{π(u)d(v, u) : u ∈ Xj }. By
symmetry, it suffices to present an algorithm for computing all values λj (v), for v ∈ Xj .
   For that, let ∂S = s0 , s1 , . . . , s|∂S|−1 . We compute all profiles pu , for u ∈ Yj , with respect
to ∂S. This can be done in O(|∂S|n) = O(δ 2 n log n) by performing a BFS for each si ∈ ∂S.
Denote by PS the set of all distinct profiles with respect to ∂S. Note that PS can be computed
in O(|∂S|n log n) = O(δ 2 n log2 n) time, by sorting. Furthermore, by Lemma 4.30, it holds
|PS | = O(|∂S||S|4 ) = O(|S|5 ) = O(δ 10 log5 n). For each p ∈ PS , let Yj,p contain all the vertices
u ∈ Yj such that pu = p. For any v ∈ Xj , let λj,p (v) = max{π(u)d(v, u) : u ∈ Yj,p }. To compute
λj (v), it suffices to compute λj,p for each p ∈ PS .
   So, let p ∈ PS be fixed. For each k with 0 ≤ k ≤ |∂S| − 1, we define the family of functions
Fj,p,k = {t 7→ π(u) · t + π(u)d(sk , u) : u ∈ Yj,p }, for which we apply Lemma 4.31. It takes
O(|∂S|n log n) = O(δ 2 n log2 n) time in total. Then, we consider each v ∈ Xj sequentially. Let
k be such that d(v, sk ) + p[k] is minimum. We claim that for any u ∈ Yj,p , sk ∈ I(v, u). To
prove this claim, as any shortest (v, u)-path contains a vertex of ∂S, it suffices to prove that
d(v, sk ) + d(sk , u) is minimum. That is indeed the case because for any k ′ with 0 ≤ k ′ ≤ |∂S| − 1,
d(v, sk ) + d(sk , u) − d(s0 , u) = d(v, sk ) + p[k] ≤ d(v, sk′ ) + p[k ′ ] = d(v, sk′ ) + d(sk′ , u) − d(s0 , u).
Consequently, by Lemma 4.31 applied for Fj,p,k and x = d(v, sk ), we can compute λj,p (v) in
O(log n) time.
   Overall, the values λj,p (v), for v ∈ Xj , can be computed in O(δ 2 n log2 n) time. It implies
that the values λj (v), for v ∈ Xj , can be computed in O(|PS |δ 2 n log2 n) = O(δ 12 n log7 n) time.
Hence, the values λ(v), for v ∈ V \ S, can be computed in O(δ 12 n log8 n) time.

                                             5. Perspectives
   The main contribution of the paper is an algorithm for computing on any δ-hyperbolic graph
with m edges, for any profile π, some vertex at distance O(δ) to the center in Õ(δm) time
(Theorem 3.6). In doing so, we answer one of the questions left open in [11] (Question 10.1).
Our dependency on δ is linear, but it is quite large. Improving this constant could be an
interesting challenge.
   Based on this main result, a framework for solving the Weighted Center problem exactly
on some classes of hyperbolic graphs is proposed. It implies optimal, or almost optimal, algo-
rithms for the Weighted Center problem on chordal graphs, chordal bipartite graphs, dually
chordal graphs and distance-hereditary graphs. However, the inclusion to our framework of
AT-free graphs, graphs of bounded asteroidal number, and other classic examples of hyperbolic
graphs, remains to be done.
   Finally, we present parameterized almost linear-time algorithms for the Weighted Center
problem on planar graphs and several other classes of graphs, where the parameter is the hyper-
bolicity. We leave for future work whether similar hyperbolicity-based parameterizations can be
also achieved for some other distance-related problems on these classes of graphs.
