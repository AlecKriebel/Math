                                                  Abstract
          We construct a ﬁnite locally CAT(0) unit cube complex with one vertex, seven edges, eight
      squares, and two three-dimensional cubes, whose fundamental group is the mapping torus of
      a 7→ a, b 7→ ab, c 7→ bcb. Its universal cover gives a proper cocompact cubical action of the exact
      group in AIM Question 5.2. All attaching maps, signed vertex links, and reversible presentation
      changes are given explicitly. We also explain a qualitative existence deduction from earlier
      WPD, Morse, and cubical completion theorems. The contribution presented here is the explicit
      small ﬁnite model; no historical ﬁrstness or minimality claim is made.


1    The exact statement
Let F = F (a, b, c) and let φ ∈ Aut(F ) be given by

                                φ(a) = a,       φ(b) = ab,       φ(c) = bcb.

Indeed its inverse sends a to a, b to a−1 b, and c to b−1 acb−1 a; composition in either order freely
reduces to the respective basis generators. We use the convention

                    G = F ⋊ϕ Z = ha, b, c, t | tat−1 = a, tbt−1 = ab, tct−1 = bcbi.                         (1)

The ﬁnal b is positive. AIM Question 5.2, attributed to Rylee Lyman, asks whether this group
acts properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex [1]. The workshop report discusses the
diﬀerent formula b 7→ aba, c 7→ bcb and an earlier three-dimensional candidate [2, p. 2]. We keep
that related example distinct.

Theorem 1. There is a ﬁnite locally CAT(0) unit cube complex Z of dimension three with cell
counts (1, 7, 8, 2) and π1 Z ∼
                             = G. Consequently G acts freely, properly, and cocompactly by cubical
isometries on the CAT(0) cube complex e Z.

    Here a cube complex allows isometric identiﬁcations of boundary faces; characteristic cubes in
the quotient need not be embedded. This convention is essential for the stated cell counts. The
ﬂag-link criterion for this convention is Leary’s Theorem C.9 [6, p. 33]. His Theorem C.4 (p. 31)
ensures that a CAT(0) universal cover is cubical under the stricter embedded-cube convention;
Corollary C.11 (p. 33) gives a strict cubical quotient after the second cubical subdivision, if desired.
The counts in Theorem 1 are before subdivision.
    The construction uses a ﬁnite cyclic local isometry into a two-square base. We verify the quotient
links directly and identify the entire fundamental group. No minimum dimension, minimum cell

2    The two-square base and cyclic domain
In attaching words, capitals denote inverse oriented edges. Vertex names A, B, C, D are independent
of this convention. Let K have one vertex, three oriented unit edges x, y, b, and two unit squares
with boundary words
                                         xyXY,        bxBY.                                     (2)
Thus H = π1 K = hx, y, b | [x, y] = 1, bxb−1 = yi. For an edge h, write h+ and h− for the outgoing
and incoming germs. The link LK has six vertices and precisely the eight edges

               {xϵ , y δ }   (, δ ∈ {+, −}),        {b− , x+ }, {b− , x− }, {b+ , y + }, {b+ , y − }.   (3)

It is a simple bipartite graph, with parts {x+ , x− , b+ } and {y + , y − , b− }. It has no triangles and is
therefore ﬂag, so K is locally CAT(0).
    The assignments
                                λ(x) = y,    λ(y) = x,         λ(b) = B                                  (4)
extend to a cubical isometric involution of K. They take the two boundary words in (2) to yxY X
and BybX, respectively. The ﬁrst is a dihedral reparametrization of the ﬁrst square; the second
is a cyclic rotation of the inverse of the second boundary. The corresponding square isometries
agree with (4) on their faces. Their squares are the identity, so they deﬁne the claimed involution,
including on cells.
    Deﬁne Q by four distinct vertices A, B, C, D and the following six unit edges. The ﬁnal column
speciﬁes a cubical map j : Q → K.

                               edge      initial vertex   terminal vertex      j-label
                                p             B                   A               x
                                q             A                   C               y
                                r             D                   C               x
                                s             B                   D               y
                                d             A                   B               b
                                e             C                   A               b

Attach two squares with words

                             pqr−1 s−1     based at B,        dpe−1 q −1    based at A.                  (5)

Their characteristic vertex cycles are (B, A, C, D) and (A, B, A, C). In particular, the two occur-
rences of A in the second square must both be retained in corner calculations. The labels take
these boundaries to xyXY and bxBY , so j is deﬁned on squares by unit-square isometries.
   The complete signed links are given below. A path lists all link vertices and all edges; there are
no additional edges or higher simplices. The last two columns record their images in LK under the
two endpoint maps.

For example, the two A-corners of the second square supply the distinct edges {d+ , q + } and
{p− , e− }, while the ﬁrst square supplies {p− , q + }. Every row maps injectively to LK . Comparison
with (3) shows that each image is a full induced subcomplex: every base-link edge between listed
vertices is listed. Consequently Q is locally CAT(0), and j and λj are cubical local isometries, by
the injective-full-link characterization.
    The edges p, q, s form a spanning tree. On collapsing them, the ﬁrst square kills r and the
second gives d = e. Hence

                      π1 (Q, A) = hdpi ∼
                                       = Z,        j∗ (dp) = bx,       (λj)∗ (dp) = By.            (6)

The maps are injective on fundamental groups; alternatively bx has inﬁnite order because the
homomorphism H → Z sending b to 1 and x, y to 0 sends bx to 1.


3    The ﬁnite quotient and its ﬂag link
Form the double mapping cylinder
                                                                
                                          Z = K ∪j,λj Q × [0, 1] ,                                 (7)

where (q, 0) is attached by j and (q, 1) by λj. Give the interval length one and the product its
cubical structure. Face identiﬁcations come from the genuine product and the two cubical maps.
They need not be globally injective. Denote the vertical edges by zA , zB , zC , zD , directed from the
j end to the λj end. These are four distinct edges, although all their endpoints become the sole
vertex of K. The second product cube has zA at two distinct boundary positions, just as the second
square of Q has two A-corners.
    There are three base edges and four vertical edges, two base squares and six edge-product
squares, and two square-product three-cubes. Thus

                         (#Z (0) , #edges, #squares, #three-cubes) = (1, 7, 8, 2).                 (8)

The Euler characteristic is 1 − 7 + 8 − 2 = 0.
     Let L be the unique vertex link of Z. It consists of LK and eight cones with apices zv+ and
zv− , v ∈ {A, B, C, D}. Their bases are the j and λj link images in the table, respectively. This
description follows corner by corner from the product: an edge-product square adds an apex–base
edge, and a square-product cube adds the cone triangle over each corresponding square corner. No
two apices are adjacent, since a product-cube corner has only one interval direction. The eight
apices are distinct germs of the four vertical edges.
     The link maps in the table are injective, so no cone has repeated vertices, edges, or triangles.
Distinct cones have distinct apices. At a repeated A-corner the two triangles from the second prod-
uct cube have distinct base edges, as displayed above; they are not a repeated simplex. Therefore
L is an actual simplicial complex, not merely a graph with prescribed clique counts.
     If a clique of L contains no apex, it lies in the ﬂag complex LK . Otherwise it contains exactly
one apex. Its other vertices lie in the full cone base, and their base-link clique spans a simplex in

                                                      3
that base. Adjoining the apex gives a simplex in the cone. Thus L is ﬂag. Its counts provide an
additional check: it has 6 + 8 = 14 vertices, 8 + 2(4 + 3 + 3 + 2) = 32 edges, and 2(3 + 2 + 2 + 1) = 16
triangles, with no four-clique. Leary’s general cube-complex criterion [6, Theorem C.9, p. 33] now
shows that Z is locally CAT(0) and that its universal cover is CAT(0).


4    The fundamental group and geometric action
We record a direct presentation computation to verify the exact group, including the sign of the
last b. Besides (2), the six edge-product squares have relators
                                         −1             −1          −1
                                  xzA Y zB  ,     yzC XzA  , xzC Y zD  ,
                                        −1         −1                 −1
                                                                                                    (9)
                                  yzD XzB  , bzB bzA  ,         bzA bzC  .

Attaching three-cells does not change the fundamental group. Write z = zA . The p, q, s relations
eliminate the other vertical generators:

                            zB = xzY,           zC = Y zx,       zD = Y xzY x.                     (10)

The r relation is then redundant using xy = yx. The d relation is z −1 (bx)z = By; the e relation is
z −1 (yb)z = xB. These agree, because bxB = y gives yb = bx and xB = By. Each elimination is a
reversible Tietze transformation, so

                        π1 Z = hx, y, b, z | xy = yx, bxB = y, z −1 (bx)z = Byi.                   (11)

    Make the invertible change of generators

               t = x,   a = xy −1 ,   c = (zx)−1 ;        x = t,    y = a−1 t,   z = c−1 t−1 ,     (12)

leaving b ﬁxed. The ﬁrst two relations of (11) become [a, t] = 1 and btb−1 = a−1 t. The latter is
equivalent to tbt−1 = ab. For the third relation put u = Bx, v = bx. Since By = xB = xuX, its
successive equivalent forms are

                              z −1 vz = xux−1       ⇐⇒       (zx)u(zx)−1 = v
                                                    ⇐⇒       c−1 (b−1 t)c = bt
                                                    ⇐⇒       tc = bcbt
                                                    ⇐⇒       tct−1 = bcb.

Thus (11) becomes exactly (1). Explicit inverse substitutions and reversible relations establish an
isomorphism, rather than only a homomorphism obtained by checking relators in G.
    The cover Ze is three-dimensional with ﬁnite links and unit Euclidean cube shapes. It is locally
ﬁnite; its natural complete length metric is proper (equivalently, local compactness and complete-
ness give properness for this geodesic metric). The deck group acts freely by cubical isometries.
Covering neighborhoods show that this action is properly discontinuous, and, on the proper cover,
that it is a proper action: only ﬁnitely many translates of a compact set meet that set. Its quotient
is the ﬁnite complex Z, so the action is cocompact. The isomorphism above identiﬁes this deck
group with the full group G. This proves Theorem 1.

5    An existence deduction from earlier tools
We give a second route to qualitative existence to clarify the relation to prior methods. This route
obtains an unspeciﬁed ﬁnite cyclic local isometry by existing completion theorems; it does not
supply the cell counts (8).
     Write A0 = hx, y | [x, y] = 1i ∼= Z2 and H = hA0 , b | bxb−1 = yi. The HNN normal-form
theorem embeds A0 . In its Bass–Serre tree, vertices are left cosets hA0 , and an oriented edge e
goes from A0 to bA0 , with stabilizer hyi. Set g = bx. The homomorphism b : H → Z, with
b (b) = 1 and b (x) = b (y) = 0, gives a height on vertices. Since gA0 = bA0 , the edges g n e
concatenate, increasing height at every step. They form a nonbacktracking bi-inﬁnite axis of g. At
A0 , the outgoing edge has stabilizer hyi and the incoming edge has stabilizer
                                    g −1 hyig = x−1 b−1 hyibx = hxi.
Their intersection is trivial in A0 . Hence the pointwise stabilizer of the axis segment from g −1 A0
to gA0 is trivial. Minasyan–Osin [7, Corollary 4.3], in the corrected v6, gives WPD for this speciﬁc
loxodromic g. No ﬁnite-extension claim is used.
    Dahmani–Guirardel–Osin [4, Theorem 6.8 and Corollary 2.9] provide a virtually cyclic hy-
perbolically embedded subgroup E(g) containing g. As b (g) = 1, g has inﬁnite order. Sisto’s
Theorem 1 [8] then makes g Morse; in particular C = hgi is quasiconvex in the word metric on H.
The metric required for completion matches exactly: K has one vertex, so K     e (1) is the Cayley graph
for {x, y, b}, with its edge metric. The two-edge circle Γ → K reading bx realizes the inclusion
C ,→ H, and its lifted image contains C and lies within distance one of it. Geodesics between its
vertices also stay uniformly close, by adding endpoint paths of length at most one and using the
Morse property. Thus no conversion from CAT(0)-metric quasiconvexity is being assumed.
    Ben-Zvi–Kropholler–Lyman [3, Theorems 1.1 and 5.1] now give a ﬁnite pointed locally CAT(0)
cube complex Q0 and a cubical local isometry j0 : Q0 → K, through which Γ → K factors, whose
fundamental-group image is exactly C. Their maximal-tree generating set is {x, y, b} here. Local-
isometry injectivity gives π1 Q0 ∼  = C, retaining the generator bx, rather than a power of it. The
original bx circle itself is not a local isometry: at its intermediate vertex the germs b− , x+ span a
base-link edge. Completion is what repairs that defect.
    Postcomposition with the explicit involution (4) gives the second local isometry λj0 on the same
cubical domain, taking the generator to By. Hence M = K ∪j0 ,λj0 (Q0 × [0, 1]) is ﬁnite. Its links
have the same general structure as those of Z: each interval germ cones oﬀ an injective full link
image, and distinct interval germs are not adjacent. The clique argument in Section 3 proves that
these links are ﬂag. This also gives a direct veriﬁcation of the local-isometry gluing discussed in
Hagen–Wise [5, Remark 3.7]. Because a local isometry into K has domain dimension at most two,
dim M ≤ 3. Van Kampen gives (11), and (12) identiﬁes π1 M with G. Its ﬁnite locally CAT(0)
quotient therefore gives another proper cocompact action of the exact group.
    The abstract tools in this deduction are prior work. Their availability does not date the discovery
or publication of this exact application. The present note makes the element, metric, compatible
maps, and group identiﬁcation explicit. Hagen–Wise’s current general fast-automorphism result [5,
Corollary 7.2] supplies free cubical actions; properness and cocompactness require the additional
argument above or the explicit model of Sections 2–4.
