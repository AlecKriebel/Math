A fixed-area exceptional set for positive squared-pole sums

By Alec Kriebel, independent researcher · 17 October 2026 · Version 1.0.0 · Unrefereed preprint

A positive sum of squared poles can have singularities scattered anywhere in the complex plane. This note shows that removing one compact set of fixed area controls the integral over every disk centered at the origin. The set depends on the sum, and the same set works for all radii greater than one.

One set for every radius

Take any finite number n ≥ 1 of complex poles zj, allowing repeated poles, and strictly positive real weights λj whose sum is 1. Set

g(z) = ∑j=1n λj / (z − zj)².

There is a compact set S(g) of planar area exactly π such that, simultaneously for every real R > 1,

∫DR ∖ S(g) |g(z)| dA ≤ 2π log R + π(log 2 + 1/e).

Here DR = {z : |z| < R}, dA = dx dy is ordinary unnormalized planar area, and log is the natural logarithm. These are ordinary finite nonnegative Lebesgue integrals. The set is chosen before R, contains a neighborhood of each pole, and need not be connected. There is no restriction on pole locations.

The leading coefficient 2π is sharp: no smaller coefficient works for every such sum with a finite absolute additive constant. The displayed additive constant is not claimed optimal. This gives an affirmative deduction for Baernstein’s question, Hayman–Lingham Problem 7.37.

How the fixed set is obtained

The construction combines two established results. Levine–Peres’s quadrature, or smash-sum, construction combines disks centered at the poles, with areas πλj, into a bounded open set Ω of area π and null boundary. Its closure S is compact with the same area and contains those disks. Harmonic averages over Ω reproduce the weighted values at the poles.

Applying that identity to the real and imaginary parts of (w − z)−2 identifies the field outside S with −T1Ω, where T is the Beurling transform with factor −1/π. The identity holds on every component outside S, including bounded holes. The contained disks keep the poles away from the integration region, giving ordinary finite integrals.

For a given R, split the already fixed Ω at radius √2 R. The Eremenko–Hamilton indicator estimate controls the near part. Direct integration of the separated far kernel controls the remainder. If a is the near area, the combined bound is

2a log R + π log 2 + a log(π/a) ≤ 2π log R + π(log 2 + 1/e), for 0 ≤ a ≤ π.

The endpoint values are understood continuously. Only the split changes with R. For sharpness, the single pole g(z) = z−2 already forces an outside integral at least 2π log R for any measurable exceptional set of area π.

Established inputs and priority limits

This is a target-specific deduction. It uses the Eremenko–Hamilton indicator-transform inequality (1995) and the Levine–Peres quadrature/smash-sum construction, available in their 1 May 2009 author manuscript and published in 2010. Earlier Astala, Sakai and partial-balayage antecedents remain credited. No new general singular-integral theorem, quadrature construction or balayage/operator mechanism is claimed.

The relevant conformal-on-E indicator bound is unaffected by the later Eremenko–Hamilton correction to a combined general-distortion statement. The note’s source comparison used the relevant publisher preview of the 2002 correction; the full chapter was unavailable. Related work by Yongxi Lin and André Guerra is credited without treating its operator conclusions as a recorded full answer to the fixed-set, all-radius question.

Historical novelty, worldwide first discovery/publication and current global openness are not certified. Original manuscript abstract

For every finite positive sum g(z)=∑jλj(z−zj)−2 with ∑jλj=1, we construct a compact set S(g) of area exactly π such that

∫{|z|<R}∖S(g)|g(z)| dA ≤ 2π log R+π(log 2+e−1) (R>1).

The same set serves every radius, with no restriction on the poles. This gives an affirmative deduction for Baernstein's question, Hayman–Lingham Problem 7.37, from the established Eremenko–Hamilton indicator estimate and Levine–Peres quadrature-set construction. The logarithmic coefficient is sharp; no optimality is claimed for the additive constant. Historical first priority is not certified. AI tools were used extensively; this note is unrefereed.
