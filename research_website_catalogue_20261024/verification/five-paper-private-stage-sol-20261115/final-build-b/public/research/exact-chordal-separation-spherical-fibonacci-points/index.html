Exact chordal separation of spherical Fibonacci points

Alec Kriebel · ORCID 0009-0001-9320-500X
Published 5 October 2026 · Zenodo version 1.0 · Unrefereed preprint

This note proves an exact minimum distance for a spherical Fibonacci configuration. The north pole and the next point are the unique closest pair, for every Fibonacci number of points from two onward. The proof controls every pair, including points far apart in the defining order.

Abstract

We prove the exact finite separation conjecture stated in Brauchart’s 2012 Oberwolfach contribution: the rational, unshifted spherical Fibonacci configuration with Fₙ points has minimum Euclidean chord 2/√Fₙ for every n ≥ 3. A latitude–longitude inequality reduces the lower bound to the classical product separation of Fibonacci lattices. We include an elementary proof of the weaker arithmetic estimate needed here, making the argument self-contained.

The exact point set and result

Let F₀ = 0, F₁ = F₂ = 1 and Fₙ₊₁ = Fₙ + Fₙ₋₁. For every integer n ≥ 3, set q = Fₙ and p = Fₙ₋₁. On the unit sphere, use the points

zk = (2√[(k/q)(1 − k/q)] cos(2πpk/q),
      2√[(k/q)(1 − k/q)] sin(2πpk/q), 1 − 2k/q),   0 ≤ k < q.

The minimum Euclidean chord is 2/√Fₙ, uniquely attained by the unordered pair {z₀, z₁}.

A chord is the straight-line distance in three-dimensional space. The corresponding shortest distance along the unit sphere is 2 arcsin(1/√Fₙ), in radians. These are two different metrics.

Why the proof works

The pole and the next point already give the stated distance. The central task is to prove that no other pair is closer. A global latitude–longitude inequality links spherical distance to an integer separation product from the Fibonacci lattice. An elementary argument using a unimodular Fibonacci basis proves the arithmetic bound needed here. Together, the inequalities give the lower bound for all pairs and identify the only equality case.

The supporting archive includes exact arithmetic and rational chord certificates, coordinate-convention controls and reproduction instructions. These finite checks supplement the written proof of the theorem for every n ≥ 3.

Conventions and limits

The formula uses exact rational longitudes 2πpk/q and unshifted heights 1 − 2k/q with the half-open range 0 ≤ k < q. It includes the north pole and excludes the south pole. At n = 2 there is only one point and no pair.

The alternative range 1 ≤ k ≤ q gives an isometric set: relabel k as q − k and apply (x, y, z) ↦ (x, −y, −z). Its closest labels are {zq−1, zq}. Including both endpoints instead produces a different set of q + 1 points. The reversed planar coordinate order used in the earlier lattice construction also gives an isometric unordered set, after k ≡ pj (mod q); the identity p² ≡ (−1)ⁿ (mod q) requires a reflection of the second spherical coordinate when n is odd.

The theorem does not cover midpoint-shifted heights, an irrational golden angle, arbitrary point counts or arbitrary coprime multipliers. It makes no claim about discrepancy or optimal packing among all point sets on the sphere. An equal-area map is not assumed to preserve distances.

Prior work and related manuscripts

The configuration and the pole upper bound are established ingredients. The exact separation question appears in Johann S. Brauchart’s 2012 Oberwolfach contribution, especially page 2438, published in 2013. The earlier spherical lattice construction is discussed by Aistleitner, Brauchart and Dick. The classical Fibonacci product index is due to S. K. Zaremba, with an explicit modern statement in Suryanarayana, Cools and Nuyens, Theorem 2.2. This note supplies the all-pair spherical lower bound and its equality analysis.

A later manuscript, Alper Ferudun’s The Minimum Distance of Unshifted Spherical Fibonacci Lattices, version 1.0, dated 27 October 2026, states the same exact minimum and unique closest pair and gives a broader rational-lattice criterion using the Zaremba index. That date follows this note’s 5 October 2026 date. The archive chronology alone establishes neither independence nor influence nor absolute priority.

The note’s historical coverage leaves the fate of the cited Brauchart–Dick work in preparation and some final graphics editions unverified. It does not establish worldwide first priority or certify that the question remained open in 2026.
