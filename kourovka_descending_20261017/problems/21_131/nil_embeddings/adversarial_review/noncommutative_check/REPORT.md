## 1. Elementary identities and finite generation

Write e_ij(a) = I+aE_ij over the unitization R. For distinct i,j,k, put A=aE_ik and T=bE_kj. Then A^2=T^2=TA=0, AT=ab E_ij, ATA=ATT=0, so

    (I+A)(I+T)(I-A)(I-T) = I+AT = e_ij(ab).

This explicitly fixes the order as **ab**, with no exchange of coefficients. The additive identity follows from E_ij^2=0. For every ordered pair i != j, a positive-degree generator word can be built inductively: if w=w's_l, choose the third index k and use [e_ik(w'),e_kj(s_l)] = e_ij(w). All ordered pairs are available simultaneously at the induction step. Products in one root subgroup then form arbitrary F_p-linear combinations. Powers implement scalar multiplication even when p=2. Thus <X> contains every e_ij(b), b in B.

Every r in R has a unique form lambda+b. Since e_ij(lambda+b)=e_ij(lambda)e_ij(b), E=<Q,X>. This proof needs the field to be the prime field F_p; it does not silently extend to an arbitrary field from only the listed X.

The set S={qxq^-1:q in Q,x in X} is finite. For q_0 in Q,

    q_0(qxq^-1)q_0^-1 = (q_0q)x(q_0q)^-1 in S.

Hence Q normalizes L=<S>. Elements of L normalize L automatically, and E=<Q,L>, so E normalizes L. This is full normality, not merely normalization by the scalar subgroup.

Let N be the E-normal closure of S_B={e_ij(b):b in B}. Because S_B is contained in L and L is E-normal, N is contained in L. Conversely X is contained in S_B, so every qxq^-1 is in N and L is contained in N. This establishes L=N without a congruence-kernel equality.

## 2. The diagonal factorization over a noncommutative ring

For any unit u, multiplication of the upper-left two-by-two blocks gives

    e_12(u)e_21(-u^-1)e_12(u) = [[0,u],[-u^-1,0]] = w(u),
    e_12(-1)e_21(1)e_12(-1) = [[0,-1],[1,0]] = w(-1),
    w(u)w(-1) = diag(u,u^-1).

Only uu^-1=u^-1u=1 is used. The third diagonal entry remains 1. If u=1+b with b in B, nilness of b gives u^-1=1-b+b^2-... as a finite sum, hence u^-1-1 belongs to B. Since L contains all B-elementary matrices and is E-normal, the quotient E/L may be used. In that quotient the six factors above can be replaced by their values at u=1; their product is I. Thus D(u) is in L. No assertion that D is multiplicative is needed or true generally.

## 3. A genuine embedding of U'

The inverse of D(uv) has diagonal entries ((uv)^-1,uv,1). Keeping all orders unchanged gives

    D(u)D(v)D(uv)^-1 = diag(1,u^-1 v^-1 u v,1).

Under the stated convention [a,b]=aba^-1b^-1, the second entry is [u^-1,v^-1]. Because inversion bijects U, every commutator [a,b] occurs: take u=a^-1 and v=b^-1. Therefore every matrix diag(1,[a,b],1) belongs to L. Since

    diag(1,h,1) diag(1,k,1) = diag(1,hk,1),

the set of h for which diag(1,h,1) is in L is a subgroup containing all commutators. It consequently contains U'. The map h -> diag(1,h,1) is a homomorphism, and its kernel is trivial by the middle matrix entry. This also handles derived-group elements that are products of multiple commutators.

## 4. The canonical nil algebra and its nonnilpotence

For q a scalar matrix and x=e_ij(s_l),

    qxq^-1-I = q(s_l E_ij)q^-1 = s_l(qE_ijq^-1).

The last expression is interpreted entrywise; scalar coefficients in F_p commute with s_l, so all entries are in B. Each difference is even square-zero, because q(s_lE_ij)^2q^-1=0. This stronger fact does not imply that their generated algebra is nilpotent.

By stable nilness M_3(B) is nil, and therefore its subalgebra C generated by the finitely many differences is nil. The ambient identity is I_3, and the usual multiplication rule

    (I+c)(I+d)=I+(c+d+cd)

keeps the product in I+C. For c^n=0, (I+c)^-1=I-c+c^2-...+(-c)^(n-1), so this is a group. Consequently the matrix group L generated by the displayed conjugates is exactly the subgroup generated by I+c_i inside I+C.

Suppose C^N=0. Words of lengths 1 through N-1 in finitely many generators span C, so C has finite dimension. Over the finite field F_p, C and I+C are finite sets. This contradicts the already established containment of infinite H in L. Hence C is not nilpotent.

For any c in C, choose p^m >= its nilpotence exponent. Because I and c commute, in characteristic p

    (I+c)^(p^m)=I+c^(p^m)=I.

Every cyclic subgroup of I+C is therefore finite. Since L contains infinite H, L is not cyclic. Removing repetitions or identity entries from the finite displayed list does not change either its generated group or its generated algebra; the remaining list must have at least two entries. The free nonunital algebra on that list maps onto C, with quotient precisely C. The group is literally the subgroup generated by the associated units in that quotient.
