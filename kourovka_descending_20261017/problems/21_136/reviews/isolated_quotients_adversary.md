## 1. Independent reconstruction of the local quotient lemma

Let U be an isolating open neighborhood of x and let pi:G to Q=G/A be the quotient. Suppose b=pi(x) has finite order r.

Every element of pi(U) is torsion. Indeed an infinite-order y there has a lift u in U, necessarily infinite-order, and isolation makes u conjugate to x. Its image would then be conjugate to finite-order b, a contradiction. The argument asserts only that this particular neighborhood consists of torsion; it does not assert that Q is torsion.

Let D=C_Q(b). This is a closed profinite subgroup. Choose a subgroup K open in D with bK contained in pi(U). Such K exists because pi(U) is an open neighborhood of b. If k is in K, then b and bk commute and both have finite order. Thus k has finite order, for example k^(rs)=1 if (bk)^s=1. Consequently K is a torsion subgroup. Since K has finite index in D, every element of D has a positive power in K and is itself torsion. One can use the finite-index core for uniform membership; no normality of K or uniform torsion exponent is required. K is not assumed open in Q.

Choose open normal N in G with xN contained in U, and let e be the finite order of xN. The ordinary integer n=1+er is greater than 1, satisfies n=1 modulo r, and gives x^n in U. The element x^n is infinite-order, so isolation provides g with x^g=x^n. Its quotient image centralizes b because b^n=b. The preceding centralizer argument therefore gives an m>0 with g^m in A.

Set a=x^r. It is an infinite-order element of A, and repeated conjugation gives

    a^(g^m) = a^(n^m).

Suppose L is an abstract locally nilpotent subgroup of finite index in A. Take positive s,t with a^s and g^(mt) in L. These powers exist from the finite permutation action on cosets of L, or its abstract finite-index core. This uses no topological property of L. With c=a^s, h=g^(mt), and d=n^(mt)>1, the elements c,h belong to L and satisfy c^h=c^d; c remains infinite-order.

The abstract two-generator subgroup generated by c,h is nilpotent of some finite class k. With [u,v]=u^-1 u^v, direct induction gives [c,_j h]=c^((d-1)^j). Its k-fold commutator is trivial, because its weight is k+1. Thus c is killed by the nonzero ordinary integer (d-1)^k, contradicting infinite order. This avoids any inference from a nilpotent element of a finite primary residue ring.

Hence b is infinite-order. Finally pi(U) is open, and any infinite-order element of it has an infinite-order lift in U. Its conjugacy to x descends to conjugacy with b, proving relative isolation in the quotient.

If A is torsion, the image b cannot be finite-order at all: x^r in A would make x finite-order. The same final open-map argument preserves isolation. This separately verifies the torsion-kernel case.

## 2. Quotient series and arbitrary cardinality

For a finite series 1=A_0 through A_n=G of closed **G-normal** subgroups, each step G/A_i to G/A_(i+1) has kernel A_(i+1)/A_i. If every such kernel is torsion or virtually abstract locally nilpotent, the proposition makes a relatively isolated infinite-order element persist through all steps. The trivial final quotient gives the contradiction. This is a finite induction, not an argument through an infinite inverse system or an arbitrary merely subnormal series.

For the original small-class hypothesis at arbitrary cardinality, the countably based quotient preserving an infinite-order element is legitimately available from the earlier checked construction. Images of the A_i are closed and normal. The factor between successive images is a continuous homomorphic image of A_(i+1)/A_i. Images of torsion groups are torsion. A homomorphic image of an abstract locally nilpotent group is abstract locally nilpotent, and its index in the corresponding image cannot increase. Thus the required finite series survives, including the possibility of repeated terms.

The global class bound also survives the quotient. Its nonempty Polish infinite-order class space is then countable and has an isolated point. The finite-series obstruction rules that out. No countable-base assumption is imposed on the original G and no CH is used. Finite-derived-length soluble groups have the required characteristic closed derived series, but arbitrary prosoluble groups need not have finite derived length.

## 3. The resulting metabelian subgroup is legitimate but insufficient

Without any restriction on A, the temporary assumption that b is finite yields infinite-order a in A, h in A, and an ordinary integer d>1 with a^h=a^d. Let C be the procyclic closure of a. Then C^h is contained in C. This inclusion is equality: in every finite quotient of G the two images are conjugate finite subgroups of the same size, so inclusion makes them equal; residual separation of closed subgroups then gives equality in G. Equivalently, conjugacy forces d to be a unit on the supernatural order of C.

Consequently C is normal in the closed two-generator subgroup H generated by a,h. The quotient H/C is procyclic, and C is abelian, so H is metabelian. Closedness of A ensures H is contained in A. This checks the topological closure assertion, rather than assuming that an ascending power inclusion can be proper inside a profinite group.

Neither H nor an arbitrary closed subgroup inherits P0, the small ambient class bound, or the required local torsion condition. Relative isolation in G supplies no such inheritance. The source explicitly avoids applying the metabelian P0 theorem to H, which is correct. Compact affine groups realize the power relation when appropriate p-adic units are used; that relation alone is not a contradiction.

## 4. Additional checks of the Lie companion

The abelian-kernel theorem is the case L=A of the local proposition. Its alternative norm-product proof also works: the displayed product for (bv)^r has conjugates of v by powers of b, each acting on a by the same integer power, so multiplying the actions gives the stated power n^(sr). The product element has finite order because bv lies in the specified torsion neighborhood. No abelian quotient or bounded quotient exponent is assumed.

The virtually soluble induction correctly takes the core of an open soluble subgroup, then its last nontrivial closed derived subgroup. That subgroup is abelian, characteristic in the core, and hence normal in G. Successive quotients lower the finite derived length and end in a finite quotient. A finite nonsoluble quotient causes no problem.

The following further local constraints are valid:

* Torsion orders are unbounded in every neighborhood of an isolated infinite-order element. Otherwise one finite exponent would kill the dense torsion subset near x, and closedness of that exponent set would kill x. Alternatively, removing that closed torsion set would make the infinite-order class have interior, which is impossible by the finite-quotient centralizer bound.
* An open torsion-free subgroup rules out isolation: pass to its normal core; every finite cyclic subgroup injects into the finite quotient, bounding all torsion orders.
* The procyclic closure of x has finite prime support. If infinitely many primes occur, a neighborhood leaves an entire prime component unrestricted. Deleting it gives a nearby infinite-order element with a different supernatural procyclic order, impossible for a conjugate. The invariant here is supernatural order, not merely the ordinary designation "infinite order."
* Isolation restricts to an open subgroup containing x after shrinking the neighborhood. There are finitely many compact orbits of that subgroup on x^G, so the other orbits can be removed. Thus the normalizer of the procyclic closure cannot be open: its closed normal abelian subgroup C would kill x in the quotient, contradicting the abelian-kernel theorem. Closedness of the normalizer then makes its index infinite.

### Small normalizer elements and small conjugators

For C isomorphic to Z_p, isolation makes the normalizer action image contain a principal unit neighborhood. The sequence of small normalizer elements asserted in the companion can be constructed even without a countable neighborhood basis: choose a normalizer element acting by a nontrivial principal unit lambda, take its p-primary component in its procyclic closure (all other primary components act trivially on this pro-p unit subgroup), and use its successive p-power powers. These converge to 1 and act by the infinite-order units lambda^(p^j). Thus x times these elements tends to x and is infinite-order.

In the countably based setting, the companion's subsequence argument chooses conjugators tending to 1: for every open normal M, the compact M-orbit x^M is relatively open in the finite partition of x^G into M-orbits. In arbitrary cardinality the corresponding simultaneous choice uses a net, as the companion explicitly notes. A sequence of arbitrary shrinking neighborhoods must not be presumed cofinal in a nonmetrizable group. The explicit p-primary sequence above removes that potential ambiguity for the first, normalizer-element sequence.

### Prior-theorem control using generalized Higman groups

The primary [Glebsky preprint, arXiv:1604.06359v1](https://arxiv.org/html/1604.06359v1) was independently reopened. Its introduction states the existence of arbitrarily large p-quotients for p dividing k-1 and separation on the adjacent Baumslag–Solitar subgroups; Corollary 1 provides the quoted finite p-group quotients. In particular generator orders are unbounded, so their images have infinite order in the pro-p completion. The cyclic permutation of the four generators preserves the presentation and extends continuously to that completion. At p=2, adjoining its order-four action is a pro-2 extension and makes the relevant adjacent generators conjugate, while retaining their power-conjugation relation.

This validates the stated use as a control against forbidding one fixed conjugate power-conjugation relation in all profinite groups. It does not provide local isolation or the uniform small-conjugator relations. This audit checked the cited prior theorem's statement and the deduction from it; it did not reproduce the whole ring-theoretic proof, and the new local quotient proposition does not depend on that theorem.

The affine pronilpotent control is also correct. In Z_3 semidirect Z_3 with multiplier 4^(-s), conjugation by h=(0,1) sends a=(1,0) to a^4, and the repeated commutators are a^(3^j), nontrivial for every j. Thus convergence of commutators to 1 in a pro-p group cannot replace termination in an abstract nilpotent subgroup.
