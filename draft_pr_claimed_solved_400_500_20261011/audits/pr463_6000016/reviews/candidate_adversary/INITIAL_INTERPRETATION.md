The target is understood provisionally as the following universal statement: a compact affine manifold with a positive-definite locally Hessian metric continues to admit some such metric under every sufficiently small deformation of its affine structure. It does not require a global potential or a continuously selected metric unless the primary source says so. One smooth family of affine structures on the same compact manifold, with Hessian central member and arbitrarily close non-Hessian members, refutes this universal statement. The primary item and surrounding restrictions must still be read.

The proposed mechanism can be independently tested on the fixed smooth torus R^2/Z^2 using a torsion-free flat connection with the sole coefficient D_X X = t Y, for globally descending commuting coordinate fields X and Y. Constant coefficients make smooth dependence on t immediate. Flatness is plausible because the sole connection endomorphism is nilpotent and the others vanish, but will be derived directly. If a positive Hessian metric has periodic components A=g(X,X), B=g(X,Y), C=g(Y,Y), the Codazzi equation should force B_x - A_y = t C. Integrating this equality over the torus gives 0=t integral(C), which contradicts positive definiteness when t is nonzero. This would address all smooth metrics, not just invariant or parallel metrics. Signs, tensor indices, and the existence of globally descending fields/forms must be checked explicitly.

Potential falsifiers: the original question restricts to fixed holonomy, hyperbolic convex domains, or another topology that excludes this family; the family is only continuous in a required smooth moduli coordinate; the connection does not define the intended affine deformation; local Hessian means something stronger or weaker; Codazzi has a missing connection term; a metric fails periodicity only because of a gauge choice; or the integral argument assumes an invariant metric. Indefinite metrics and universal-cover metrics do not refute a positive-definite compact-quotient claim, but must be documented as boundaries.

Priority remains entirely undecided. The mere existence of a classical affine torus family does not prove earlier nonexistence of arbitrary Hessian metrics. A historical theorem could, however, bridge that central gap if its exact hypotheses apply. I will distinguish this possibility from a first-solution claim and from the shorter present proof.
