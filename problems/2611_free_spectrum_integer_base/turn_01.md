# Attempt 1 — finite joins, nilpotent bounds, and the minimal obstruction

This is the first substantive author proof attempt. Retrieval and source checking were completed separately. Let F_G(n) be the free group of rank n in var(G), and put a_G(n)=log|F_G(n)|, with natural logarithms. The task asks whether a_G(n)^(1/n) always converges to an integer. No general solution is obtained here.

## 1. Pointwise join inequalities

Suppose var(G) is the join of var(H_1),...,var(H_t). In the absolutely free group X_n let K_i be the subgroup of words that vanish identically on H_i. A word vanishes on the join exactly when it vanishes on all H_i, so the identity kernel for G is the intersection of the K_i. Thus the diagonal map gives an embedding

F_G(n) -> product_i F_{H_i}(n),

and the maps onto each factor are surjective. Consequently

max_i a_{H_i}(n) <= a_G(n) <= sum_i a_{H_i}(n).                (1)

If all the individual root limits L_i exist, (1) and the inequality sum_i a_i <= t max_i a_i prove that the root limit for G exists and equals max_i L_i. This includes zero terms from trivial varieties. This is a pointwise strengthening, for purposes of proving convergence, of the potential/limsup join formula already credited to Olshanskii, Lemma 3. It is not a novelty claim.

## 2. Direct elementary proof for a nontrivial nilpotent generator

Let G be finite of nilpotency class c and exponent e. Both properties are identities, so F_G(n) has class at most c and exponent dividing e. Its lower central factor of weight j is generated by at most n^j simple commutators of weight j. Each such factor is abelian of exponent dividing e. Hence

|F_G(n)| <= e^(n+n^2+...+n^c).                              (2)

For a lower bound, G contains an element of prime order p, so C_p belongs to var(G), and F_G(n) surjects onto C_p^n. Therefore

n log p <= a_G(n) <= (n+n^2+...+n^c) log e.

Taking nth roots proves the limit is 1. For G=1 it is 0. This is a rederivation of the known nilpotent case, included to control convergence rather than only the limsup.

## 3. Quotient-domination squeeze

Suppose G has an abelian normal subgroup M and the group Q=G/M is monolithic with nonabelian monolith. Olshanskii's Theorem 1 supplies constants c>0 and N such that a_Q(n)>=c|Q|^n for n>=N. Quotient monotonicity gives a_G(n)>=a_Q(n). Proposition 1, in its explicit proof, supplies a_G(n)<=C n |Q|^n for a fixed C. Thus

c|Q|^n <= a_G(n) <= Cn|Q|^n,

so the full limit for G is |Q|. This also handles central and noncentral abelian extensions of such Q. No splitting hypothesis is needed.

## 4. A least-order counterexample is sharply constrained

Assume a counterexample exists and choose one of least order G. It is nontrivial and not nilpotent by section 2. If G is not monolithic, choose nontrivial normal N_1,N_2 with trivial intersection. Then G embeds subdirectly in G/N_1 x G/N_2 and has the same generated variety as these two quotients. They are smaller, hence both have integer limits by minimality. Section 1 then gives an integer limit for G, a contradiction. So G is monolithic.

Its monolith cannot be nonabelian, by Olshanskii's Theorem 1. Hence its monolith M is abelian. Since M is finite and minimal normal, it is elementary abelian of some prime characteristic. Moreover, G/M cannot be a monolithic group with nonabelian monolith, by section 3.

The remaining case is not eliminated: a nonnilpotent monolithic finite group with elementary abelian monolith. Proposition 1 only bounds its limsup by the integer |G/M|. An integer upper bound does not establish integrality, and does not establish convergence.

## Outcome

The full target remains unresolved. The useful reduction is a pointwise max rule for finite joins plus a least-order counterexample reduction. The next attempt will try to produce matching lower bounds using the action on an abelian monolith, without conflating the quotient order with its potential.
