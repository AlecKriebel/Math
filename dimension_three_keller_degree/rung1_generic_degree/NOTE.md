# The generic-degree spectrum of Keller counterexamples in dimension three

**First program record:** 2026-07-24T22:42:23Z.

**First public release:** 2026-07-24T23:04:08Z.

> **Status and priority.**  The exclusion proved below is classical.  Campbell
> proved the complex Galois case in 1973; Razar and Wright supplied
> characteristic-zero algebraic proofs, and Trushin's May 2026 preprint

> in Section 3.  The exact minimum generic degree three was already stated

## 1. Statement

Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic zero, let
\[
F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n):\mathbb A^n_k\longrightarrow\mathbb A^n_k
\]
be a polynomial map with \(\det JF\in k^\times\), and put
\[
A=k[F_1,\ldots,F_n]\subset B=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n],\qquad
K=\operatorname{Frac}A\subset L=\operatorname{Frac}B.
\]
The generic degree of \(F\) is \([L:K]\).

### Theorem 1 (quadratic exclusion)

If \([L:K]\leq2\), then \(F\) is a polynomial automorphism.  In particular, a
Keller counterexample over a characteristic-zero field cannot have generic
degree one or two.

### Theorem 2 (exact complex spectrum)

For Keller counterexamples
\(\mathbb A^3_{\mathbb C}\to\mathbb A^3_{\mathbb C}\), the set of generic
degrees is exactly
\[
\{3,4,5,\ldots\}.
\]
Thus generic degree three is minimal.  Every generic-degree-three
counterexample has geometric monodromy \(S_3\).

The two uses of “degree” must not be confused.  These theorems concern the
function-field degree \([L:K]\), not
\(\max_i\deg F_i\).  The total-polynomial-degree problem begins separately at
degree four.  The dimension-three degree-three result was first obtained by
Moh--Sathaye in an unpublished computer calculation; Vistoli's 1999 paper
gives the published conceptual proof over an algebraically closed
characteristic-zero field.  Faithfully flat descent gives the same floor over
an arbitrary characteristic-zero field.

## 2. The classical Galois proof

The Keller condition makes the \(F_i\) algebraically independent.  Indeed, a
polynomial relation among them, differentiated and composed with the
invertible matrix \(JF\), would have zero gradient; in characteristic zero the
relation is constant.  Hence \(F\) is dominant and \(L/K\) is a finite
separable extension.

The Campbell--Razar--Wright theorem states:

> If \(F:\mathbb A^n_k\to\mathbb A^n_k\) is Keller in characteristic zero and
> \(L/K\) is Galois, then \(A=B\).

This result is dimension-free and assumes neither properness nor
surjectivity.  A degree-one extension is trivially Galois.  Every separable
quadratic extension is Galois.  Theorem 1 follows immediately.

For clarity, “the Galois closure has a Galois group” is not the hypothesis:
every finite separable extension has a Galois closure.  The theorem requires
the original extension \(L/K\) itself to be normal.

## 3. A self-contained quadratic proof over \(\mathbb C\)

Trushin's May 2026 preprint already contains both the square-root construction
and the branch/singular-locus mechanism below.  We reproduce the short
specialization because it exposes the codimension obstruction directly; it is
not a prior-art-independent proof.
Assume for contradiction that \([L:K]=2\).  Since
\(A\simeq\mathbb C[y_1,\ldots,y_n]\) is a unique factorization domain, after
removing a square from a representative of the nontrivial square class we may
write
\[
L=K(\sqrt h)
\]
for a nonconstant squarefree \(h\in A\).  Let \(g=\sqrt h\in L\).  It satisfies
\[
g^2=h(F)\in B.
\]
Thus \(g\) is integral over the integrally closed ring \(B\), so \(g\in B\).

The polynomial \(g\) is nonconstant and therefore has a zero over
\(\mathbb C\).  At every \(p\in V(g)\), differentiation gives
\[
0=d(g^2)_p=d(h\circ F)_p=(dh)_{F(p)}\circ dF_p.
\]
The differential \(dF_p\) is invertible, so \(dh\) vanishes at \(F(p)\);
also \(h(F(p))=0\).  Consequently
\[
F(V(g))\subseteq \operatorname{Sing}V(h).
\tag{1}
\]

Because \(F\) is étale, it is quasi-finite.  Every irreducible hypersurface
component of \(V(g)\) therefore has image closure of dimension \(n-1\).
On the other hand, squarefreeness of \(h\) in characteristic zero implies
that \(V(h)\) is reduced and has no codimension-one singular component:
\[
\dim\operatorname{Sing}V(h)\leq n-2.
\tag{2}
\]
Equations (1) and (2) contradict each other.  This proves the quadratic case
without invoking the Galois-case theorem.

The same argument does not automatically extend to prime degree \(p>2\):
a degree-\(p\) extension need not be generated by a \(p\)-th root lying in the
normal source ring, and it need not be Galois.

## 4. Existence in every generic degree at least three

For completeness, we record the explicit weighted-lift family already
available in the repository.  Fix \(d\geq3\), and set
\[
u=1+xy,\qquad
\gamma=1-\frac d{d-1}xy+x^2z.
\]
Define \(F_d=(A_d,B_d,C_d)\) by
\[
A_d=
\frac{(d-2)u+u^2-(d-1)u^d\gamma^{d-2}}{(d-2)x^2},
\]
\[
B_d=
\frac{(d-2)+2u-du^{d-1}\gamma^{d-2}}{(d-2)x},
\qquad
C_d=x\gamma.
\tag{3}
\]
To see the divisibility without expansion, put \(v=xy\) and
\(\tau=x^2z\).  Both numerators are polynomials in \(v,\tau\).  The numerator
of \(B_d\) has zero constant term, while that of \(A_d\) has zero constant
term and zero coefficient of \(v\); every remaining monomial is divisible
by \(x\) and \(x^2\), respectively.  Thus these are polynomials over
\(\mathbb Q\).

Put \(w=u\gamma\), \(P=B_dC_d\), and \(Q=A_dC_d^2\).  Direct elimination gives
the inverse equation
\[
w^d-w^2+(d-2)Pw-(d-2)Q=0.
\tag{4}
\]
If \(U=(d-2)P\) and \(V=-(d-2)Q\), then (4) is
\[
T^d-T^2+UT+V.
\tag{5}
\]
It is irreducible in \(\mathbb C(U,V)[T]\): in
\(\mathbb C[U,V,T]\) it is primitive and linear in \(V\), and a
factorization would force a factor independent of \(V\) to divide the
coefficient \(1\) of \(V\).  The source coordinates are rationally recovered
from a root \(w\) away from a proper denominator locus:
\[
x=\frac{C_d}{\gamma},\qquad
u=\frac w\gamma,\qquad
y=\frac{u-1}{x},\qquad
z=\frac{\gamma-1+\frac d{d-1}(u-1)}{x^2},
\]
where \(\gamma=P-p_d(w)\) and
\[
p_d(w)=\frac{2w-dw^{d-1}}{d-2}.
\]
Hence the generic degree is exactly \(d\), with no extraneous branch from the
elimination.

The exact Jacobian calculation is most compact after the target-coordinate
change
\[
(A_d,B_d,C_d)\longmapsto
(P,Q,C_d)=(B_dC_d,A_dC_d^2,C_d).
\]
Writing
\[
q_d(w)=\frac{w^2-(d-1)w^d}{d-2}
\]
gives \(q_d'(w)=wp_d'(w)\); the corresponding \(2\times2\) Jacobian collapses
to \(-\gamma\), and comparison with the input changes
\((x,y,z)\mapsto(x,xy,x^2z)\mapsto(x,w,\gamma)\) yields
\[
\det JF_d=1.
\]

Finally, the target \((A,B,C)=(s_d,s_d,1)\), with
\[
s_d=\frac{4-2^d}{d-2},
\]
makes (4) have the distinct roots \(1\) and \(2\).  The rational recovery
formulas have nonzero denominators there:
\[
\gamma_1=\frac{d+2-2^d}{d-2}\neq0,\qquad
\gamma_2=2^{d-1}\neq0.
\]
They produce two distinct rational source points with the same image.
Therefore \(F_d\) is a Keller counterexample of generic degree \(d\).

Together with Theorem 1 this proves Theorem 2.

## 5. Monodromy consequences

A transitive subgroup of \(S_3\) is either \(C_3\) or \(S_3\).  If the
geometric monodromy of a separable cubic extension were \(C_3\), the extension
itself would be Galois, so the Galois-case theorem would make the Keller map an
automorphism.  Thus every cubic counterexample has geometric monodromy \(S_3\).

More generally, the monodromy action of a Keller counterexample cannot be
regular: a regular transitive action would make the point stabilizer trivial,
so the original field extension would already be Galois.
